## Corrections and exclusions

- Changed the incidental-music date from 1842 to 1843, and the interval after the overture from sixteen to seventeen years.
- Dated the Hebrides opening sketch before the Staffa visit.
- Removed the portrait’s conflicting mid-1840s date; did not substitute the filename date as evidence.
- No quotation has been invented. The three direct quotations in the life are separately sourced above; the Simrock letter was researched but not used.
- Identity fields, image filenames, poem, ABC, reading list and lineage arrays are unchanged. The obsolete `featured` field was removed.

## Fact-check (independent)

Checked 19 October 2026. Fetched and read: the Journal of the American Viola Society article, the Mendelssohn-Gesellschaft biography, Henle’s Op. 49 preface, the Yale and Mendelssohn-in-Scotland pages on the August 1829 letter, the Wikisource letter to Souchay and Scheideler’s article, Chris Darwin’s Octet note, the Hyperion Octet and Op. 80 pages, the Yale Center for British Art record, Wikipedia (Mendelssohn, the *Midsummer Night’s Dream* and Op. 80 articles) and a search-retrieved account of Fanny’s 1846 correspondence. Labels: MEAS = read in the primary or edition text; EXT = reliable external source.

### Changes

- “his seven-year-old sister Fanny” → “his sister Fanny”. The Mendelssohn-Gesellschaft gives “siebenjährige Schwester … 1812”, but Fanny was born on 14 November 1805 and was six for most of 1812; the letter itself is undated in the source. The age was unverifiable precision and is dropped; the quotation, year and recipients (friends in Hamburg) stand. EXT: [Mendelssohn-Gesellschaft](https://www.mendelssohn-gesellschaft.de/mendelssohns/biografien/felix-mendelssohn-bartholdy).
- “After an unhappy spell as music director in Düsseldorf” → “After a frustrating spell…”. Wikipedia: “His frustration at his everyday duties in Düsseldorf, and the city’s provincialism, led him to resign his position at the end of 1834.” “Unhappy” overstated an episode in which he also wrote of being content there. EXT: [Wikipedia, Felix Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn).

### Quotations confirmed

- “heart palpitations”: Mendelssohn to Karl Klingemann, 31 January 1836, “und hatte Herzklopfen als ich zum erstenmal vor dem Publicum Bratsche spielte”; the article states he played the second viola part in a public performance of the Octet. MEAS: [Shaver-Gleason, JAVS 27/2, p. 19 and note 13](https://americanviolasociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/JAVS-27.2.pdf).
- “Felix has a strong voice and sings all day”: “Felix hat eine starke Stimme und singt den ganzen Tag”, Fanny to friends in Hamburg, 1812 (the Society paraphrases the letter and names no date within the year). [Mendelssohn-Gesellschaft](https://www.mendelssohn-gesellschaft.de/mendelssohns/biografien/felix-mendelssohn-bartholdy).
- “If you ask me what I was thinking, I say: precisely the song as it stands”: Mendelssohn to Marc-André Souchay, Berlin, 15 October 1842, written in answer to a question about the meaning of some *Songs without Words* (Scheideler: Souchay had suggested underlaying words); our translation agrees in sense with the published one (“If you ask me what was in my mind while composing a particular song, I answer, the song itself precisely as it stands”). MEAS: [Selected Letters, Letter 29](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Selected_letters_of_Mendelssohn/Letter_29); [Scheideler, ZGMTH 2015](https://www.gmth.de/zeitschrift/artikel/820.aspx).
- Puck’s lines (V.1) match the standard text; the four wind chords accompany Puck’s speech in the finale: [Wikipedia, *A Midsummer Night’s Dream* (Mendelssohn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Midsummer_Night%27s_Dream_(Mendelssohn)).

### Other claims confirmed

- Octet: composed 1825 as a birthday present for Eduard Rietz, first violin part written for him, Mendelssohn’s own note asking for “symphonic orchestral style”; scherzo marked *Allegro leggierissimo*, ending on a rising pianissimo arpeggio; finale starts in the second cello; the scherzo theme returns in the finale. MEAS: [Darwin note](https://users.sussex.ac.uk/~cjd/WebProgNotes/pdfs/MendelssohnOctet.pdf); EXT: [Hyperion Octet note](https://www.hyperion-records.co.uk/dw.asp?dc=W869_66356) (“The Scherzo theme reappears briefly in the Finale”).
- Baptism of the children on 21 March 1816; move to Berlin 1811; Zelter introducing Felix to Goethe in 1821; Terence’s *Andria* translated 1825, published 1826; *St Matthew Passion* on 11 March 1829 with Devrient’s help and Bella Salomon’s gift of the score; Düsseldorf Lower Rhine Festival 1833 opening with *Israel in Egypt*; Gewandhaus from 1835; *St Paul* at Düsseldorf in 1836; Schubert’s C major Symphony on 21 March 1839; Leipzig Conservatoire in 1843; marriage on 28 March 1837 and five children; ten visits to Britain; *Midsummer Night’s Dream* overture written in 1826 and the incidental music in 1843: [Wikipedia, Felix Mendelssohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Mendelssohn).
- Overture: opening four wind chords in E major, divided violins (“in four parts”) in E minor, Bottom’s bray in the strings; incidental music adds three trombones, and the Nocturne has a solo horn doubled by bassoons: [Wikipedia, *A Midsummer Night’s Dream* (Mendelssohn)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Midsummer_Night%27s_Dream_(Mendelssohn)); [Hollywood Bowl note](https://www.hollywoodbowl.com/musicdb/pieces/893/a-midsummer-nights-dream-overture-and-incidental-music).
- *Song without Words* Op. 19b No. 1 (Scheideler’s “op. 19/1”): the theme’s consequent phrase is extended, with a pause on the pre-dominant in bars 9–10 where “Melodie und Bass antworten einander”, and a second cadence needed to reach the tonic. MEAS: [Scheideler, ZGMTH 2015, section III](https://www.gmth.de/zeitschrift/artikel/820.aspx). Barenboim’s recordings were made in Paris and London in 1973: [Deutsche Grammophon essay](https://www.deutschegrammophon.com/de/kuenstler-innen/daniel_barenboim/pressestimmen/daniel-barenboim-klavierstuecke-im-stile-von-liedern-267201).
- Hebrides sketch: letter and sketch of 7 August 1829 (Tobermory); Staffa visit the next day: [Yale, *Art and Music in Britain*](https://artandmusic.yale.edu/romantic-landscapes/mendelssohn-scotland/mendelssohn-gallery2-letter-his-family); [Mendelssohn in Scotland](https://mendelssohninscotland.com/highlands-fort-william-oban-and-over-mull).
- D minor Trio: first autograph dated 18 July 1839; wholesale revision after a private performance with David on 29 August 1839; second autograph dated 23 September 1839; Hiller’s urging to modernise the piano part; further changes in proofs; first public performance in the Gewandhaus on 1 February 1840 with David, Carl Wittmann and the composer. MEAS: [Henle Op. 49 preface](https://www.henle.de/media/a8/b0/4a/1697725910/1531-1697725910-sync.pdf).
- Fanny’s decision to publish was announced to Felix on 9 July 1846; he replied on 12 August giving his “professional blessing”: [BBC Music Magazine, Fanny Mendelssohn](https://www.classical-music.com/features/composers/fanny-mendelssohn); his earlier opposition, and Fanny’s diary entry of 14 August 1846 (“Felix has written, and given me his professional blessing”): [Kapralova Society journal 9](http://www.kapralova.org/journal9.pdf). Fanny died 14 May 1847; Op. 80 written that summer and autumn in Switzerland, Felix dying on 4 November: [Hyperion CDA68330 notes](https://www.hyperion-records.co.uk/dc.asp?dc=D_CDA68330).
- Turner reached Staffa in 1831 by paddle-steamer from Tobermory; “the tiny red dot is the ship’s furnace”: [YCBA record B1978.43.14](https://collections.britishart.yale.edu/catalog/tms%3A5018). The record’s 2026 gallery label gives 1813 for the visit, a slip; the same record’s older labels, provenance and Warner’s catalogue essay give 1831.
- Violin Concerto première on 13 March 1845 with David and Gade, *Elijah* on 26 August 1846, Op. 80 in F minor: standard and consistent across the sources above; Mendelssohn’s Gewandhaus and Düsseldorf dates as in Wikipedia.

### Not changed, noted

- Epithet: “…eight string players sound like the most exhilarating orchestra in Europe” is an evaluative image. The score itself asks for “a symphonic orchestral style” (Darwin), so the comparison to an orchestra is grounded; the superlative is editorial.
- Portrait caption names the painter only; the file name’s “(1833)” was not used as evidence.

### Protected-data findings

- None demonstrably wrong. The Hebrides and Puck texts match their sources.
