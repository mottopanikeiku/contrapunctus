## Principal sources (all opened)

- **Habets** — Alfred Habets, *Borodin and Liszt*, translated by Rosa Newmarch (Digby, Long, second edition; Newmarch’s preface was written after Tchaikovsky’s death in 1893 and Rubinstein’s in 1894, and the back-matter list is dated July 1896, so “c. 1896” is the research desk’s dating; the Internet Archive record says 1890, which cannot be right). https://archive.org/details/borodinlisztilif00habe — full OCR text read: chapters I–X, Borodin’s letters of 1875–86 and the Weimar letters of 1877 and 1881, catalogue of works. Page numbers below are the book’s own. Habets dates events in the Russian calendar unless the letter was written abroad (1 July 1877 is a Sunday in the Western calendar).
- **MML** — Rimsky-Korsakov, *My Musical Life*, trans. Judah A. Joffe (Knopf, 1923), IMSLP PDF https://s9.imslp.org/files/imglnks/usimg/7/72/IMSLP721391-PMLP52433-My_Musical_Life_eng_edt.pdf — passages on Borodin read in chapters VI (1865–66, p. 53: Rimsky-Korsakov meets Borodin in 1865; the flat and the laboratory), XIV (pp. 162–164, the household, cats), XV (pp. 176–178, the Polovtsian Dances scored in pencil; the summer of 1879, the tableaux and the *Steppes*), XX (death, manuscripts, pp. 238–246) and XXI (staging, 23 October 1890).
- **RBS** — *Russian Biographical Dictionary*, vol. 3 (1908), “Borodin, Alexander Porfiryevich”, Wikisource: https://ru.wikisource.org/wiki/%D0%A0%D0%91%D0%A1/%D0%92%D0%A2/%D0%91%D0%BE%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B4%D0%B8%D0%BD,_%D0%90%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%BA%D1%81%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B4%D1%80_%D0%9F%D0%BE%D1%80%D1%84%D0%B8%D1%80%D1%8C%D0%B5%D0%B2%D0%B8%D1%87 — read in full (Russian, translated by the research desk). Gives 31 October 1834 for the birth, which is wrong; modern sources and the Russian state library give 1833.
- **GBS** — Saint Petersburg State Library, “К 190-летию со дня рождения А. П. Бородина” (2023): birth on 12 November 1833, Gagarinskaya Street 9; father Prince Luka Gedianov, mother Avdotya Antonova; registered as son of Porfiry Borodin; polka “Hélène” at nine; Pisa and fluorine work; students carried the coffin to the Tikhvin cemetery. https://www.gbs.spb.ru/ru/pages/resursy/novinki/rubrika_staraia_plastinka/arkhiv_rubriki_za_2023_g/k_190_letiiu_so_dnia_rozhdeniia_aleksandra_porfirevicha_borodina
- **CMS** — Stephen Walsh, “Who Was Alexander Borodin?”, Chamber Music Society of Lincoln Center: https://www.chambermusicsociety.org/about-the-music/composers/alexander-borodin — quartet begun 1874; the only string player in the Five.
- **BSO** — Harlow Robinson, “Overture to Prince Igor”, https://www.bso.org/works/overture-to-prince-igor — born 12 November 1833, died 27 February 1887; what was orchestrated; Glazunov’s account of the overture; “controversial” additions.
- **Hyperion** — David Fanning, note for CDA68166 (2017): https://www.hyperion-records.co.uk/dw.asp?dc=W333_68166 — Quartet No. 2 composed summer 1881, dedicated to his wife; Quartet No. 1 on a theme from the replacement finale of Beethoven’s Op. 130; Scherzo and Notturno themes taken for *Kismet*. Album page https://www.hyperion-records.co.uk/dc.asp?dc=D_CDA68166 (recording details).
- **Okazoe** — Takashi Okazoe, *Proc. Jpn. Acad. B* 85 (2009) 276 ff., https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/pjab/85/8/85_8_276/_pdf — benzoyl fluoride, 1862, first halogen exchange with fluoride. **Quibell et al.**, *Chem. Sci.* 9 (2018) 3860, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5935059 — Borodine, *Liebigs Ann. Chem.* 119 (1861) 121–123, first demonstration of the reaction later named for Hunsdiecker.
- **LOC** — Library of Congress, Ballets Russes exhibition items: https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/ballets-russes/exhibition-items.html — *Polovtsian Dances*, music by Borodin completed and partly orchestrated by Rimsky-Korsakov and Glazunov, sets and costumes by Roerich, choreography by Fokine, 19 May 1909, Théâtre du Châtelet.
- **Russian Museum** — Repin portrait: https://www.virtualrm.spb.ru/ru/node/2525 (painted after Borodin’s death, from a model and photographs); Vasnetsov painting: https://rusmuseumvrm.ru/data/collections/museums/gtg/vasnecov_v._m._posle_poboischa_igorya_svyatoslavicha_s_polovcami._1880._inv._1009._gtg/index.php (Tretyakov Gallery inv. 1009; shown at the eighth Itinerants’ exhibition; bought by Tretyakov).
- **IMSLP** — *In the Steppes of Central Asia*: https://imslp.org/wiki/In_the_Steppes_of_Central_Asia_(Borodin,_Aleksandr) — dedication to Liszt; first publication Rahter, Hamburg, 1882; score files 1016329 and 283781 (typeset) read.

## Sound aspects

Whole-tone scale, stacked fifths, “pecking” (*klevaniya*): RBS, paragraph on the First Symphony and the songs; Habets p. 111 (Borodin on Liszt’s fingers “pecking”, Mussorgsky’s word). Laroche, *Golos* 1874: Habets p. 24. “Oriental dances, for the Polovtsi were an eastern people” (15 April 1875): Habets p. 48; Hunfalvi and songs of Central Asia: Habets p. 35. “Recitative does not enter into my nature or disposition” (10 June 1876): Habets p. 52. Walsh on the only string player: CMS.

## Notation

1. **Steppes, bars 5–9, first clarinet, sounding pitch** — typeset score IMSLP 1016329 (checked against 283781). Pitches read by staff-line detection on a 330 dpi render and cross-checked by pitch-tracking the hosted recording, which gives E5 D5 C♯5 | B4 C♯5 | A4 E4 | B4 (dotted) C♯5 B4 | A4 E5 at the expected times. The part is printed for clarinet in A; transposed down a minor third to A major. 2/4, ♩ = 92 (the score’s mark; the hosted recording is slower).
2. **Whole-tone scale** and 3. **stacked fifths** — invented schematics, labelled as such; the reasons for them are in the RBS paragraph.
- Not used: the Notturno cello melody (the cello part lies on many ledger lines in the 1888 Belaieff print; staff reading was not secure, and pitch-tracking the Musopen recording failed), and the opening of Symphony No. 2 (Bessel print, IMSLP/Sibley scan; the opening bars were not transcribed with confidence).

## Unresolved

- Whether Borodin’s marriage was in 1862 or April 1863 (GBS and CMS differ); the text avoids the year.
- Where exactly he died: Habets and Rimsky-Korsakov say at home; CMS says at an Academy ball. The text follows the first two.
- The Notturno recording’s players, the *Steppes* orchestra and conductor, and both session dates are not documented.
- The identity of the melody in the flutes and violins at letter G of the *Steppes* is described by its triplet figures, not named.
- Section and cue times are estimates from the audio and the score; all cues are passage precision.
- The first-edition cello part’s Notturno melody was not transcribed; no Notturno ABC is on the page.

## Fact-check (independent)

Checked 19 October 2026 against sources fetched afresh: Habets (full OCR, archive.org `borodinlisztilif00habe`), Rimsky-Korsakov, *My Musical Life* (IMSLP PDF), the *Russian Biographical Dictionary* entry, the Saint Petersburg State Library page, Okazoe and Quibell et al., the BSO, CMS, Hyperion, Chandos, LOC, IMSLP and Russian Museum pages, the Tretyakov record, the YouTube page of the Hyperion trailer, and the ADMV programme list. Every quotation and every dated claim in the composer page, bibliography, elsewhere, geo and influence files was compared with those texts.

### Changes

- **Baden-Baden, June 1880 → 20 May 1880.** The composer page (life, 1877–86), the Liszt edge in `content/influence/borodin.json` and its locator said June 1880, following the date printed in Habets (Borodin to Stasov, 23 July 1880: “the 20th of June 1880”). The Allgemeiner Deutscher Musikverein’s 17th Tonkünstler-Versammlung at Baden-Baden ran 19–23 May 1880, and Borodin’s symphony (listed there as “2. Symphonie in Esdur”, i.e. the E♭ symphony, now No. 1) was in the first orchestral concert on 20 May. [Carleton University ADMV programme list, 1880](https://www3.carleton.ca/admv/program1880.html); [Thüringen digital library, ADMV 1880 list](https://www.db-thueringen.de/servlets/MCRFileNodeServlet/dbt_derivate_00063592/1880.pdf). The page now says 20 May 1880; the edge says May 1880 and its locator records Habets’s differing date.
- **Antwerp speech.** “at the last concert he made a speech” → “when called on made a speech”. Habets (letter of 21 September 1885) gives the speech after the series of concerts and does not name the concert.
- **Geo, Verona.** “walking journey” → “journey”. Mendeleev’s account in Habets has knapsacks but a carriage.
- **Elsewhere, Hyperion trailer.** 3:02 → 3:01 (YouTube page: 181 s).

### Quotations confirmed (wording, speaker, addressee, date)

- “For me, it is a relaxation, a pastime which distracts me from my principal business, my professorship.” Borodin to Lyudmila Karmalina, 10 June 1876 (Habets pp. 49–52; Newmarch prints “principle”). The same letter has “never say to me, ‘I hope you are well,’ but, ‘I hope you are ill.’” and “Recitative does not enter into my nature or disposition.” “Oriental dances, for the Polovtsi were an eastern people” is from the letter of 15 April 1875 to the same correspondent.
- “you make a mistake in hunting two hares at once”: Zinin to Borodin, as Borodin reported it to Gavrushkevich (Habets p. 6).
- “You have written a fine symphony”, “Do not touch it, alter nothing”: Liszt to Borodin at Weimar, Sunday 1 July 1877, in Borodin’s letter to his wife, Jena, 3 July 1877 (Habets pp. 107–116). Habets’s “Sunday, July 1st” is correct in the Western calendar.
- “written by a master hand”: Liszt to Borodin, 3 September 1880 (Habets p. 22).
- “must always introduce a dissonance somewhere”: Laroche, *Golos*, 1874 (Habets p. 24).
- “gone the round of Europe from Christiania to Monaco”: Borodin to Gavrushkevich, 6 May 1886 (Habets pp. 85–86). The “encored in Vienna and Paris” paraphrase matches “encored almost everywhere … as at the Strauss concerts in Vienna and the Lamoureux concerts in Paris”.
- “transfusing emptiness into vacancy”, “Listen, dear Sir, this is too much of a good thing”, “gay and animated, among guests gathered at his house”, “hung them out like wash on lines in my study”: Rimsky-Korsakov, *My Musical Life*, in the passages named in the ledger above.
- “impregnated with Mendelssohnism”: Habets p. 5 (“to use his own expression”). Silver-crown inscription: Habets p. 19 and RBS.
- The Polovtsian Dances aside, the tableaux of summer 1879 for the twenty-fifth anniversary (due in 1880), the list of numbers orchestrated, Glazunov and the overture, the memorial concert with the March, the overture and two movements of the Third Symphony, and Kuchera’s rehearsal of the 1890 staging are all in Rimsky-Korsakov as stated. BSO gives 5 November 1887 for the first performance of the overture.

### Facts confirmed

Birth, parents and Porfiry Borodin (GBS; Habets and RBS give 1834, which is wrong); flute concerto and *Robert le diable* trio, 1847 (Habets p. 3, RBS); Mussorgsky in the autumn of 1856 at seventeen (Habets pp. 7–8); doctorate 3 May 1858, Heidelberg and Erlenmeyer, Mendeleev, the Verona episode (Habets pp. 13–14), Protopopova and Schumann and Chopin (Habets pp. 14–15; GBS places the Pisa stay with her); adjunct professor 1862, professor 1864, women’s courses from 1872 (RBS; Habets pp. 18–19); benzoyl fluoride 1862 and the 1861 Hunsdiecker-type paper (Okazoe, Quibell et al.); Balakirev, Beethoven, Schumann, Berlioz, Liszt and Glinka four-hand (RBS; the page omits Liszt from the list, which is accurate for the sentence); the first symphony of 1862–67 and its performance on 4 January 1869 OS (Habets pp. 20–22); *Mlada* and the winter of 1874 (Habets pp. 35–38); the Second Symphony given on 2 February 1877 OS under Nápravník (Habets p. 55; the Belcanto reference page agrees; IMSLP and Wikipedia give 10 March 1877, a conflict not resolved, and the page says only “February 1877”); Weimar 1877 and 1881 (Habets); *Steppes* dedication, “embraced me and thanked me warmly” (Habets p. 192) and first publication by Rahter, Hamburg, 1882 (IMSLP); Quartet No. 1 dedicated to Mme Rimsky-Korsakov and Quartet No. 2 to his wife (Habets catalogue; Hyperion); Antwerp dates of 9, 16 and 19 September 1885 and the Liège and Brussels visits (Habets pp. 66–81); death at a party in his flat, in national costume, of a ruptured aneurysm (Habets p. 89; Rimsky-Korsakov), burial beside Mussorgsky. Two sources differ: RBS says rupture of the heart, and CMS (and BSO) say a ball at the Academy with a ruptured aorta; the page follows Habets and Rimsky-Korsakov.

Sound aspects checked against RBS (fifths in the Andante, the second and the whole-tone scale in *The Sleeping Princess*, “klevaniya” in the scherzo, the finale, Konchak’s aria and the overture) and Habets p. 111 (“progression of distant intervals, pizzicato”). RBS names *The Sea Queen* beside *The Sleeping Princess* for the second; the page names only the second song and does not misstate. The opera’s “airs, cavatinas, duets and trios” is in Habets’s general account of *Igor* in the chapters on the opera; the voice-first remark is in the letter of 10 June 1876 (Habets pp. 52–53).
