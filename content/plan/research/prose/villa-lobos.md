## Works list

W numbers, scorings, dedications and first performances are from the IMSLP list (Appleby numbers). Where IMSLP and another source disagree the chapter either gives the month or leaves the date out: Chôros No. 7 (IMSLP and the Museu record both give 17 September 1925, now used in full; a Wikipedia lead says 19 September and is not used), Chôros No. 3 (IMSLP and the Museu 30 November 1925; BIS booklet 30 November 1926 for a performance), Chôros No. 12 (IMSLP 23 February 1945; BIS booklet and the Museu 21 February 1945; not used), Twelve Études (autograph 1928 per Yates; 1929 usually given; Eschig 1952 per IMSLP, 1957 per Yates; chapter says “in the 1950s”), Cinq Préludes (IMSLP gives the first performance of the set as 11 December 1943; Fraga and the Hyperion note say Carlevaro played Nos. 3 and 4 only, the latter dating it 11 November; the chapter says Nos. 3 and 4, 1943). The Suite populaire brésilienne dates are from the long IMSLP note (Fraga’s “Eschig, 1924” disagrees with IMSLP’s 1955 and is not used). The claim that the BB1 opening movement was added in 1938 is Wright’s; IMSLP says it is believed to date from 1936–38.

## Influence edges

Four edges: Rubinstein → Villa-Lobos (championed; Appleby, EBSCO, ArtsWatch, Guinle), Bach → Villa-Lobos (modelled; Wright, ABM, Sato), Villa-Lobos → Segovia (dedicated; Classical Guitar, Yates, IMSLP), Medeiros → Villa-Lobos (quoted; Coli, LA Phil, IMSLP). No figure entry for Villa-Lobos existed in the other batches.

## Unresolved

- Which Guinle brother paid for the 1923 stay (Arnaldo in Bulcão’s timeline, Carlos in the ArtsWatch retelling of Rubinstein’s memoir).
- The date of Chôros No. 3’s first performance (1925 or 1926) and of the Bandeira review.
- The session date and place of the Sayão recording (see its dossier); the date of first release is bounded only by the 1946 Columbia catalogue.
- The release date and the exact order of events for the Victor 11214 disc before February 1936; the description cites a February 1937 review that was not opened.
- The structure of Chôros No. 7 beyond its scoring: no score or programme note was opened, so the recording’s listening notes are limited to documented facts and the sections are labelled by position only.
- The Dansa (Martelo) and the complete Bachianas No. 5 have no pre-1963 hosted recording found with a dated first publication; the Victoria de los Ángeles recording (EMI/Angel, 1956–58) was not documented well enough to host.

### Confirmed

- Raul Villa-Lobos as a National Library employee and cello teacher, using a viola at first (Fraga); his death from smallpox in 1899 (Appleby, EBSCO); Noemia taking in laundry, “Tuhú”, the medical classes, the chorões and the aunt who played Bach preludes and fugues (Appleby); the sale of rare books from his father’s library to pay for the first journey and Appleby’s remark that there is “little evidence” of systematic folk-song collecting; the marriage on 12 November 1913 and the concert of 13 November 1915 (EBSCO); Milhaud at the French embassy in 1917 and the Rubinstein recommendation of 1923, with the Salle Gaveau concerts of 24 October and 5 December 1927 (Appleby). The *Simples* manuscript of 12 August 1911 and its inscription “This is a study; I don’t consider it as serious music” (Fraga, section V.viii, which also gives Segovia’s foreword to the Études and “continuous arpeggio” for No. 1).
- Semana de Arte Moderna, 13, 15 and 17 February 1922; “20 peças de sua autoria” over the three days; Villa-Lobos conducting the *Quarteto simbólico* and the *Danças características africanas*; tailcoat and slippers attributed to gout “segundo pesquisas históricas”: Sesc São Paulo, 7 February 2022 (Camila Fresca and a second speaker quoted).
- Segovia anecdote of 1924: WPR gives it “according to Villa-Lobos”: “Well, if it isn’t used, cut it off.”
- First performances from the IMSLP list: *Quarteto simbólico*, Rio, 21 October 1921 (W181); Nonet, Paris, 30 May 1924, Villa-Lobos conducting (W191); Chôros No. 8, Salle Gaveau, 24 October 1927, Concerts Colonne (W208); Chôros No. 10, Teatro Lírico, Rio, 11 November 1926 (W209, dedicated to Paulo Prado).
- Pedrosa, 1929, “the naïve and total sincerity of a mountain torrent” (Sato, with the French in note 6; the page’s “artless” is its own rendering of “ingénue”). The 1936 flight on the *Graf Hindenburg*, leaving Brazil on 4 April and returning on 12 June (Sato).
- Wright (Hyperion): “a universal folkloric source”, quoted in the page’s words; the Brazilian premieres of the *Missa solemnis* (1933), the *Missa Papae Marcelli* (1933) and the B minor Mass (1935).
- Coli (BIS): Chôros No. 11 “strong, big and robust, like an elephant”; Pareci themes recorded by Roquette-Pinto; Bandeira’s “Villa needed a word – any word – ending in ‘il’”, which he wrote after a performance the booklet dates 30 November 1926 (the page gives no date).
- “more than thirty thousand performers” in the canto orfeônico concerts (EBSCO); the stadium concerts under Vargas (ABM). The New Yorker, 10 February 1945: the Waldorf, “some fifteen hundred works”.
- LA Phil on Chôros No. 10: the subtitle comes from a poem by Catulo da Paixão Cearense, and the composer allows a vocalise on “Ah!” in place of the Portuguese text. The lawsuit by Catulo’s heirs and the second edition without words or subtitle are from the Coli booklet and were not independently found in the LA Phil note.

### Second pass (FactVL, 19 October 2026)

Every source below was fetched afresh. Labels: MEAS = read in a fetched source, score or image in this pass; EXT = a statement in a source, repeated without independent proof; INFERENCE = my reading, marked where used. Files changed: `content/composers/villa-lobos.mjs`, `content/geo/villa-lobos.json`, `content/elsewhere/villa-lobos.json`, `content/bibliography/villa-lobos.json`, `content/influence/villa-lobos.json` and this ledger. `node tools/validate.mjs` on the first four and `node tools/validate-influence.mjs` print `ok`.

#### Fixed

1. **Chôros Nos. 13 and 14** (chapter, 1930 paragraph). “Believed to have been lost then” (Coli’s hedge) became Wright’s plain statement: evicted “together with various belongings, including the now lost autograph scores of Chôros Nos 13 and 14”, with a surviving short-score fragment of No. 13 (EXT, Hyperion booklet). “Could not send his rent” became “could not pay the rent” (Wright). IMSLP and the BIS-1520 label page also give both as lost (MEAS).
2. **Cinq Préludes** (works list). The chapter had “first performed in Montevideo on 11 December 1943 by Abel Carlevaro” (IMSLP). Fraga (“partial debut … numbers 3 and 4”) and Thérèse Saba’s Hyperion note for SIGCD404 (“premièred Preludes Nos 3 and 4”, dated 11 November 1943) both limit it to Nos. 3 and 4. The chapter now says Carlevaro gave the first performance of Nos. 3 and 4 in Montevideo in 1943 and drops the day.
3. **Dates narrowed.** Chôros No. 7: 17 September 1925 (IMSLP W199 and Museu record 1993.21.0036, “1ª 17/9/1925”). Chôros No. 2: “in São Paulo” added (Coli). Chôros No. 11: 18 July 1942 (IMSLP W228; Museu record 1990.21.0107).
4. **Segovia’s account** (margin note). “His chords and fingerings were original” misreported WPR: Segovia said Villa-Lobos tried several times to begin and gave up because his fingers were clumsy, that the chords were full of dissonances, the melodic fragments original and the fingering ingenious.
5. **Sound, “Bach and Brazil”.** “Nos. 1 and 5 use cellos alone” was wrong for No. 5. Wright: both are scored for an orchestra of cellos; the Hyperion booklet lists No. 5 as “for soprano and eight cellos”.
6. **Recordings.** Hyperion CDH55316: “cello-only Bachianas” corrected; the disc also holds the Suite for voice and violin (Peter Manning) and four Bach preludes and fugues (booklet track list), so the elsewhere item now names them and its year range starts at 1923. BIS-686: the label page lists Chôro No. 1 after the Études, inside the 78'28 total; chapter and elsewhere item now name it.
7. **The New Yorker** (last years). “Found him at the Waldorf in February 1945 … due to conduct” became “a profile in the issue of 10 February 1945 … conducting the Philharmonic that week”. The web page shows 3 February and says the visit was “last week”; the day of the interview is not given. The Museu record for Chôros No. 8 lists the Philharmonic concert on 8 February 1945 (MEAS), which fits but is not stated.
8. ***Forest of the Amazon*.** “In 1959 Villa-Lobos conducted” and “recorded it in 1959” became “United Artists issued … in 1959” and “is dated 1959”. The Internet Archive record dates the LP 1959; MusicWeb says “late 1950s”; no session date was found.
9. **Oito Batutas note** (arts). “Choro group” dropped: the IMS chronology says “Orquestra Típica” and the Cravo Albin entry “conjunto típico”; no source opened says “choro group”. “Paid for their six months” became Cravo Albin’s “custeados por Arnaldo Guinle” for the trip (embarked 28 January 1922; opened mid-February at the Dancing Sherazade; season ran to the end of July; IMS says six months). The instruments are tied to the 1921 photograph, whose Commons caption lists cavaquinho, violões, bandola, flute and ganzá (MEAS).

#### Yará ABC example

MEAS. The Musica Brasilis PDF (3 pages; cover “Yára (1916)”, edition by Eduardo Virgilio; CC BY-SA 4.0 per the Commons record; page 2 headed “YÁRA / Schottisch / Anacleto de Medeiros”) was rendered at 450 dpi and the noteheads of the first system located against the five staff lines by script. Common time, no key signature (G♯ appears as an accidental from bar 3, so A minor is a reading), no tempo word on the page. Bar 1: E5 A4 A4 B4 C5, then D5–C5 as slurred semiquavers, B4 A4. Bar 2: G5–F5 slurred, D5 E5, F5 as a crotchet, crotchet rest. The ABC (`e A A B c (d/c/) B A | (gf) d e f2 z2`) matches pitch, rhythm, slurs and metre; it is the top line of the right hand only, as the caption says, and the left hand is omitted. `Q:1/4=100` is a playback setting, not from the score. The *Odeon* example (labelled schematic): the Commons PDF’s first system is in 2/4 and E major (four sharps), headed “tango brasileiro … 1909”, marked “gingando”, and in bars 1–3 the left hand has an eighth rest then an eighth chord over a dotted-quaver–semiquaver bass on each beat, which is the rhythm the caption describes. The third example (continuous arpeggio) is invented and says so.
