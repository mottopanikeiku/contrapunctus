# Modernism era — prose source record

This record supports the rewrite of `content/eras/6-modernism.mjs`. The sources below were located through web searches and then opened and read. Era objects have no source field, so the evidence belongs here rather than in additional schema fields or external links in the page prose.

The rewrite covers the subtitle, précis, essay and asides, eight glossary definitions, both score captions and both arts notes. The existing ABC, poem and translation, artwork metadata, era identity and chronology fields were outside the prose edit. No test, validation, build, formatter, audio-analysis or art-fetch command was run.

## Schoenberg and the Second Viennese School

- [Arnold Schönberg Center: Second String Quartet, Op. 10](https://schoenberg.at/en/schoenberg/kompositionen/second-string-quartet-op-10), essay by Therese Muxeneder. Supports the quartet’s F♯ minor opening, soprano in the last two movements, George texts, and the finale’s alternation between suspended tonality and tonal cadences. Includes Schoenberg’s introduction for the Kolisch Quartet’s private recording in Los Angeles, 1936–37: he recalls the December 1908 Vienna première with the Rosé Quartet and Marie Gutheil-Schoder, audience interruptions, and silence for the final string coda. The scene is attributed to that retrospective account; no invented gesture or audience dialogue is supplied. The rewrite avoids calling the whole quartet simply music without a tonal centre.
- [Arnold Schönberg Center: Pierrot lunaire, Op. 21](https://schoenberg.at/de/schoenberg/kompositionen/pierrot-lunaire-op-21), Therese Muxeneder. Supports Albertine Zehme’s commission, Berlin in 1912, the reciter and five instrumentalists, and the instrumental doublings. The German account was read. The prose uses the commission and ensemble, without importing unattributed sayings about singing.
- [Gabriel Mălăncioiu: “Nacht” analysis, Musicology Papers 39/2 (2024)](https://musicologypapers.edituramediamusica.ro/images/issues/MP_2024-1_XXXIX-2_03_Gabriel_Malancioiu.pdf). Supports the E–G–E♭ cell, its transformations and recurrence throughout the twenty-six bars, and the bass-clarinet, cello and piano scoring. The giant black moths/butterflies covering the sun belong to the poem set in the movement, not to an invented biographical scene. The interval description is E–G, a minor third up, then G–E♭, a major third down. Numerological and psychological speculation in the article was not adopted.
- [Sprechgesang / Sprechstimme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprechgesang). Secondary source reproducing the pertinent passage of Schoenberg’s 1912 preface to *Pierrot lunaire*: rhythm is observed precisely; a pitch is immediately abandoned by rising or falling. This supports the paraphrase in the aside and glossary. An attempted university-hosted copy of the preface could not be retrieved because of a certificate error; no claim is attributed to that inaccessible PDF. There is no direct English quotation from the preface in the era prose.
- [Arnold Schönberg Center: Suite for Piano, Op. 25](https://schoenberg.at/en/schoenberg/kompositionen/suite-for-piano-op-25), Eike Feß. Supports the dates 1921–23; the gavotte, musette, Intermezzo and gigue; transformations of the row; the Intermezzo’s accompanying figure above a low melody; and the gigue’s 2/2 metre with occasional 3/4 bars. The source’s overly categorical statement that twelve-tone composition prohibits pitch repetition was not repeated. The chapter explains row order and transformations without pretending that a row chooses rhythm, dynamics or instrumentation.
- [Open Music Theory: Twelve-Tone Analysis Examples — Webern Op. 21 and 24](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/twelve-tone-analysis-examples-webern-op-21-and-24/), Mark Gotham. Supports the 1928 Symphony, the first movement’s inverted canons, and the melodic route from horn through clarinet to cello. The source distinguishes inversion of a contrapuntal line from the row’s retrograde/transposition properties; the prose does not conflate those operations.
- [Boston Symphony Orchestra: Berg, Violin Concerto](https://www.bso.org/works/berg-violin-concerto), Michael Steinberg. Supports the 1935 date, the opening open-string notes G–D–A–E, and the row’s overlapping minor and major triads. Its initial G–B♭–D–F♯–A includes G minor and D major, sharing D. This supplies the essay and row glossary examples.
- [Arnold Schönberg Center: Timeline](https://schoenberg.at/en/schoenberg/timeline). Supports Berg and Webern becoming Schoenberg’s pupils in 1904; the 1933 expulsion from the Berlin Academy under the Nazis; return to the Jewish community in Paris with Chagall as witness; emigration with his wife and daughter; teaching in Boston and New York; and the UCLA professorship in 1936. The American appointments are specific events, not an assertion that exile ended his professional difficulties.

## Rhythm, collected songs and Ives

- [National Arts Centre: programme notes for The Rite of Spring](https://nac-cna.ca/en/event/notes/30031), Hannah Chan-Hartley. Supports the Paris première on 29 May 1913, Nijinsky’s choreography, repeated string chords in *The Augurs of Spring*, and eight horns reinforcing irregular accents. The essay teaches the separation of steady quavers from shifting stresses. It does not retell the uncertain details of the première’s supposed riot.
- [The Rite of Spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rite_of_Spring), particularly the first-performance account and “Music” section. Supports the stamping choreography and the written spelling of the Augurs chord: an E♭ dominant seventh over an F♭ major triad. [Indianapolis Symphony programme notes](https://www.indianapolissymphony.org/backstage/program-notes/stravinsky-the-rite-of-spring/) describe the lower triad enharmonically as E major; the prose uses F♭ to match the score spelling. The Indianapolis page’s more sensational première anecdotes were not used.
- [Pál Richter, Institute for Musicology: Bartók’s Folk Music Collections](https://bartok-gyujtesek.zti.hu/en). Supports the encounter with Lidi Dósa from Kibéd at Gerlice-puszta in 1904; the subsequent systematic collecting; Kodály’s and Bartók’s use of the phonograph in 1905–06; slower playback for detailed transcription; old Hungarian pentatonic melodies and descending fifth-shift structures; Slovak and Romanian collecting; and Bartók’s taking the Romanian collection’s fair copy to America in October 1940 and continuing its preparation there. The collected repertories remain distinct; the prose does not present all rural music as a single Hungarian style.
- [Ilkka Oramo: Some Aspects of Bartók’s Notation](https://relatedrocks.com/1996/03/01/some-aspects-of-bartoks-notation/), lecture for ESTA, Budapest, 1996. Supports Bartók’s discussion of simultaneous modes over a common fundamental in his Harvard lectures of February 1943. The C Lydian / C Phrygian notes in the glossary are a simple explanatory example of the modes, not an alleged quotation from a particular Bartók bar.
- [Boston Symphony Orchestra: Ives, The Unanswered Question](https://www.bso.org/works/the-unanswered-question), Jan Swafford. Supports Ives’s Danbury band and church background; the separate string, trumpet and woodwind layers; revisions in the 1930s; and first public performance in 1946. The page gives differing formulations of the early composition dates, so no exact original composition year is asserted.
- [Nashville Symphony: Charles Ives, The Unanswered Question](https://www.nashvillesymphony.org/classical-program-notes/charles-ives-the-unanswered-question/), Thomas May. Supports the trumpet’s seven statements, increasingly rapid and agitated woodwind responses, the final unanswered question, and the strings’ continuing independent music. “Question” and “answers” paraphrase the work’s programme; they are not invented spoken exchanges.
- [Open Music Theory: Twentieth-Century Rhythmic Techniques](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/twentieth-century-rhythmic-techniques/). Supports unequal beat groups, the 2+2+3 grouping of seven quavers, distinctions between notated metre and heard accent, and the definition of an ostinato. The three-against-two piano example in the glossary is an invented explanatory example, not a repertoire claim.

## Old forms and instrumental sound

- [IRCAM: Pulcinella](https://ressources.ircam.fr/en/work/pulcinella). Supports the 1919–20 composition, Diaghilev’s Pergolesi-attributed material, Massine and Picasso, the 15 May 1920 Paris première and the chamber scoring without clarinets or percussion. The prose says that the supplied music was attributed to Pergolesi; it does not assert that all the source pieces are actually his.
- [Berliner Philharmoniker: Pulcinella Suite](https://www.berliner-philharmoniker.de/en/programme-notes/igor-stravinsky-pulcinella-suite-revised-version-from-1949/), Malte Krasting. Supports retaining much of the old melody while removing bars or beats and changing the harmonic setting. The later suite is distinguished from the 1920 ballet when using the page’s historical discussion.
- [BnF: Pulcinella première record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb396842046). Names Picasso’s designs, Massine as choreographer and performer of Pulcinella, and the première at the Paris Opéra on 15 May 1920. No claim about the original singers’ physical placement remains: the record identifies them but does not establish where they stood. [Mariinsky’s ballet synopsis](https://www.mariinsky.ru/en/playbill/playbill/2019/4/30/2_1930/?sid=98888) also documents the character’s amorous plot, without being treated as evidence for every staging detail of 1920.
- [Boston Symphony Orchestra: Prokofiev, Symphony No. 1, Classical](https://www.bso.org/works/prokofiev-symphony-no-1-classical), Harlow Robinson. Supports completion in 1917, four movements, paired winds and Haydn references; the third-movement gavotte’s octave leaps, bassoon grace notes and contrasting major triads; and its short duration. No diary quotation is used, since the programme note does not attach dates to each quoted diary passage.
- [Cal Performances: Philharmonia programme notes, 2012](https://calperformances.org/learn/program_notes/2012/pn_philharmonia.pdf), Richard Rodda, *Wozzeck* discussion and scene/form table. Supports the Berlin première on 14 December 1925, the Doctor’s dietary experiments, the Act I passacaglia, Act II’s five-movement symphonic scheme, and the Act III invention on one note. [Wozzeck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wozzeck), “Composition” and Act III scene descriptions, supplies the specific B and the two crescendos following Marie’s murder beside the pond. The prose marks the note B♮ and avoids unsupported claims that the whole opera is twelve-tone.
- [Boosey & Hawkes: Sonata for Two Pianos and Percussion](https://www.boosey.com/pages/Opera/catalogue/cat_detail?musicid=3343). Supports the 1937 date, the Basel première on 16 January 1938, Bartók and Ditta as the pianists, two percussionists, and the precise instrumentation, including xylophone, side drums with and without snares, suspended cymbal and tam-tam.
- [Camerata Pacifica: April 2025 programme notes](https://cameratapacifica.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/CP_Program-Notes_04_25.pdf), Rebecca Winzenried, Bartók discussion. Supports the quiet opening timpani roll and piano introduction and the première performers. The sound descriptions of wood, hammered strings, cymbal and tam-tam are editorial descriptions of the named instruments, not claims of having analysed a recording.

## Concert organisations, recording, film and political power

- [Arnold Schönberg Center: Waltzes by Johann Strauss](https://schoenberg.at/en/schoenberg/kompositionen/waltzes-by-johann-strauss), Therese Muxeneder. Supports the Society for Private Musical Performances, founded in 1918; its prohibition of applause and use of repeated performances; the financial constraints behind chamber arrangements; the 27 May 1921 fundraising concert; Schoenberg on violin, Berg on harmonium and Webern on cello; and Schoenberg inadvertently buying Webern’s manuscript for 9,000 crowns while attempting to raise its auction price. This replaces general statements about modern music needing more rehearsals than older orchestral repertory.
- [Barbican: BBC Symphony Orchestra 90th-birthday concert](https://www.barbican.org.uk/whats-on/event/bbc-sostasevska-90th-birthday-celebration). Supports the orchestra’s first public concert on 22 October 1930 under Adrian Boult and its full-time salaried players. No claim of being the first radio orchestra worldwide is made.
- [Library of Congress: A Brief History of Recorded Sound](https://www.loc.gov/static/managed-content/uploads/sites/6/2017/02/recorded_sound_timeline-2013.pdf), 1925 entry. Supports Columbia and Victor’s commercial adoption of successful electrical recording, a microphone replacing the acoustic horn, and the wider range of captured sound. The source also records an earlier electrical recording; 1925 is consequently described as commercial introduction by these firms, not the invention of the technology.
- [Mariinsky: Alexander Nevsky programme note](https://www.mariinsky.ru/en/playbill/playbill/2018/10/24/3_1900/). Supports the 1938 Eisenstein film and the following year’s concert cantata. [Alexander Nevsky (Prokofiev)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Nevsky_%28Prokofiev%29) supports the close-microphone distortion of horns for the Teutonic knights, separate brass and choral recording and mixing, and reorchestration and reduction of repetitions for the cantata. These studio details are drawn from that secondary account, not from listening or audio analysis.
- [Dmitri Shostakovich official site: 1936 chronology](https://www.shostakovich.ru/47ru). Reproduces and discusses the 28 January 1936 *Pravda* editorial attacking *Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District*. The era’s sole new direct quotation, “could end very badly”, is our rendering of «может кончиться очень плохо» from «Это игра в заумные вещи, которая может кончиться очень плохо». The editorial is unsigned; the page names the newspaper, date and work under attack. No individual author is invented and the passage is not attributed to a personal conversation with Stalin.
- [IRCAM: Serge Prokofiev biography](https://ressources.ircam.fr/en/composer/serge-prokofiev/biography). Supports the family’s settlement in Moscow in May 1936, subsequent foreign touring, and the authorities’ retention of his passport after a Western tour in 1938 under the pretext of an administrative formality. The prose does not describe the return to the Soviet Union as a single undated event or imply that the political circumstances were identical to Schoenberg’s expulsion.

## Painting and poetry

- [Lenbachhaus: Group Dynamics — The Blue Rider, English catalogue](https://www.lenbachhaus.de/fileadmin/Redaktion/Ausstellungen/2021/Der_Blaue_Reiter/Pressebereich/Neu/Gruppendynamik_DerBlaueReiter_EN_doppelseitig.pdf), section on music in the almanac. Supports Kandinsky, Münter and Marc attending the Schoenberg concert in Munich on 2 January 1911; the quartet and piano pieces on the programme; Kandinsky painting *Impression III* the next day; the ensuing correspondence; and Schoenberg’s essay, reproduced paintings and the musical contributions by Schoenberg, Berg and Webern in the 1912 almanac. This is a documented exchange, not an asserted causal influence of one pictured work on a particular composition.
- [Kunstmuseum Basel: Castaway Modernism exhibition booklet](https://kunstmuseumbasel.ch/en/file/5062/031b52f8/ZEM_Saalbooklet_EN.pdf), discussion of Marc’s *Fate of the Animals*. Supports the 1913 painting; loss of its right third in a storeroom fire in 1917; and Klee’s 1919 reconstruction using studies and reproductions, with darker colours deliberately making the lost portion legible. The diagonals are a description of the supplied image. The note does not project a prophetic knowledge of the coming war onto Marc.
- [Wikisource: Zone](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Alcools/Zone). The four preserved French lines match this text; the Eiffel Tower/shepherdess, bleating bridges, classical antiquity and already-old motorcars all occur in the supplied excerpt. This transcription is from the 1920 third edition, so it is not labelled a facsimile of the 1913 first edition. [BnF/CCFr: Zone in the Michel Décaudin papers](https://ccfr.bnf.fr/portailccfr/ark%3A/16871/004b1626210) places the poem under *Alcools* (1913). The era’s original poem and translation were not rewritten. The comparison with Stravinsky’s abrupt changes is explicitly editorial, not a claim that the poem influenced the ballet.

## Schematic scores and editorial limits

Both captions continue to identify their examples as invented. The row caption describes the existing first four bars and their pitch-class inversion around C: D maps to B♭, E to A♭, and F♯ remains the tritone. It makes no claim that this row belongs to Schoenberg. The rhythmic caption describes the existing E-quaver pattern, unchanged 4/4 and the regrouping from 3+3+2 to 2+3+3. These are explanations of the supplied schematics, not transcriptions of *The Rite of Spring*.

Removed or replaced: the blanket description of Schoenberg’s 1908 music as lacking a tonal centre; claims about unspecified conservatoire assumptions; general claims about score density and relative rehearsal demands; an undifferentiated comparison of Nazi and Soviet coercion; and assertions that painting, poetry and music were jointly solving an abstract problem of representation. The replacement accounts name works, performers, institutions and dated events. The previous external links in arts notes have moved into this record. No unresolved factual claim is knowingly retained.

- *Pravda*, 28 January 1936, unsigned, “Muddle Instead of Music”: «Это игра в заумные вещи, которая может кончиться очень плохо» ([shostakovich.ru](https://www.shostakovich.ru/47ru)); editorial unsigned ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muddle_Instead_of_Music)).

### Confirmed facts

- *Pierrot lunaire*: 1912, Berlin, commissioned by Albertine Zehme; one reciter and five players ([Schönberg Center](https://schoenberg.at/de/schoenberg/kompositionen/pierrot-lunaire-op-21)). *Nacht*: bass clarinet, cello, piano; E–G–E♭ “butterfly” cell; measures 1–26; giant black butterflies/moths ([Mălăncioiu, Musicology Papers 39/2](https://musicologypapers.edituramediamusica.ro/images/issues/MP_2024-1_XXXIX-2_03_Gabriel_Malancioiu.pdf)).
- Suite for Piano, Op. 25: 1921–23, gavotte and musette, calm low-register melody and accompanying pattern in the Intermezzo, Gigue in 2/2 with occasional 3/4 bars ([Eike Feß](https://schoenberg.at/en/schoenberg/kompositionen/suite-for-piano-op-25)).
- Webern Op. 21 (1928): first movement a double canon by inversion, horn 2 → clarinet → cello answered by horn 1 → bass clarinet → viola ([Open Music Theory](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/twelve-tone-analysis-examples-webern-op-21-and-24/)).
- Berg Violin Concerto: 1935; row G B♭ D F♯ A C E G♯ B C♯ E♭ F, its bold notes the open strings, each carrying a minor or major triad ([BSO](https://www.bso.org/works/berg-violin-concerto)).
- *Rite of Spring*: première 29 May 1913, Nijinsky’s stamping choreography; the Augurs chord repeated 32 times in the strings with accents on eight horns ([NAC](https://nac-cna.ca/en/event/notes/30031)); E♭ dominant seventh over an F♭ major triad ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rite_of_Spring)).
- Bartók: Lidi Dósa of Kibéd at Gerlice-puszta in 1904; phonograph from 1905–06 with Kodály; slowed playback for detail; descending fifth-shifting pentatonic songs; Slovak and Romanian collecting; Romanian fair copy taken to the United States in October 1940, volumes finished 1942 and 1945 ([Institute for Musicology](https://bartok-gyujtesek.zti.hu/en)).
- Ives, *The Unanswered Question*: 1908, revised 1935, first performance 11 May 1946 at Juilliard; Danbury; seven trumpet questions, woodwind answers ([BSO](https://www.bso.org/works/the-unanswered-question); [Nashville Symphony](https://www.nashvillesymphony.org/classical-program-notes/charles-ives-the-unanswered-question/)).
- *Pulcinella*: 1919–20, première 15 May 1920 with Massine as Pulcinella, Picasso designs, no clarinets or percussion in the 18-player orchestra ([IRCAM](https://ressources.ircam.fr/en/work/pulcinella); [BnF](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb396842046)); melodies “largely untouched”, bars or beats removed ([Berliner Philharmoniker](https://www.berliner-philharmoniker.de/en/programme-notes/igor-stravinsky-pulcinella-suite-revised-version-from-1949/)).
- *Classical* Symphony: completed 10 September 1917, four movements, paired winds, bassoon grace notes and octave leaps in the Gavotte, clashing major triads ([BSO](https://www.bso.org/works/prokofiev-symphony-no-1-classical)).
- *Wozzeck*: première 14 December 1925, Berlin State Opera; Act I scene 4 passacaglia for the Doctor; Act II a symphony in five movements; Act III scene 2 an invention on the single note B, with a twofold crescendo on B in the interlude after the murder ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wozzeck); [Cal Performances notes](https://calperformances.org/learn/program_notes/2012/pn_philharmonia.pdf)).
- Bartók Sonata: 1937, Basel première 16 January 1938, Bartók and Ditta Pásztory ([Boosey & Hawkes](https://www.boosey.com/pages/Opera/catalogue/cat_detail?musicid=3343)); quiet timpani roll and piano introduction ([Camerata Pacifica](https://cameratapacifica.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/CP_Program-Notes_04_25.pdf)).
- Society for Private Musical Performances: founded in 1918, approval and disapproval forbidden, works repeated; 27 May 1921 concert (Berg harmonium, Schoenberg first violin, Webern cello); Schoenberg “became the unintentional winner” of Webern’s waltz at 9,000 crowns ([Schönberg Center](https://schoenberg.at/en/schoenberg/kompositionen/waltzes-by-johann-strauss)).
- BBC Symphony Orchestra first public concert 22 October 1930, Boult, full-time salaried ([Barbican](https://www.barbican.org.uk/whats-on/event/bbc-sostasevska-90th-birthday-celebration)); electrical recording introduced by Columbia and Victor in 1925 ([Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/static/managed-content/uploads/sites/6/2017/02/recorded_sound_timeline-2013.pdf)).
- *Alexander Nevsky*: horns for the Teutonic knights played close to the microphones for a crackling, distorted sound; brass and chorus recorded in different studios and mixed ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Nevsky_(Prokofiev))); orchestra and chorus recorded in different rooms, brass blown into the microphone ([Peter Gutmann, Classical Notes](http://www.classicalnotes.net/classics5/nevsky.html)); cantata of 1939 with Prokofiev’s own “completely reorchestrated” and cuts of repetitions (Wikipedia quoting Prokofiev, 22 March 1942).
- Schoenberg 1933–36: expelled from the Berlin Academy, returned to the Jewish community in Paris (witness Marc Chagall, 24 July 1933), emigrated with wife and daughter, taught at the Malkin Conservatory in Boston and in New York, UCLA professorship in 1936 ([Schönberg Center timeline](https://schoenberg.at/en/schoenberg/timeline)).
- Prokofiev: family settled in Moscow May 1936; allowed to travel until a 1938 Western tour, after which the passport was confiscated “under the pretence of an administrative formality” ([IRCAM](https://ressources.ircam.fr/en/composer/serge-prokofiev/biography)).
- Kandinsky, 2 January 1911: Marc, Kandinsky, Münter and others heard Schoenberg’s Second Quartet and Three Piano Pieces; *Impression III* painted a day later; correspondence followed; the 1912 almanac carried Schoenberg’s “The Relationship with Text”, two of his paintings, and songs by Schoenberg, Berg and Webern bound in ([Lenbachhaus catalogue](https://www.lenbachhaus.de/fileadmin/Redaktion/Ausstellungen/2021/Der_Blaue_Reiter/Pressebereich/Neu/Gruppendynamik_DerBlaueReiter_EN_doppelseitig.pdf)).
- Marc, *Fate of the Animals*: 1913, Kunstmuseum Basel; right third lost in a storeroom fire; Klee’s 1919 reconstruction from studies and illustrations in darker colours (Kunstmuseum Basel booklet above).
- Score captions: the row in ABC bars 1–4 uses all twelve pitch classes; its inversion about C maps D to B♭, E to A♭ and keeps F♯; the accent patterns are 3+3+2 then 2+3+3 on eight quavers — all checked directly against the unchanged ABC.
