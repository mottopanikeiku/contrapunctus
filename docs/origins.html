Origins

Before Bach

A vibrating string, a remembered chant, a second voice: the techniques behind Bach’s music were made over two thousand years. Their history runs through Greek mathematics, monastic classrooms, Parisian choir stalls, Italian workshops and the first opera theatres.

𝄐
ic. 530 BC

A string divided

Pythagoras settled at Croton in southern Italy around 530 BC. He left no writings, and the musical discoveries attached to his name belong to a tradition assembled by later authors. What survives is a proposition that could be tested: the octave, fifth and fourth correspond to small whole-number ratios. Music already had long histories in Mesopotamia, Egypt and Greece. The ratios made its intervals available to mathematical demonstration.

The blacksmith storyLater writers describe Pythagoras discovering consonance from hammers of different weights. The experiment does not work as told: hammer weight alone does not determine pitch, and string frequency varies with the square root of tension, not directly with the suspended weight.

A monochord is one string stretched over a resonating box, with a movable bridge that changes its vibrating length. Keep the string’s tension and mass per unit length constant and halve that length: the frequency doubles, producing an octave. Stop the string at two-thirds of its original length and the frequency becomes three-halves, a perfect fifth above the original note. Three-quarters of the length gives four-thirds of the frequency, a perfect fourth. The frequency ratios are therefore 2:1, 3:2 and 4:3; the corresponding length ratios run in the opposite direction. A fifth divided by a fourth gives 9:8, the Pythagorean whole tone.

A real stretched string also vibrates in several patterns at once. Its whole length produces the fundamental; two equal sections produce the second partial, three sections the third, and so on. For an ideal flexible string these frequencies form the harmonic series: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 times the fundamental. Over C, the first six partials are C, C, G, C, E and G in successively higher registers. The third partial is a fifth above the second, the fourth a fourth above the third. The fifth introduces the pure major third, 5:4, above the fourth.

Sixteen partialsThe diagram gives integer multiples of C’s frequency. The seventh partial lies about 31 cents below the nearest equal-tempered B♭; the eleventh lies about 49 cents below F♯. A cent is one hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone.

The harmonic series supplies a modern acoustical explanation. Pythagorean tuning builds notes from successive pure fifths; its major third is 81:64, wider than the series’ 5:4. A flute, a violin and a voice emphasise their partials differently, which helps distinguish their timbres even on the same pitch. Bells and other vibrating bodies can have strongly inharmonic partials, whose frequencies depart from whole-number multiples of one fundamental.

Boethius’s De institutione musica, written around the beginning of the sixth century AD, carried Greek mathematical theory into Latin education. Music joined arithmetic, geometry and astronomy in the quadrivium: number studied in relation to sounding intervals. Ancient theorists organised scales through tetrachords — four notes spanning a fourth — and used terms such as harmonia and tonos in several distinct senses.

The Greek names Dorian, Phrygian and Lydian were later reassigned to church modes. Ancient Greek Dorian and medieval Dorian name different octave arrangements. A modern white-note scale from D to D is a useful first model of church Dorian. The names survived through changes of theory, repertory and musical practice.

Pythagoras sits in the lower-left foreground, writing beside a tablet of musical proportions. Raphael placed the mathematical study of consonance among philosophy’s principal disciplines, more than two millennia after Pythagoras. The Vatican’s account identifies the interval under discussion as the diatessaron, the fourth. The fresco records a Renaissance view of ancient theory.

iic. 750–1200

One line, many functions

Gregorian chant is a repertory of Latin liturgical melodies bearing the name of Pope Gregory the Great. Gregory died in 604; the repertory’s decisive formation and dissemination belong to the eighth- and ninth-century encounter between Roman worship and the Frankish church under the Carolingians. Its exact relationship to the separately transmitted Old Roman chant remains a subject of scholarship. The papal name gave authority to a body of music transmitted first through singers, then through books.

Chant is monophonic: one melodic line, whether sung by a soloist or a group. It serves two large structures, the Mass and the Divine Office, the daily cycle of services organised around psalms, readings and prayers. The Mass Ordinary includes recurring texts such as the Kyrie, Gloria and Sanctus; the Proper supplies texts that change with the day or feast. A syllabic chant gives mostly one note to each syllable. A melismatic chant stretches a syllable over many notes, sometimes turning one vowel into a substantial musical span.

Four finals, eight modesModes 1–2 end normally on D, 3–4 on E, 5–6 on F and 7–8 on G. The odd-numbered modes are authentic; their even-numbered partners are plagal. These eight categories describe melodic behaviour.

The eight-mode system classified chants by their final, range and characteristic melodic relationships. An authentic melody generally occupies the octave above its final, with some extension below; a plagal melody places the same final more centrally in its range. The customary names are Dorian and Hypodorian, Phrygian and Hypophrygian, Lydian and Hypolydian, Mixolydian and Hypomixolydian. Actual melodies often exceed the textbook compass. B♭ can appear where a white-note diagram would suggest B♮, and some older chants fit their assigned category imperfectly.

A psalm tone is a reusable formula. An opening intonation leads to a reciting pitch on which many syllables can be sung; a cadence marks the middle of the verse, and another closes it. The amount of recitation expands or contracts with the words. An antiphon, a short chant sung around the psalm, determines the appropriate tone and ending. Modal classification thus solved a practical problem: how to connect hundreds of different antiphons with a manageable stock of psalm formulas.

Reading a gestureStaffless neumes record ascent, descent, grouping and details of delivery without consistently fixing interval size. The St Gall Cantatorium, copied in the early tenth century, preserves exceptionally detailed signs for solo chants.

The earliest neumes sit above the text like marks of vocal movement. They usually assume that the singer already knows the melody. Later heighted notation makes relative pitch more visible, and the staff fixes it more precisely. Rhythmic interpretation remains less secure. The Latin accent, the grouping of notes and the liturgical sentence all shape delivery.

The Dies irae is a later medieval sequence. Its opening stanza describes the day of judgement and invokes David and the Sibyl as witnesses. The tune begins F–E–F–D–E–C–D–D, returning repeatedly to D without a leading-note C♯. Berlioz quotes it in the Symphonie fantastique; Rachmaninoff repeatedly uses its opening contour. Hildegard of Bingen’s twelfth-century songs show a different extension of monophony: unusually wide ranges and long melismas, composed for a women’s religious community.

Hildegard receives the fiery light of inspiration while a scribe records her words. The image belongs to the visual transmission of Scivias, her account of visions completed in 1151. Her songs and the musical drama Ordo Virtutum connect this visionary theology with the daily sung worship of her community. Their single melodic lines articulate register, verbal emphasis and formal return on their own.

iiic. 1025

A melody that can be read

Guido of Arezzo, a Benedictine monk active in the early eleventh century, addressed a costly part of a singer’s education: learning an enormous repertory by repetition. His Micrologus, written around 1025–28, and his other teaching texts combine interval theory with practical methods for reading unfamiliar chant. Earlier musicians had used pitch letters, reference lines and heighted neumes. Guido made these resources into a teachable system.

A staff gives pitch a repeatable location. A line labelled F or C fixes the surrounding succession of tones and semitones; the spaces count as positions just as the lines do. Coloured reference lines could make F and C recognisable before the singer read a letter. Four-line staves became standard in square chant notation after a period of varied layouts. The five-line staff familiar from a piano score was a later convention.

A clef is a letterThe C and F clefs identify one staff position, from which the others can be inferred. A change of clef need not change the melody: it can keep a wide-ranging phrase on the same small staff.

Guido also described learning intervals through a familiar hymn to John the Baptist, Ut queant laxis. In the teaching melody, six successive phrases begin one degree higher: C, D, E, F, G, A. Their opening syllables supply ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la: Ut queant laxis / resonare fibris / mira gestorum / famuli tuorum / solve polluti / labii reatum. The singer recalls a known phrase to find an unknown note. The crucial small interval is always mi–fa, the semitone between the third and fourth steps.

Later medieval teaching developed this into overlapping six-note units, or hexachords. The natural hexachord begins on C; the hard hexachord on G includes B♮; the soft hexachord on F includes B♭. Each has the same interval pattern: tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone. A melody extending beyond six notes requires a change of hexachord, called mutation. The pitch itself need not change when its syllable does: G can be sol in the natural hexachord and ut in the hard one.

Six syllablesThe seventh syllable si and the replacement do belong to solmisation histories later than Guido’s method. In the medieval system, each syllable can move between pitches.

The Guidonian hand puts the gamut — the available succession of named pitches — onto the joints and fingertips of a left hand. A teacher can point to a location; a singer retrieves its pitch-name and possible syllables. Surviving hand diagrams belong to the later transmission of Guidonian pedagogy; attribution of the familiar diagram to Guido himself remains uncertain. The painted hand is both a picture and a working memory aid: spatial position carries information that would otherwise have to be recited.

Notation expanded the written record while oral teaching continued. A staff can specify the interval between two notes while leaving pronunciation, vocal production and much rhythmic detail to the community that uses it. It also makes comparison possible: the same melody can be copied, corrected and discussed far from its original singers. When musicians begin coordinating independent lines in organum, that stable record of pitch becomes indispensable; exact duration will require another set of inventions.

The enlarged hand makes a human body into a diagram of pitch and solmisation. Dating from more than two centuries after Guido’s teaching, it records the method’s later use. The outer circle repeats the syllables that are distributed across the hand, giving the pupil two spatial routes through the same information.

ivc. 900–1200

A second voice above the chant

The anonymous ninth-century treatise Musica enchiriadis describes ways of singing a second part with a chant. Some passages proceed in parallel fourths or fifths, sometimes doubled at the octave; others hold one voice still while the other moves, allowing the parts to meet in unison. The treatise gives a written account of these polyphonic practices.

Organum encompasses several changing techniques. In the simplest parallel model, every note of a melody acquires a companion at a constant interval. In more independent writing, the voices can move in contrary directions, embellish their approach to a consonance or place several notes against one. Eleventh- and twelfth-century sources show a growing distance between the borrowed chant and the newly composed part. The chant supplies an inherited sequence; the added voice decides how much time each part of that sequence will occupy.

The tenor holdsTenor comes from Latin tenere, to hold. Here it names the part that holds the pre-existing chant. A single tenor note can support a long phrase in the upper voice.

In late-twelfth-century Paris, the repertory associated with Notre-Dame developed this contrast on a much larger scale. Léonin is linked with the Magnus liber organi, the ‘great book of organum’, a collection for important services. In sustained-note passages, one chant note lies underneath a freely unfolding upper line. In discant passages, both voices move in coordinated measured rhythm. A setting can pass between these textures and plainchant, reserving polyphonic elaboration for particular liturgical portions.

Our principal account of Léonin and Pérotin comes from a later thirteenth-century theorist conventionally called Anonymous IV. He credits Léonin with the great book and describes Pérotin’s revisions and compositions in three and four parts. The surviving manuscripts preserve a repertory copied and altered by successive musicians. The familiar connection between Pérotin’s four-part Viderunt omnes and Christmas 1198 rests on historical reconstruction.

Rhythmic modesPatterns of long and short values organise the upper parts. The shape and grouping of ligatures — several notes joined as one written sign — help indicate the pattern. Here ‘mode’ describes a rhythmic arrangement.

Viderunt omnes elaborates the Christmas gradual whose text begins ‘All the ends of the earth have seen the salvation of our God’. Three upper parts move above the chant-bearing tenor. At the opening, the first syllable is extended so far that the listener hears changing rhythmic combinations before hearing a complete word. Repeated figures pass between voices; coordinated rests expose the separate strands; unisons, fifths and octaves articulate points of arrival. The governing contrast is between the very slow progress of the inherited melody and the faster activity above it.

A discant section could be detached and replaced by another setting of the same tenor fragment: such a unit is called a clausula. Adding words to an upper part helped generate the thirteenth-century motet, in which different voices could eventually sing different texts at once. The route from chant to polyphony was therefore also a route from one shared liturgical utterance to several simultaneous layers of language. Counterpoint begins with the practical task of making those independent lines agree at chosen moments.

The Florence manuscript is one of the principal witnesses to the Notre-Dame repertory. Its written ligatures encode groups of notes. A later copy, the page records the work’s transmission.

vc. 1320–1370

Time can be counted twice

Around 1320, French musicians acquired more precise ways of writing divisions of musical time. The label ars nova, ‘new art’, is associated with Philippe de Vitry and the theoretical writings transmitted under his name. Thirteenth-century mensural notation, particularly that described by Franco of Cologne, had already made note shapes carry durational information. Fourteenth-century practice extended the system’s treatment of smaller values, binary division and syncopation.

Mensuration specifies how one value divides into the next. A breve can contain three semibreves, called perfect tempus, or two, imperfect tempus; a semibreve can contain three minims, major prolation, or two, minor prolation. These two decisions are independent. A circle and a semicircle, with or without a dot, eventually provide a compact visual vocabulary for the combinations. Our C-shaped common-time sign descends from the semicircle.

Duration in contextMedieval note values can change through context, imperfection, alteration and coloration. These rules determine the notes’ actual durations alongside the mensural sign.

Isorhythm separates a repeating rhythmic pattern from a repeating sequence of pitches. The rhythmic pattern is conventionally called the talea; the pitch sequence, the color. Their lengths need not agree. If three durations cycle through a five-note melody, the two beginnings meet again only after fifteen note attacks. A composer can also restate a rhythmic structure at a changed proportional speed. The procedure is especially associated with the tenor of fourteenth-century motets, although other voices may participate in the design.

The scheme becomes audible through its effects. A returning tenor rhythm regulates where upper voices can settle, where a cadence can arrive and where a new span begins. Above it, syncopation can carry a note across an expected division, and hocket can split a line between voices by alternating notes and rests. Vitry’s motets combine these resources with texts that can address political and ecclesiastical circumstances. The composed surface moves against a framework whose repetitions are often slower than an ordinary melodic phrase.

A modern analytical nameThe term ‘isorhythm’ was coined long after the medieval repertory to identify a compositional procedure.

Guillaume de Machaut, a poet as well as a composer, brought together courtly song, motet and large-scale sacred music. His four-voice Messe de Nostre Dame, usually dated to the 1360s, is the earliest surviving complete polyphonic Mass Ordinary attributed to one composer. It includes Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei and Ite, missa est. Earlier polyphonic Mass movements and assembled cycles survive. Its precise original occasion remains unknown; the familiar claim that it was written for Charles V’s coronation in 1364 is unsupported.

The shorter texts — Kyrie, Sanctus, Agnus Dei and dismissal — permit chant-based isorhythmic construction. The long Gloria and Credo move much more often in syllabic, coordinated declamation. A contratenor works against the tenor, while the two upper parts can carry more active lines; the lowest sounding part can change as the voices move. Machaut’s secular ballades, rondeaux and virelais organise another kind of recurrence through poetic refrains and fixed forms. His manuscript collections place music alongside narrative poems, making the control of musical time one part of a larger practice of authorship.

The allegory belongs to the presentation of Machaut as an author: Nature supplies the faculties with which he makes poetry and music. His literary and musical works circulated together in carefully organised books. Music stands beside Rhetoric because Machaut practised both.

vic. 1430–1600

Each voice has a sentence

Guillaume Dufay worked across the church and court networks that connected northern France, the Low Countries and Italy. His motet Nuper rosarum flores was written for the consecration of Florence Cathedral in 1436. Its large-scale proportional design retains an isorhythmic inheritance, while the sonorities of fifteenth-century music increasingly make room for thirds and sixths. The changing treatment of these familiar intervals altered how voices could combine between the open fifths and octaves of cadences.

A cantus firmus — literally a ‘fixed song’ — can organise an entire Mass by returning in successive movements. It may come from chant or from a secular melody such as L’homme armé, which Dufay and many later composers used. A voice may present it slowly while other parts move faster; a composer may change its register, proportion or position in the texture. Another technique, fauxbourdon, produces chains of sixth-chord sonorities through a partly prescribed relationship between voices. Each part retains its own melodic progress within these sonorities.

Imitation is an entryOne part states a melodic idea; another begins the same idea later, often at a different pitch. A point of imitation can run through the ensemble and then dissolve. In a strict canon, the copying continues throughout.

Josquin des Prez makes these entries an audible means of articulating a text. In Ave Maria … virgo serena, the opening greeting passes through successive voices before the texture changes. New clauses can receive new motifs, paired voices can answer other pairs, and all the singers can come together for a direct utterance. Imitation draws its force from contrast with fewer parts, longer notes and coordinated syllables. Ottaviano Petrucci’s Misse Josquin of 1502 gave one composer’s Masses a printed collection, helping the repertory circulate beyond the institutions that commissioned or copied it.

Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina’s Roman church music controls dissonance through the motion of individual parts. A suspension begins as a consonant note, is held while another voice changes beneath it, then resolves down by step. Passing notes usually occupy weaker positions. Leaps are balanced by stepwise movement, and the lines avoid repeatedly reaching perfect consonances in parallel motion. Later counterpoint teaching extracted a more regular classroom style from this varied musical practice.

The legend of the rescueThe Council of Trent met between 1545 and 1563. Its reforms concerned abuses in worship. The story that one performance of Palestrina’s Missa Papae Marcelli saved all polyphony from prohibition lacks contemporary evidence; Giuseppe Baini’s 1828 biography helped consolidate the legend.

The Missa Papae Marcelli, published in 1567, normally uses six voices and expands to seven in its final Agnus Dei. Its Gloria and Credo often align the words across groups of singers, giving polyphony clear declamation. Palestrina’s carefully prepared dissonances also make musical tension audible without requiring abrupt chromatic changes. To hear the mechanism, follow a single held note across the moment when the other voices move: the same pitch changes from stable to unstable before its own resolution.

Thomas Tallis’s Spem in alium, probably composed around 1570, uses forty separate parts arranged in eight five-part choirs. Entries travel through successive groups, accumulate and give way to passages for the full ensemble. Its original occasion and layout remain uncertain. The technical problem is concrete: maintaining independent lines while controlling when the listener hears one group, neighbouring groups or all forty voices. Polyphony can organise physical separation as well as melodic agreement.

The angels crowd around one book, their mouths opened differently as they sing. The panel belongs to the same early-fifteenth-century ecclesiastical culture in which Dufay’s music circulated. Its lectern and shared volume make the material conditions of choral reading visible. The facing musical panel gives their companions an organ and stringed instruments.

viic. 1400–1700

Wood, gut, wind and wire

The instruments of Bach’s ensembles had separate histories. Church organs, courtly lutes, domestic viol consorts and professional violin bands developed in different settings, with different ways of producing and sustaining a note. Their mechanisms mattered to composition. An organ can hold a tone while the key remains down and wind is supplied; a lute or harpsichord note begins to decay as soon as its string has been plucked. A bowed instrument can change pressure and speed during a single note.

The organ’s ancestry reaches back to the ancient Mediterranean hydraulis, whose water mechanism regulated wind pressure. Medieval European organs used bellows, and by the later Middle Ages builders were separating ranks of pipes into selectable stops. A stop gives access to a set of pipes with a particular construction and pitch level. At an 8-foot pitch a key sounds at its ordinary register, a 4-foot rank an octave higher, a 16-foot rank an octave lower. Multiple manuals and pedal keyboards eventually let an organist distribute parts and colours between hands and feet.

What eight feet meansThe foot designation refers to the nominal length of an open pipe at the bottom of a rank. Stopped pipes can sound the same pitch at roughly half that length. Mixture stops combine several higher ranks.

The European lute derives from the Arabic ʿūd, transmitted through Mediterranean contact. Its rounded back, fretted neck and paired strings, called courses, support both accompaniment and independent polyphony. Renaissance players could reduce a vocal piece to one instrument, deciding which voices to sustain, repeat or ornament when several lines competed for the same hand. Tablature often records where the fingers go rather than giving every sounding pitch on a staff. Longer-necked bass members of the lute family, especially the theorbo, later supplied low notes and chords to the continuo group.

Viols and violins form distinct, related bowed families. Renaissance viols usually have frets and six strings tuned mainly in fourths with a central third; they are supported against or between the legs, including the smaller sizes. Their consort repertory distributes polyphonic lines among treble, tenor and bass instruments. The violin family normally has four strings tuned in fifths and no frets. Its curved bridge helps the bow select one string, while the fretless fingerboard lets the left hand adjust intonation freely.

The Cremonese traditionAndrea Amati’s surviving sixteenth-century instruments are central evidence for the early violin family. Nicolò Amati and Antonio Stradivari developed the Cremonese tradition in the seventeenth century. A formal apprenticeship of Stradivari to Nicolò remains uncertain.

Andrea Amati, his descendants and the other Cremonese workshops established models that later makers repeatedly studied. Stradivari’s career extended into Bach’s lifetime; he died in 1737. Many old instruments subsequently acquired altered necks, bass bars and other fittings, combining a seventeenth-century body with a later playing setup. Early violin ensembles served dances, ceremonies and theatre as well as the developing sonata. Sixteenth-century bass instruments varied in size and tuning; the cello’s orchestral prominence came later.

A harpsichord key raises a jack whose plectrum plucks a string; a damper stops the string when the key is released. Its rectangular relatives include the virginals, familiar from Dutch paintings. Unlike a piano, the harpsichord does not normally make a note louder simply because the player strikes the key harder. Articulation, timing, added notes and changes of register produce its contrasts. It can therefore sustain the harmonic outline of a vocal piece through repeated or newly voiced chords even while each individual string is decaying.

The singer holds a lute while a violin and music books lie on the table. The painted notation includes madrigals by Jacques Arcadelt: repertory written for separate vocal parts could also belong to a singer’s instrumental practice. The presence of both lute and violin matters more than a general association with ‘music’: it places two different string technologies beside the printed song they could help perform. The Metropolitan Museum’s study of the related version examines the instruments and identifiable music.

viiic. 1570–1607

The words take command

In Florence during the 1570s and 1580s, Giovanni de’ Bardi’s circle of musicians and scholars discussed how ancient Greek drama had been performed. The group later called the Camerata included Vincenzo Galilei, Galileo’s father, and the singer-composer Giulio Caccini. Their knowledge of ancient sound was fragmentary. What they could act on was an argument: a single voice, supported without competing words, could deliver a text more directly than several singers pursuing different phrases at once.

Monody means a solo vocal line with instrumental accompaniment. Around 1600, an explicitly declamatory style over a written bass gave this longstanding practice new forms in print and on stage. Caccini’s Le nuove musiche, published in 1602, offered songs with instructions on delivery and ornament. Jacopo Peri’s largely lost Dafne, performed in Florence in 1598, and his surviving Euridice of 1600 applied related ideas to continuous drama.

Recitative is composed speechA singer may repeat one pitch through several syllables, lengthen a stressed word and move at a cadence while the bass changes slowly. The rhythm follows dramatic syntax more closely than a dance, but the notes and bass still make a composed musical argument.

The dispute over expressive dissonance developed partly within the older medium of the polyphonic madrigal. In publications of 1600 and 1603, Giovanni Maria Artusi attacked passages from music by Claudio Monteverdi, initially without naming him. His examples included unprepared or unusually approached dissonances that violated the contrapuntal teaching Artusi defended. Removing the words from such examples made their intervals easy to inspect but excluded the evidence Monteverdi considered decisive: the particular meaning and emphasis of the text.

Monteverdi’s Fifth Book of Madrigals appeared in 1605 with Cruda Amarilli, one of the disputed works, and a brief response promising an account of his practice. In 1607 his brother Giulio Cesare’s explanation in the Scherzi musicali distinguished the seconda pratica, in which the words govern the musical treatment, from the inherited prima pratica. Monteverdi continued to compose in learned older styles, while allowing a poetic demand to justify a dissonance that a detached interval exercise would reject.

A documented sequenceThe publications, letters and performances behind the controversy are set out in the Journal of Seventeenth-Century Music chronology. The argument concerns madrigals before it becomes shorthand for the birth of opera.

L’Orfeo, with a libretto by Alessandro Striggio, was first performed at the Gonzaga court in Mantua on 24 February 1607. Its five acts combine recitative, solo song, choruses, dances and instrumental ritornellos, making the contrast between musical types part of the drama. A shepherd’s measured song and the Messenger’s report of Eurydice’s death demand different kinds of delivery. In ‘Tu se’ morta’, Orpheus’s repeated questions and widening vocal gestures turn the news into a resolve to enter the underworld.

The printed score of 1609 names a substantial instrumental collection, including strings, cornetts, trombones, harpsichords, organs, chitarroni and a double harp. The contrasting bass resources help distinguish pastoral scenes from the underworld. In ‘Possente spirto’, Orpheus attempts to persuade the ferryman through increasingly elaborate song, with instrumental responses between the vocal phrases. The score supplies both an ornamented vocal line and a plainer alternative. Vocal display has a dramatic function: Orpheus is a musician trying to make his art change another character’s decision.

The courts supplied the earliest productions with money, performers and invited audiences. Venice’s public opera theatres, beginning in 1637, later changed that institutional arrangement. By then Monteverdi had served at San Marco since 1613; the composer of courtly Orfeo would also write for the city’s commercial stage. The new genre’s continuity lay in a practical division of labour: the singer delivers the words, the bass supports their pacing, and instruments can intervene when speech alone is no longer the scene’s only action.

The sitter’s identification rests on comparison with the securely identified engraving published in the commemorative Fiori poetici of 1644. The portrait is later than Orfeo and belongs to the composer’s Venetian decades. Strozzi also worked in Venice: painting and opera shared a city of commissioned portraits, church appointments and, from 1637, ticket-selling musical theatres.

ixc. 1600–1722

The bass becomes a working plan

A continuo part gives an accompanist a bass line from which to build the harmony. Figures specify intervals above that bass; the player supplies a suitable spacing, texture and voice-leading. A keyboard or plucked-string instrument can provide the harmony, often with a bowed bass instrument or bassoon reinforcing the written line. The combination varies with place, genre and available musicians. ‘Continuo’ names the practice and its function across these different ensembles.

Some roots of the practice lie in organ accompaniment that followed the lowest sounding voice of a choral texture, the basso seguente. Around 1600, an independent written bass became central to solo song, sacred concertos and theatre. Lodovico da Viadana’s Cento concerti ecclesiastici of 1602, for small vocal forces with organ, helped disseminate the practice. A composer could now write a solo line against a continuous harmonic support, leaving the inner parts to the accompanist.

Read upwards from the bassA figure 6 normally asks for a sixth above the written note, with a third commonly understood. A bare sharp or flat commonly alters the third. The figures describe sounding intervals measured from the bass.

With C in the bass, an unfigured consonant sonority will often contain E and G. With E in the bass and a 6, the player may supply G and C: the same C-major triad in another position. The bass note can therefore differ from the chord’s root. A 4–3 figure can require C to remain over a G bass before resolving down to B. That one suspension joins the older discipline of prepared dissonance to a notation designed for practical accompaniment. Realisation still requires decisions about register, doubling, rhythm and the movement of each part.

Seventeenth-century music increasingly organises these sonorities around a tonic and cadences that confirm it. A bass moving from G to C, together with B rising to C in another voice, can establish C as a local centre. Repeated cadential patterns, sequences and contrasting key areas gradually make major–minor tonality a stronger organising principle. Modal habits, contrapuntal formulas and newer bass-led patterns coexist for decades. Rameau’s Traité de l’harmonie of 1722 later explains chords through roots and inversions, distinguishing a theoretical fundamental bass from the lowest notes actually played.

The tuning compromiseTwelve pure fifths differ from seven octaves. A tuning must distribute or isolate the discrepancy. Changing the tuning changes the sizes of thirds and fifths while the written notes stay the same.

Quarter-comma meantone narrows the fifths so that several major thirds become pure 5:4 intervals. On a conventional twelve-note keyboard, however, the chain leaves a badly mistuned ‘wolf’ interval and makes some remote keys impractical. Extra keys or split accidentals can provide alternatives, but require a different instrument. Twelve-tone equal temperament instead divides the octave into twelve equal semitones. Every key then has the same interval pattern: fifths are slightly narrow and major thirds appreciably wider than pure thirds. These adjustments allow unrestricted transposition.

Unequal circulating temperaments offer another solution: all keys remain usable, but their intervals differ. ‘Well-tempered’ encompasses a wider range of tunings than ‘equal-tempered’. Bach’s first book of The Well-Tempered Clavier, dated 1722, contains a prelude and fugue in each of the twelve major and twelve minor keys. The title leaves the precise tuning open. The practical inheritance visible in those pieces includes far more than tuning: a fixed staff, measured durations, independent voices, recurring bass patterns and the ability to carry a musical argument through several keys.

The two halves of a continuo team in one room: a woman with her back to us plays a cello — a bowed instrument that can sustain a bass line — while another sits at a harpsichord, the instrument that turned a figured bass into chords. Ter Borch paints the satin and the scroll of the cello more carefully than any music on the desk; the picture records the instruments and the domestic setting of accompanied music.
