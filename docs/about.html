About Contrapunctus

A listening museum in daylight.

Real performances, a guide to what you are hearing, and a way to follow the music into scores, lives and documents.

One institution, three doors

You do not need to read music to begin. Listen offers performances with short notes about a phrase, a texture or a returning idea. Explore follows composers, periods and the objects around the music. Learn explains a musical idea through an example before asking you to go deeper. The same player stays with you as you move between them.

This is for a newcomer finding a first piece, a hobbyist following a curiosity, a student reading a score, and a historian or archivist asking which performance or document supports a claim. Those are different ways of entering the same collection, not separate levels of membership. Listening, reading and the source records are free to access.

The collection, and its limits

The free recordings currently concentrate on repertoire from approximately 1700–1930. The reading reaches the present, with an Origins path looking further back. This is a selected view of Western classical music, not an exhaustive account of musical traditions, people or places.

Recording availability and reuse rights shape what can be heard here. A composer’s absence from the player does not imply that their music is unimportant or that no free recording exists. Reading pages also recommend recordings to seek out elsewhere; those recommendations do not grant us permission to stream or redistribute them.

Keep four dates distinct: a composer’s life, the creation or revision of a work, the performance or recording session, and the publication or transfer of a particular file. Lifelines charts the first of these. Its period colours are overlapping editorial groupings, not a claim that one style ended on a particular day.

How the listening notes are made

Listening notes combine score study and documentary sources with measurements of the particular recording. Measurements can suggest a boundary, a silence, a change of level or a repeated passage. They cannot, by themselves, identify an oboe entrance, a sung word or a composer’s intention.

No aural timing sign-off yetNo one on the team has yet checked the listening-note timings by ear against these recordings. Score alignment is provisional until checked by ear. A precise-looking time or a moving score display is not evidence that this review has happened.

Each note’s How we know disclosure makes its basis visible. The basis and the precision answer different questions: where the claim comes from, and how narrowly its position can be located.

From the score / score-aligned
A score location mapped to this performance. The written pitches or bars and their estimated placement in the recording are separate claims; the timing remains provisional.
Score proportions
A position estimated from the score’s structure or bar counts, with allowance for performance timing. Read this as approximate.
Audio feature
A position supported by a measured change in this recording, such as a silence or level boundary. The measurement does not establish an instrument’s identity.
Documented fact
A historical or biographical statement based on a source. Its display time is not a date or a musical event.
General description
An invitation to notice something through a stretch of music, not a claim about one exact instant.
Moment
An intended location within about two seconds, supported by alignment or a strong audio boundary. This is a precision label, not an aural-review certificate.
Passage
A description of the surrounding stretch. It should still make sense if displayed a little before or after a local event.

Pictures of sound are models

Machine estimates are labelled. Estimated notes can confuse overtones with played notes and can miss inner voices in a dense mixture. Estimated key regions are not an authoritative harmonic analysis. A spectrogram shows energy at frequencies, including overtones; it is not a score. Repeated-span analysis proposes relationships that need musical interpretation.

Short notation examples distinguish quotations from the score from original teaching schematics. Their synthesised playback is a demonstration, not the performance in the recording. Art pairings also carry an evidence label: documented use, documented encounter, shared context or editorial comparison. The full relationship note matters more than the label alone.

Sources and rights

The recording is a particular object

Audio files in the player come from Wikimedia Commons. Each recording page has a dossier for its performers, session information, carrier or release where known, transfer credits, source chain and rights. Documented, inferred, disputed, unknown and not-applicable fields are kept distinct. Conflicting dates should remain visible rather than being resolved by guesswork; an upload or release date is not automatically a session date.

Commons is an access point and metadata source, not an infallible authority. Follow a dossier’s links to the original carrier, archive, performer or upstream transfer where one is documented. Biographies, work notes and lessons give further reading and links to source documents; an interpretative comparison is not a substitute for a citation.

A composition is not its recording

The rights in a musical composition, an edition or arrangement, a performance and its sound recording can differ. A public-domain composition does not make a recent recording public domain. A free-to-listen page does not itself permit copying. Creative Commons licences may require attribution, a licence link, identification of changes or share-alike terms; use the licence attached to the actual file.

Copyright status depends on jurisdiction. A tag such as PD-EU-audio addresses particular European rules; it is not a promise of worldwide freedom to reuse. United States and other national rules may differ. The dossiers retain caveats rather than replacing them with a blanket “public domain” claim. Consult the source record and the law relevant to your intended use; this site is not legal advice.

Images and texts

The image reproductions are sourced from Commons and re-encoded as WebP derivatives for this site. The credits below link every file to its source and licence or public-domain statement. The status of an old artwork and the status of its photograph or reproduction are distinct questions. Read the source file, especially for modern photographs, and retain the required credit.

Texts identify their author and the excerpt or version where provided. “Our translation” means a Contrapunctus translation, not a historical translation by the composer. Translation, transcription and interpretation should not be mistaken for the source document itself.

How to cite

Use the permanent page address, its title and the date you consulted it. Give the particular recording or object, rather than citing only a composer’s name.

Contrapunctus, “Page title”, Contrapunctus, page URL, accessed day month year.
  • A listening note: identify the composer, work and movement, performers, recording-page URL, note title and timestamp or range. Include the displayed basis and precision when the timing matters. A link with ?t=88, for example, points to 88 seconds in that recording; it does not certify the timing.
  • A recording: use the dossier’s citation, including the session or issue information actually documented, the Commons file page, upstream source or transfer credit and access date. Preserve uncertainty and distinguish the recording from the composition.
  • An image or text: cite maker or author, title, date or edition, collection or repository, the Commons or textual source URL, and the licence or rights statement where relevant. The object’s link in Art and documents identifies its relationship notes here.

For a historical claim, cite the underlying document as well as our discussion when it is available. For a quotation, record the edition and page, folio or bar number supplied by that source.

Report an error

A correction is most useful when it can be checked. Include the page URL, the words or field in question, a source or explanation, and, for a recording, its title and the time range you heard. Please distinguish a mistaken claim from an uncertain one.
