The atlas

Composers

60 composers in seven chapters, ordered by year of birth. Those marked ✦ have extended portraits.

𝄢
I

The Baroque

1600–1750
1668–1733

François Couperin

A Parisian organist whose harpsichord pieces turn ornament, delayed resolution and the timing of a released key into structural events. 1678–1741

Antonio Vivaldi

A violinist and theatre composer who made the returning orchestral refrain organise a soloist’s changing keys, textures and physical risks. 1683–1764

Jean-Philippe Rameau

A theorist of chord roots who began his operatic career at fifty and made harmonic surprise part of French theatrical action. 1685–1750

Johann Sebastian Bach

An organist, teacher and church musician who made a bass line, a dance rhythm and a fugue subject carry the same argument. 1685–1759

George Frideric Handel

A German-trained composer of Italian opera who made the English chorus an actor in public musical drama. 1685–1757

Domenico Scarlatti

An Italian court musician in Lisbon and Madrid who made keyboard sonatas out of hand crossings, repeated notes and abrupt changes of harmonic ground.
II

Classical

1750–1827
1714–1788

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach

A keyboard player who made hesitation, interruption and a change of mind into musical form. 1732–1809

Joseph Haydn

A court musician who taught the symphony and quartet to argue with the listener’s expectations. 1756–1791

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

An opera composer whose instrumental music gives different voices the freedom to disagree. 1770–1827

Ludwig van Beethoven

A pianist who turned small rhythmic cells into long arguments, then rebuilt the forms that contained them.
III

Romanticism

1815–1890
1797–1828

Franz Schubert

A Viennese songwriter who made the piano an equal narrator, and carried the harmonic dislocations of the Lied into symphonies and chamber music. 1803–1869

Hector Berlioz

He made orchestral colour, the position of a sound in space, and the return of a remembered melody into structural events. 1805–1847

Fanny Hensel

Her Berlin musical gatherings supported a body of songs, piano cycles and chamber works whose public publication began only near the end of her life. 1809–1847

Felix Mendelssohn

A composer-conductor who turned quicksilver string writing, Bachian counterpoint and remembered landscapes into tightly organised concert music. 1810–1849

Frédéric Chopin

He made the piano sing through ornament, contrapuntal voice-leading and the unstable accents of Polish dance. 1810–1856

Robert Schumann

His music lets remembered fragments, concealed names and competing inner voices interrupt the forms that contain them. 1811–1886

Franz Liszt

The touring pianist who made a single theme change character, and eventually stripped virtuosity down to a few unresolved notes. 1813–1901

Giuseppe Verdi

An opera composer who made a change of vocal phrase, bass note or ensemble balance alter the relations between people on stage. 1813–1883

Richard Wagner

An opera composer who made the orchestra remember what the characters conceal, and built a theatre for the result. 1819–1896

Clara Schumann

A concert pianist for more than sixty years, she composed songs, chamber music and piano works whose technical demands arise from the independence of their voices. 1824–1896

Anton Bruckner

A church organist who built symphonic movements from long harmonic spans, separate instrumental blocks and the silences between them. 1833–1897

Johannes Brahms

A composer who made a small interval generate a large form, and let conflicting rhythms keep a familiar melody unsettled. 1839–1881

Modest Mussorgsky

He made Russian speech, public crowds and the noises of ordinary life determine the shape of opera. 1840–1893

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

He used the timing of dance and theatre to make a symphony’s return, interruption or collapse physically audible. 1841–1904

Antonín Dvořák

A theatre violist who made dance accents, remembered songs and classical development work inside the same movement. 1844–1908

Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov

A naval officer turned teacher who made orchestral register and symmetrical scales the machinery of Russian fairy-tale opera.
IV

Fin de siècle

1880–1918
1854–1928

Leoš Janáček

A Moravian composer who studied the inflections of speech and built operas from short cells, repeated figures and abrupt changes of perspective. 1857–1934

Edward Elgar

A Worcester violinist who made orchestral variations into portraits of friends and the concerto into an argument with memory. 1858–1924

Giacomo Puccini

An opera composer who made a remembered tune, a stage noise or a change of orchestral register determine the timing of a scene. 1860–1911

Gustav Mahler ✦

A conductor who composed in the summers, and wanted each symphony to hold a whole world — the sublime and the street band together. 1864–1949

Richard Strauss

An orchestral storyteller who took opera to the harmonic edge in Salome and Elektra, then made conversation and recollection its central events. 1865–1957

Jean Sibelius

A symphonist who let small figures gather into themes and movements change shape while their underlying pulse continued. 1872–1915

Alexander Scriabin

A pianist who replaced the need for a resolving tonic with recurring harmonic fields, accelerating figures and carefully organised light. 1873–1943

Sergei Rachmaninoff ✦

A composer-pianist who built long melodies over tolling basses, and carried Russian chant into the concertos and dances of his American exile.
V

The French Line

1870–1937
1845–1924

Gabriel Fauré

A church organist who made the French song and the chamber ensemble laboratories for harmony. 1862–1918

Claude Debussy

He made a chord’s spacing, a scale’s missing notes and an instrument’s register determine the course of a piece. 1866–1925

Erik Satie

A cabaret pianist who separated repetition from development, and musical seriousness from ceremonial behaviour. 1875–1937

Maurice Ravel ✦

An engineer of instrumental colour who made a piano imitate water, an orchestra imitate a machine, and old dances carry the names of the dead. 1893–1918

Lili Boulanger

Her songs and psalms give a sustained bass, a repeated interval and the weight of a choral unison the force of a large formal argument. 1899–1963

Francis Poulenc

His songs, concertos and church music keep a singable line in view while the harmony changes its footing without warning.
VI

Modernism

1908–1945
1874–1954

Charles Ives

A church organist and insurance executive who made hymns, marching bands and incompatible tempos coexist inside concert music. 1874–1951

Arnold Schoenberg

A Viennese teacher who took motivic development beyond the key signature, then made twelve notes carry the work of a tonal system. 1881–1945

Béla Bartók

A pianist and fieldworker who turned the modes, rhythms and performance habits of peasant music into new kinds of quartet and orchestral argument. 1882–1971

Igor Stravinsky

A composer of ballets who made the placement of an accent as consequential as a change of harmony. 1883–1945

Anton Webern

A conductor and scholar of Renaissance polyphony who compressed canons and variations into a few exposed notes. 1885–1935

Alban Berg

A composer of operas and coded chamber music who made twelve-tone rows accommodate triads, folk song and a Bach chorale. 1891–1953

Sergei Prokofiev ✦

A pianist who made the keyboard strike like percussion, and a theatre composer whose longest melodies keep their footing through abrupt changes of key. 1906–1975

Dmitri Shostakovich

Fifteen symphonies and fifteen quartets turn marches, fugues and a four-note signature into arguments about public ceremony and individual memory.
VII

After 1945

1945–present
1908–1992

Olivier Messiaen

An organist who made Catholic theology, symmetrical modes, unequal rhythms and transcribed birdsong the materials of a new musical language. 1913–1976

Benjamin Britten

An opera composer who made the English speaking voice, the Suffolk coast and the repeated bass into instruments of drama. 1923–2006

György Ligeti

He packed canons so closely that their lines disappeared, then made competing pulses and unequal tunings audible again. 1925–2016

Pierre Boulez

He extended the series beyond pitch, then spent decades turning its ordered material into proliferating instrumental forms. 1926–1987

Morton Feldman

Soft attacks, unequal repetitions and lengths beyond ordinary musical memory replace the argument towards a climax. b. 1926

György Kurtág

A composer of messages, memorials and interrupted sentences, who can make two notes carry the weight of a scene. 1931–2025

Sofia Gubaidulina

She made the crossing of registers, the pressure of a bow and the breath of an accordion carry the argument of a sacred drama. b. 1935

Arvo Pärt

He joined a moving scale to the notes of a single triad, then used that rule to rebuild his musical language. b. 1936

Steve Reich

He made the changing relationship between repeated patterns audible, then brought speech, Hebrew psalms and recorded testimony into the process. b. 1947

John Adams

Repeated pulses support changing harmonies, long orchestral arguments and operas about the people who make public history. 1952–2023

Kaija Saariaho

She made the passage between a bowed note and the noise around it into a way of composing harmony, form and opera. b. 1971

Thomas Adès

His harmonies move by independent interval cycles, so a familiar chord can become unfamiliar without any voice breaking its rule.
