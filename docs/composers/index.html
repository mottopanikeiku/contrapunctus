Composers

71 composers in seven chapters, ordered by year of birth. Or browse every work in the Concert Hall.

𝄢
I

The Baroque

1600–1750
1668–1733

François Couperin

He played for Louis XIV on Sundays and taught the harpsichord to speak through a held note, a trill and a well-placed silence. 1678–1741

Antonio Vivaldi

He wrote concertos for the women of Venice’s Pietà, operas for its theatres, and a barking dog for the viola. 1683–1764

Jean-Philippe Rameau

He explained the roots of chords in a treatise, then sent them through hell in his first opera, at fifty. 1685–1750

Johann Sebastian Bach

He supplied Leipzig’s churches with music for twenty-seven years and left keyboard players enough work for a lifetime. 1685–1759

George Frideric Handel

He filled London theatres with Italian opera, then put an English-speaking chorus at the centre of the drama. 1685–1757

Domenico Scarlatti

A royal teacher whose sonatas send the hands flying across the keyboard, then bring them together for a song.
II

Classical

1750–1827
1714–1788

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach

Frederick the Great’s accompanist wrote keyboard music that breaks off mid-sentence and makes a single note tremble. 1732–1809

Joseph Haydn

He could empty an orchestra one player at a time, or keep a quartet going after everyone thought the piece had ended. 1756–1791

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

In his operas a servant can outwit a count; in his concertos the piano can interrupt before the orchestra has finished its opening sentence. 1770–1827

Ludwig van Beethoven

He could make a symphony out of four notes, and spent his last years finding things four string players had never said.
III

Romanticism

1815–1890
1792–1868

Gioachino Rossini

He made a roomful of quarrelling people sing like clockwork, then gave William Tell an orchestra of mountains and storms. 1797–1828

Franz Schubert

At seventeen he set a spinning wheel turning under Gretchen’s voice; at thirty-one he could make two cellos sing as if they had always belonged together. 1803–1869

Hector Berlioz

He could make an orchestra thunder, and knew when to leave a single cor anglais calling into silence. 1805–1847

Fanny Hensel

She ran Berlin’s Sunday concerts, wrote more than 450 works, and put her own name on published collections only in the last year of her life. 1809–1847

Felix Mendelssohn

At sixteen, he made eight string players sound like the most exhilarating orchestra in Europe. 1810–1849

Frédéric Chopin

He brought the opera singer’s breath and the mazurka’s wayward accents to a piano that could whisper across a room. 1810–1856

Robert Schumann

A bookseller’s son who put lovers, masks and arguments into piano music, and made criticism a form of friendship. 1811–1886

Franz Liszt

He could fit an orchestra under ten fingers, then end a lifetime at the piano with a few notes left hanging in the air. 1813–1901

Giuseppe Verdi

He could make a catchy tenor tune sound like a death sentence simply by letting the wrong man hear it. 1813–1883

Richard Wagner

He hid the orchestra beneath the stage at Bayreuth and taught it to remember everything his characters wanted to forget. 1819–1896

Clara Schumann

‘It is the air I breathe,’ she wrote of making music, after forty years on the concert platform. 1824–1896

Anton Bruckner

He spent half his life learning his trade, then wrote symphonies in which a horn call can grow into a wall of brass. 1833–1897

Johannes Brahms

He kept his First Symphony waiting until he was forty-three, then began the Second beside an Austrian lake the next summer. 1838–1875

Georges Bizet

A factory worker sings that love obeys no law. Beneath her voice, four bass notes keep the dance going. 1839–1881

Modest Mussorgsky

He could make a singer sound like a frightened child, a drunken monk or a tsar who can no longer bear his own thoughts. 1840–1893

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

He could make a ballroom glitter, then let one unguarded melody tell us who was unhappy there. 1841–1904

Antonín Dvořák

He had played viola in the opera pit for nine years before the world discovered the composer of the <i>Slavonic Dances</i>. 1843–1907

Edvard Grieg

The Bergen merchant’s son who put Norwegian fiddle tunes, a troll king and a wedding party into concert-hall music. 1844–1908

Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov

He became a professor before he could write counterpoint, then built Russian fairy-tale operas around an orchestra he knew instrument by instrument.
IV

Fin de siècle

1880–1918
1854–1928

Leoš Janáček

He wrote down the voices of strangers in Brno, then gave opera some of its most painfully recognisable people. 1857–1934

Edward Elgar

He made the <i>Enigma Variations</i> from his friendships and preserved his own way with the music on gramophone records. 1858–1924

Giacomo Puccini

He could make a whole theatre wait with a woman at a window, then break its heart with a tune it had heard before. 1860–1911

Gustav Mahler

A conductor who composed in the summers, and wanted each symphony to hold a whole world — the sublime and the street band together. 1864–1949

Richard Strauss

The son of a horn player, he gave the orchestra a taste for scandal and the soprano some of her longest, loveliest farewells. 1865–1957

Jean Sibelius

He watched sixteen swans circle over Ainola, gave their flight to the horns, and spent four years getting the Fifth Symphony right. 1867–1944

Amy Beach

A New Hampshire prodigy taught herself the orchestra from the Boston Symphony’s concerts, then gave Boston a symphony built on Irish tunes. 1872–1915

Alexander Scriabin

He wrote a part for coloured light that had to be omitted at the work’s first performance. 1873–1943

Sergei Rachmaninoff

In exile he earned his living at the piano, while the bells of his Russian childhood kept sounding through the music he wrote.
V

The French Line

1870–1937
1835–1921

Camille Saint-Saëns

A pianist at ten and an organist for twenty years, he wrote the Organ Symphony, a dance of skeletons with a xylophone, and a zoo he forbade anyone to print. 1845–1924

Gabriel Fauré

He spent his days at the organ and in the classroom, saving for songs and chamber music the harmonies nobody else could quite imagine. 1862–1918

Claude Debussy

He trusted his ear over the rules — “pleasure is the law” — and made the piano, the flute and the orchestra sound new. 1866–1925

Erik Satie

The composer of the <i>Gymnopédies</i> asked pianists to prepare for 840 repetitions with ‘serious immobilities’. 1875–1937

Maurice Ravel

He made a ballet from two tunes and a snare drum, and gave his war dead a suite of dances. 1876–1946

Manuel de Falla

He took a Cádiz ear to Paris, learnt from Dukas and Debussy, and brought Andalusian song and the guitar into the orchestra. 1893–1918

Lili Boulanger

At nineteen she won the Prix de Rome; before her death at twenty-four she had written songs of lost love and psalms that shake the room. 1899–1963

Francis Poulenc

The pianist who wanted butter in the sauce wrote prayers, love songs and an opera in which a guillotine silences the choir.
VI

Modernism

1908–1945
1872–1958

Ralph Vaughan Williams

He wrote down an Essex folk song in 1903, edited the English Hymnal and carried a Tudor psalm tune into the concert hall. 1874–1954

Charles Ives

He sold life insurance in New York and wrote music in which a hymn across the river could outlast a whole marching band. 1874–1951

Arnold Schoenberg

He removed the home key from music, kept learning from Bach and Brahms, and found room for a minuet among all twelve notes. 1881–1945

Béla Bartók

He heard a future for concert music in village songs, and went out with a phonograph to learn how they were sung. 1882–1971

Igor Stravinsky

He could make a roomful of dancers lose their footing with one displaced accent, then write a psalm that scarcely moves. 1883–1945

Anton Webern

He passed melodies from horn to clarinet to cello, making a whole phrase out of sounds that each instrument could hold for only a moment. 1885–1935

Alban Berg

He gave an exploited soldier a voice in <i>Wozzeck</i> and fitted a Bach chorale into a twelve-note concerto in memory of Manon Gropius. 1887–1953

Florence Price

She put the juba dance and the brass hymn at the centre of the American symphony. 1891–1953

Sergei Prokofiev

He gave <i>Romeo and Juliet</i> its singing strings and the piano some of the twentieth century’s sharpest teeth. 1898–1937

George Gershwin

He carried Broadway’s rhythmic snap into the concerto, and made a clarinet slide the calling card of American concert music. 1900–1990

Aaron Copland

A Brooklyn modernist gave America its open fifths, its cowboy dances and a fanfare slow enough to let the brass ring. 1906–1975

Dmitri Shostakovich

He wrote fifteen symphonies under Soviet rule and signed some of his most personal music with four notes: D–E♭–C–B.
VII

After 1945

1945–present
1908–1992

Olivier Messiaen

For sixty years he played the organ at La Trinité, while birdsong and Yvonne Loriod’s piano carried his music far beyond the church. 1912–1992

John Cage

He put bolts between piano strings, composed with chance operations, and gave the sounds around a concert four minutes and thirty-three seconds of their own. 1913–1976

Benjamin Britten

He wrote Grimes and Aschenbach for Peter Pears, an arkful of animals for Suffolk children, and a requiem that lets dead soldiers speak. 1923–2006

György Ligeti

His orchestras hide dozens of moving parts inside one sustained sound, while his piano études set the hands running on different clocks. 1925–2016

Pierre Boulez

He wrote music of explosive attacks and lingering echoes, and built the ensembles, concert series and laboratories it needed. 1926–1987

Morton Feldman

He wrote a string quartet that can last six hours, and made a softly struck piano note worth waiting for. b. 1926

György Kurtág

His <i>Játékok</i> grew from children’s piano games into an address book of friends, birthdays and farewells. 1931–2025

Sofia Gubaidulina

Her <i>Offertorium</i> dismantles Bach’s royal theme and rebuilds it for Gidon Kremer’s violin. b. 1935

Arvo Pärt

He rebuilt his music from a melody and three notes, and made a descending scale carry a lament for Britten. b. 1936

Steve Reich

Two tape machines slipped out of time, and he taught pianists, drummers and singers to make music from the gap. b. 1947

John Adams

A New England clarinettist who put Nixon on stage and sent a symphony orchestra hurtling along to a woodblock beat. 1952–2023

Kaija Saariaho

She built music from the grain of a cello note and wrote an opera around two people who had never met. b. 1971

Thomas Adès

He put fishing reels in an opera pit and gave a string quartet a twenty-fifth hour.
