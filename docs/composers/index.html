The atlas

Composers

80 composers in seven chapters, ordered by year of birth. Every work in the Concert Hall is listed separately.

𝄢
I

The Baroque

1600–⁠1750
1619–⁠1677

Barbara Strozzi

Venetian singer and composer of cantatas, arias and madrigals, whose numbered books of vocal music appeared in Venice between 1644 and 1664. 1665–⁠1729

Élisabeth Jacquet de La Guerre

Parisian harpsichordist and composer who played for Louis XIV as a child and published harpsichord suites, violin sonatas, cantatas and the opera Céphale et Procris. 1668–⁠1733

François Couperin

Musician who played for Louis XIV on Sundays, known at the harpsichord for held notes, trills and well-placed silences. 1678–⁠1741

Antonio Vivaldi

Venetian composer of concertos for the women of the Pietà and operas for the city’s theatres, who wrote a barking dog for the viola. 1683–⁠1764

Jean-⁠Philippe Rameau

Composer and theorist who explained the roots of chords in a treatise and wrote his first opera at the age of fifty. 1685–⁠1750

Johann Sebastian Bach

Composer who supplied music for Leipzig’s churches for twenty-seven years and wrote a large body of keyboard music. 1685–⁠1759

George Frideric Handel

Composer who filled London theatres with Italian opera and later put an English-speaking chorus at the centre of the drama. 1685–⁠1757

Domenico Scarlatti

Composer and royal teacher, known for his keyboard sonatas.
II

Classical

1750–⁠1827
1714–⁠1788

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach

Accompanist to Frederick the Great and composer of keyboard music known for abrupt interruptions and trembling single notes. 1732–⁠1809

Joseph Haydn

Composer known for an orchestral work in which the players leave one at a time, and for a quartet that continues after listeners think it has ended. 1756–⁠1791

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

Composer of operas in which a servant outwits a count, and of piano concertos in which the soloist sometimes enters before the orchestra has finished its opening. 1770–⁠1827

Ludwig van Beethoven

Composer known for a symphony built from a four-note motif and for the string quartets of his last years.
III

Romanticism

1815–⁠1890
1792–⁠1868

Gioachino Rossini

Composer known for ensembles of quarrelling characters sung with clockwork precision, and for the orchestral depiction of mountains and storms in William Tell. 1797–⁠1828

Franz Schubert

Composer who at seventeen wrote a song for Gretchen with a spinning-wheel accompaniment and at thirty-one wrote music for two cellos. 1803–⁠1869

Hector Berlioz

Composer who used the full orchestra at full volume and also a single cor anglais playing alone against silence. 1805–⁠1847

Fanny Hensel

Berlin composer who ran the city’s Sunday concerts, wrote more than 450 works and published collections under her own name only in the last year of her life. 1809–⁠1847

Felix Mendelssohn

Composer who wrote a work for eight string players at the age of sixteen. 1810–⁠1849

Frédéric Chopin

Composer for the piano, known for music that drew on operatic singing and on the irregular accents of the mazurka. 1810–⁠1856

Robert Schumann

Composer and music critic, a bookseller’s son, whose piano music depicts lovers, masks and arguments. 1811–⁠1886

Franz Liszt

Pianist whose playing drew orchestral sound from the keyboard, and who at the end of his life turned to sparse music at the piano. 1813–⁠1901

Giuseppe Verdi

Composer whose catchy tenor tunes could carry a fatal meaning when the wrong man heard them. 1813–⁠1883

Richard Wagner

Composer who placed the orchestra beneath the stage at Bayreuth, hidden from the audience. 1819–⁠1896

Clara Schumann

Performer on the concert platform for forty years, who wrote of making music, “It is the air I breathe.” 1824–⁠1896

Anton Bruckner

Composer who studied his craft for half his life before writing symphonies in which a horn call can expand into full brass. 1824–⁠1884

Bedřich Smetana

Czech composer and conductor from Litomyšl, whose operas for Prague’s Provisional Theatre include The Bartered Bride; deaf from 1874, he then wrote Má vlast and the quartet From My Life. 1833–⁠1887

Alexander Borodin

Russian chemist, professor at the Medical-⁠Surgical Academy in Saint Petersburg, who composed two symphonies, two string quartets and the opera Prince Igor. 1833–⁠1897

Johannes Brahms

Composer who completed his First Symphony at the age of forty-three and began his Second beside an Austrian lake the following summer. 1838–⁠1875

Georges Bizet

Composer of an opera in which a factory worker sings that love obeys no law, while four bass notes beneath her voice keep the dance going. 1839–⁠1881

Modest Mussorgsky

Composer who could make a singer sound like a frightened child, a drunken monk or a tsar who could no longer bear his own thoughts. 1840–⁠1893

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

Composer who could write a brilliant ballroom scene and, within it, a single melody showing that someone present was unhappy. 1841–⁠1904

Antonín Dvořák

Composer of the Slavonic Dances, who played viola in an opera orchestra for nine years before his music became widely known. 1843–⁠1907

Edvard Grieg

Composer, the son of a Bergen merchant, who used Norwegian fiddle tunes, a troll king and a wedding party in concert-hall music. 1844–⁠1908

Nikolai Rimsky-⁠Korsakov

Russian composer known for fairy-tale operas and his detailed knowledge of the orchestra; he became a professor before he had learned counterpoint.
IV

Fin de siècle

1880–⁠1918
1854–⁠1928

Leoš Janáček

Composer who wrote down the voices of strangers in Brno and wrote operas known for their recognisable characters. 1857–⁠1934

Edward Elgar

Composer of the Enigma Variations, a set of variations drawn from his friendships, who also recorded his own music for the gramophone. 1858–⁠1924

Giacomo Puccini

Composer who held a theatre audience through a scene of a woman at a window, then brought back a tune it had heard before. 1860–⁠1911

Gustav Mahler

Conductor who composed during the summers and intended each symphony to contain a whole world, combining the sublime with the music of the street band. 1864–⁠1949

Richard Strauss

Composer, the son of a horn player, who wrote long farewell songs for soprano. 1865–⁠1931

Carl Nielsen

Danish composer, violinist and conductor, born on Funen and based in Copenhagen, who wrote six symphonies, the opera Maskarade, a wind quintet and a large body of songs and hymn tunes. 1865–⁠1957

Jean Sibelius

Composer who took the horn theme of his Fifth Symphony from sixteen swans he watched circling over Ainola, and who worked on the symphony for four years. 1867–⁠1944

Amy Beach

American composer and former prodigy from New Hampshire who taught herself orchestration by attending Boston Symphony concerts; known for a symphony built on Irish tunes, first given in Boston. 1872–⁠1915

Alexander Scriabin

Composer who wrote a part for coloured light that had to be omitted at the work’s first performance. 1873–⁠1943

Sergei Rachmaninoff

Russian composer and pianist who earned his living at the piano in exile and whose music uses the sound of the bells of his childhood.
V

The French Line

1870–⁠1937
1835–⁠1921

Camille Saint-⁠Saëns

Pianist from the age of ten and organist for twenty years, known for the Organ Symphony, a dance of skeletons featuring xylophone, and a zoo suite he forbade anyone to publish. 1845–⁠1924

Gabriel Fauré

Organist and teacher who wrote songs and chamber music in an unusual harmonic language. 1862–⁠1918

Claude Debussy

Composer who trusted his ear over the rules (“pleasure is the law”) and wrote new sounds for the piano, the flute and the orchestra. 1866–⁠1925

Erik Satie

Composer of the Gymnopédies, who asked pianists to prepare for 840 repetitions with “serious immobilities”. 1875–⁠1937

Maurice Ravel

Composer of a ballet built from two tunes and a snare drum, and of a suite of dances dedicated to his war dead. 1876–⁠1946

Manuel de Falla

Composer from Cádiz who studied in Paris with Dukas and Debussy and is known for bringing Andalusian song and the guitar into orchestral music. 1893–⁠1918

Lili Boulanger

Composer who won the Prix de Rome at nineteen and, before her death at twenty-four, wrote songs of lost love and settings of psalms. 1899–⁠1963

Francis Poulenc

Pianist and composer of sacred works, love songs and an opera in which the choir is silenced by the guillotine.
VI

Modernism

1908–⁠1945
1872–⁠1958

Ralph Vaughan Williams

Collector of an Essex folk song in 1903, editor of the English Hymnal, and the man who brought a Tudor psalm tune into the concert hall. 1874–⁠1954

Charles Ives

Composer and New York life-insurance salesman whose music sets a hymn heard across a river against a marching band. 1874–⁠1951

Arnold Schoenberg

Composer who abandoned tonality, continued to study Bach and Brahms, and wrote a minuet using all twelve notes. 1881–⁠1945

Béla Bartók

Composer who saw a future for concert music in village songs and used a phonograph to record how they were sung. 1882–⁠1971

Igor Stravinsky

Composer of dance music in which a single displaced accent could unsettle the dancers, and of a psalm setting that scarcely moves. 1883–⁠1945

Anton Webern

Composer known for passing melodies from horn to clarinet to cello, with each instrument holding only a few notes of a phrase. 1885–⁠1935

Alban Berg

Composer of Wozzeck, an opera about an exploited soldier, and of a twelve-note concerto that quotes a Bach chorale and is dedicated to the memory of Manon Gropius. 1887–⁠1953

Florence Price

American composer whose symphonic music used the juba dance and the brass hymn as central elements. 1887–⁠1959

Heitor Villa-⁠Lobos

Brazilian composer and conductor, born in Rio de Janeiro, who wrote the Chôros, the Bachianas Brasileiras and a body of music for guitar. 1891–⁠1953

Sergei Prokofiev

Twentieth-century composer known for Romeo and Juliet, with its writing for strings, and for his piano music. 1898–⁠1937

George Gershwin

Composer who brought Broadway rhythms into the concerto, known for a clarinet slide. 1900–⁠1990

Aaron Copland

Modernist composer from Brooklyn, known for his open fifths, his cowboy dances and a slow fanfare for brass. 1906–⁠1975

Dmitri Shostakovich

Composer of fifteen symphonies written under Soviet rule, who signed some of his most personal music with the four-note motif D–E♭–C–B♮.
VII

After 1945

1945–present
1908–⁠1992

Olivier Messiaen

Organist at La Trinité for sixty years, whose music drew on birdsong and was played by the pianist Yvonne Loriod. 1912–⁠1992

John Cage

Composer who put bolts between piano strings, composed with chance operations, and wrote a piece of four minutes and thirty-three seconds made up of the sounds around a concert. 1913–⁠1976

Benjamin Britten

Composer who wrote Grimes and Aschenbach for Peter Pears, a work with an ark full of animals for Suffolk children, and a requiem in which dead soldiers speak. 1918–⁠1990

Leonard Bernstein

American conductor, pianist and composer, music director of the New York Philharmonic from 1958 to 1969 and composer of West Side Story and the Chichester Psalms. 1922–⁠2001

Iannis Xenakis

Greek composer, born in Romania and naturalised French in 1965, who trained as an engineer, worked for Le Corbusier in Paris and composed with probability theory, set theory and computers. 1923–⁠2006

György Ligeti

Composer of orchestral works in which dozens of moving parts are contained within one sustained sound, and of piano études in which the two hands play at different speeds. 1925–⁠2016

Pierre Boulez

Composer of music built on explosive attacks and long echoes, who also founded the ensembles, concert series and laboratories needed to perform it. 1926–⁠1987

Morton Feldman

Composer of quiet, softly struck piano writing and of a string quartet that can last six hours. b. 1926

György Kurtág

Composer known for Játékok, which began as children’s piano games and grew to include pieces for friends, birthdays and farewells. 1931–⁠2025

Sofia Gubaidulina

Composer whose Offertorium, for Gidon Kremer’s violin, takes apart and rebuilds Bach’s royal theme. b. 1935

Arvo Pärt

Composer who rebuilt his music on a melody and three notes, and who wrote a lament for Britten based on a descending scale. b. 1936

Steve Reich

Composer who built music from two tape machines drifting out of time with each other, and later applied the same phasing process to pianists, drummers and singers. b. 1937

Philip Glass

American composer and keyboard player, born in Baltimore, who wrote operas, symphonies, string quartets and film scores and led his own ensemble in New York. b. 1947

John Adams

A New England clarinettist and composer, known for bringing Nixon to the stage and for a fast orchestral piece driven by a woodblock beat. 1952–⁠2023

Kaija Saariaho

Composer who built music from the timbre of a single cello note and wrote an opera about two people who had never met. b. 1971

Thomas Adès

Composer who wrote fishing reels into the pit orchestra of an opera.
