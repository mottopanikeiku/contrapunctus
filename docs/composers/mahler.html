Life

“The symphony must be like the world. It must embrace everything.”

To Jean Sibelius, Helsinki, 1907, as Sibelius remembered it

Mahler was born in a Bohemian village and grew up in the garrison town of Iglau (Jihlava), the second of fourteen children, eight of whom died young. Iglau gave him the sounds that never left his music: bugle calls and marches from the barracks, Czech and German folk song, the town band, the synagogue and the Catholic church. A story he told late in life, to Sigmund Freud, describes running from a violent quarrel between his parents into the street, where a barrel organ was grinding out Ach, du lieber Augustin. Tragedy and the banal, side by side: it is a fair description of half his slow movements.

The Freud walkIn August 1910, in a crisis over Alma’s affair with the architect Walter Gropius, Mahler met Freud in Leiden and walked with him for four hours. The barrel-organ story is Freud’s, recounted by his biographer Ernest Jones.

At fifteen he entered the Vienna Conservatory, studying piano and composition, and sat in on Bruckner’s harmony lectures at the University. His friends were Hugo Wolf and the short-lived Hans Rott, whose symphony anticipates Mahler’s so closely that Mahler later called Rott the founder of the new symphony. The cantata Das klagende Lied, written at eighteen and nineteen, failed to win the Beethoven Prize in 1881, and Mahler turned to the career that would pay for everything else: conducting.

The kapellmeisterSeventeen years in the pit

He worked up through the provincial houses — Bad Hall, Laibach, Olmütz, Kassel, Prague, Leipzig — and at twenty-eight ran the Royal Hungarian Opera in Budapest. In Hamburg (1891–97) he became the most exacting opera conductor in Germany. In 1897, having converted to Catholicism to make an appointment possible in an anti-Semitic city, he took over the Vienna Court Opera. For ten years he rebuilt it: darkened the auditorium, barred latecomers, abolished the claque, cut nothing from Wagner, and with the designer Alfred Roller staged Tristan and Fidelio with abstract light and colour that looked forward to the twentieth century.

That left only the summers for composing. From 1893 he worked in a series of one-room huts — on the Attersee, then on the Wörthersee, then near Toblach — writing in the mornings, walking in the afternoons, and orchestrating in Vienna in the winters. “I am a summer composer,” he said.

Progressive tonalitySeveral symphonies end in a different key from the one they began in — the Second (C minor → E♭), Fourth (G → E), Fifth (C♯ minor → D), Ninth (D → D♭). The key scheme becomes a plot.

The symphoniesTen worlds

The First (1888) begins with the strings holding a single A across seven octaves, marked wie ein Naturlaut — like a sound of nature — while woodwinds drop falling fourths into the silence and a clarinet cuckoo calls, a fourth instead of the usual third. Its slow movement is a funeral march built on the round Frère Jacques in the minor, introduced by a muted solo double bass in its uncomfortable high register. The Second, “Resurrection”, needed six years and an idea that came to him at the funeral of the conductor Hans von Bülow in 1894, when a choir sang Klopstock’s hymn Aufersteh’n from the organ loft. The Third, in six movements and about a hundred minutes, climbs from inanimate nature through flowers, animals and humanity (Nietzsche’s Midnight Song) to the angels and, finally, love.

The middle symphonies — Five, Six and Seven — drop the sung texts and the programmes. The Sixth, which acquired the nickname “Tragic” in Mahler’s lifetime, is a classical four-movement argument in A minor whose finale is punctuated by blows from a hammer. The Eighth (1906) is the reverse: a choral cathedral in two parts, setting the Latin hymn Veni, Creator Spiritus and the closing scene of Goethe’s Faust, premiered in Munich in 1910 by over a thousand performers — the biggest public triumph of his life.

1907Three blows

In one year he left the Vienna Opera after a press campaign, lost his elder daughter Maria to diphtheria, and was told he had a heart-valve defect. The works that followed — Das Lied von der Erde, the Ninth, the unfinished Tenth — are farewells, though not in the self-pitying sense legend has given them. They are also his most adventurous music: harmony pushed toward the edge of tonality, counterpoint that tangles voices into near-chaos, and endings that do not so much finish as dissolve. In New York he conducted at the Metropolitan Opera and then the Philharmonic. He fell ill there in February 1911 with bacterial endocarditis, sailed back to Europe, and died in Vienna in May. He was fifty.

My time will come.
Mahler, of his own music, in a letter to his wife Alma, 1902

It came about fifty years later. Through the 1960s — Bernstein’s complete cycle in New York, Deryck Cooke’s performing version of the Tenth, a centenary that turned into a revival — Mahler went from a conductor’s curiosity to the centre of the orchestral repertoire. The composers who learned most from him were the ones he defended in his lifetime: Schoenberg, Berg and Webern, and later Shostakovich and Britten.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Orchestration

Enormous forces used like chamber music. Doublings are avoided so that single lines stay audible; instruments are placed at the extremes of their range (the solo double bass in the First, the high clarinets in E♭ that shriek over marches). Mahler’s scores are covered in instructions: Schalltrichter auf (bells up), ohne Ausdruck (without expression), wie ein Naturlaut.

He brought outdoor sounds indoors: offstage brass bands, cowbells, a posthorn, sleigh bells, a birch switch (Rute) on the drum shell, mandolin and guitar, and the hammer of the Sixth.

Harmony

Wagnerian chromaticism laid over a basically diatonic, folk-song melodic language. His signature gestures are simple: the major triad that darkens to minor (the motto of the Sixth), the plagal and modal turns of chorale and folk tune, and in late works suspended, unresolved sonorities — Das Lied von der Erde ends on a chord with an added sixth that never settles.

The climax of the Tenth’s Adagio piles a nine-note dissonance over a sustained high trumpet A — the closest Mahler came to the threshold Schoenberg would cross.

Melody

Melody comes from song. Themes from the Wayfarer songs and the Wunderhorn settings walk straight into the symphonies; the first movement of the First is built on Ging heut’ morgen übers Feld. Long lines unfold by variation rather than repetition, constantly re-harmonised.

Rhythm & genre

Two rhythmic types run through everything: the march (military, funeral, grotesque) and the Ländler, the rustic Austrian dance in heavy three. Mahler sets them against the sublime without warning; Adorno called this his “breakthrough” technique — an outside force bursting into the form.

Form

Sonata form stretched, interrupted and narrated. Movements can last half an hour; symphonies have five or six movements, voices and choirs, and key schemes that travel. The model is the novel more than the classical symphony: digression, collage, parody, return.

Counterpoint

Increasingly polyphonic. In the Fifth’s scherzo and the Ninth’s Rondo-Burleske fugal lines collide at speed; in Das Lied a melody and its decorated variant sound together — heterophony — so that the texture shimmers rather than stacks.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue. “Hear it” plays the named recording or movement.

  1. 1884–85
    Lieder eines fahrenden Gesellen (Songs of a Wayfarer)Voice and orchestra; Mahler’s own texts
    Four songs about a jilted journeyman. The second and fourth supply themes for the First Symphony.
  2. 1888
    Symphony No. 1 in D majorOrchestra
    Begins in a haze of string harmonics and birdcalls; the third movement is the Frère Jacques funeral march. Premiered in Budapest in 1889 as a “symphonic poem in two parts”; the nickname “Titan” was dropped by Mahler himself.
  3. 1888–94
    Symphony No. 2 “Resurrection”Soprano, alto, chorus, orchestra, offstage brass and percussion
    From a C minor funeral rite to a choral E♭ major. In the finale, the offstage band and an onstage flute and piccolo trade calls — the “Great Summons” — before the choir enters almost inaudibly.
    IV. Urlicht — Sehr feierlich, aber schlicht — recording guide
  4. 1893–96
    Symphony No. 3 in D minorAlto, women’s and boys’ choirs, orchestra, offstage posthorn
    Six movements, nearly a hundred minutes. The first movement alone, with its brass and percussion march, outlasts many complete symphonies. The finale is a slow movement, a hymn for strings that builds to a blazing D major — an ending Mahler would return to, transformed, in the Ninth.
  5. 1899–1900
    Symphony No. 4 in G majorSoprano and a classically sized orchestra (no trombones)
    The lightest and most ironic. In the scherzo the leader plays a second violin tuned a tone high, for a scratchy “Death strikes up the dance”. The finale is a child’s vision of heaven from Des Knaben Wunderhorn.
    I. Bedächtig. Nicht eilen — recording guide
  6. 1901–04
    Kindertotenlieder (Songs on the Death of Children)Voice and orchestra; poems by Friedrich Rückert
    Five settings of poems Rückert wrote after losing two children. Completed three years before Mahler lost his own daughter.
  7. 1901–02
    Symphony No. 5Orchestra
    Opens with a solo trumpet’s funeral fanfare in C♯ minor and ends in D major with a contrapuntal rondo. The fourth movement, the Adagietto for strings and harp, is his best-known music.
    IV. Adagietto — Sehr langsam — recording guide
  8. 1903–04
    Symphony No. 6 in A minorLarge orchestra with hammer, cowbells, celesta
    The most classically built of the symphonies and the bleakest. A march-like motto rhythm and a major-to-minor chord recur like a verdict; the finale is a thirty-minute struggle in which the hammer falls.
  9. 1904–05
    Symphony No. 7Orchestra with tenor horn, mandolin, guitar, cowbells
    Two Nachtmusik movements frame a spectral scherzo marked Schattenhaft (shadowy). Schoenberg admired it above the others, and wrote to Mahler about it after hearing it in Vienna in 1909.
  10. 1906
    Symphony No. 8 in E♭ majorEight soloists, two mixed choirs, boys’ choir, very large orchestra, organ
    Part I sets the ninth-century hymn Veni, Creator Spiritus; Part II, the last scene of Goethe’s Faust, ending on the Chorus mysticus: “All that is transient is only a likeness.”
  11. 1908–09
    Das Lied von der Erde (The Song of the Earth)Tenor, alto (or baritone) and orchestra
    Six songs on Hans Bethge’s free German versions of Tang-dynasty poems. The last, Der Abschied (The Farewell), lasts half an hour and ends on the word ewig — forever — repeated into silence. Mahler added the final lines himself.
  12. 1909–10
    Symphony No. 9Orchestra
    Begins in D major with a halting, syncopated figure in the cellos and horn and ends in D♭ with an Adagio that thins to nothing, marked ersterbend — dying away. Berg called the first movement the most heavenly thing Mahler ever wrote.
  13. 1910
    Symphony No. 10 (unfinished)Orchestra
    Mahler left the Adagio almost fully scored and the rest in a continuous short score covered in anguished notes to Alma. Deryck Cooke’s performing version (final form 1976) is the most often played.

Sister arts

Objects and texts, with the nature of each musical connection made explicit.

Shared context

A children’s picture-sheet from Munich: hares carry banners, a fox weeps, deer and boars follow the coffin of the hunter who used to shoot them. Mahler cited this kind of image — an old Austrian nursery print — as the seed of the funeral march in the First Symphony. It explains the movement’s tone exactly: a procession that is both mournful and absurd, with a klezmer-like band (oboes, E♭ clarinets, bass drum with cymbal attached) breaking in on the solemnity.

Shared context

Klimt painted the frieze for the Vienna Secession’s 1902 exhibition in honour of Beethoven, as a visual reading of the Ninth Symphony’s finale; its last panel takes its title from Schiller’s Ode to Joy. At the opening, Mahler conducted his own arrangement of a passage from that finale for wind instruments. The armoured knight on the frieze’s long wall has often been said to carry Mahler’s features — an attribution that cannot be proved, but tells you how Vienna saw its opera director.

Shared context

The lake where Mahler built his first composing hut, at Steinbach, and wrote the Second and Third symphonies (1893–96). Klimt summered there from 1900 and painted it on square canvases, cropping the horizon almost out of the picture until the water becomes a field of colour. When the conductor Bruno Walter visited Steinbach and stared at the mountains, Mahler told him not to bother: “I have already composed all that.”

Documented useFriedrich Rückert

Ich bin der Welt abhanden gekommen

c. 1821; set by Mahler 1901
GermanIch bin der Welt abhanden gekommen, mit der ich sonst viele Zeit verdorben, sie hat so lange nichts von mir vernommen, sie mag wohl glauben, ich sei gestorben! Ich bin gestorben dem Weltgetümmel und ruh’ in einem stillen Gebiet! Ich leb’ allein in meinem Himmel, in meinem Lieben, in meinem Lied!
English · Contrapunctus translationI have been lost to the world on which I once wasted so much time; it has heard nothing of me for so long it may well think that I am dead. I am dead to the world’s commotion and rest in a quiet place. I live alone in my heaven, in my love, in my song.

The middle stanza is omitted here. Mahler set the poem in F major for voice and a reduced orchestra, with the cor anglais carrying a line that seems to grow out of the voice. He said of it: “It is my very self.” The Adagietto of the Fifth Symphony, composed the same summer, is in the same key and breathes the same air.

Documented useGustav Mahler, after Hans Bethge

Der Abschied — closing lines

1908
GermanDie liebe Erde allüberall blüht auf im Lenz und grünt aufs neu! Allüberall und ewig blauen licht die Fernen! Ewig … ewig …
English · Contrapunctus translationThe dear earth, everywhere, blossoms in spring and grows green again! Everywhere and forever the distances shine blue! Forever … forever …

Bethge’s Die chinesische Flöte (1907) gave Mahler loose German paraphrases of Tang poets — Li Bai, Wang Wei, Meng Haoran — already filtered through French and German translations. For the end of Der Abschied Mahler stopped adapting and wrote these lines himself.

Later connectionsArnold SchoenbergAlban BergAnton WebernDmitri ShostakovichBenjamin BrittenLeonard Bernstein

Mahler championed Schoenberg when Vienna jeered him, and Schoenberg’s 1912 Prague lecture on Mahler is one of the great composer-on-composer tributes. Berg’s Three Orchestral Pieces and Shostakovich’s Fourth Symphony are Mahlerian in scale and in their juxtaposition of the grotesque and the tragic; Britten heard the Fourth Symphony as a student and never forgot its scoring. Bernstein’s New York cycle of the 1960s did more than anything to put the symphonies into the repertoire.
