Clara Schumann

Born 13 September 1819, Leipzig
Died 20 May 1896, Frankfurt am Main

A concert pianist for more than sixty years, she composed songs, chamber music and piano works whose technical demands arise from the independence of their voices.

Life

Clara Wieck was born in Leipzig in 1819 to two professional musicians. Her mother, Mariane, was a pianist and singer; her father, Friedrich, taught piano and dealt in instruments. After their marriage broke down, Clara remained chiefly under her father’s control. He organised a systematic education in piano playing, theory, composition, languages and physical exercise, and managed her early career. Her public debut came in Leipzig in 1828. By her early teens she was touring, meeting established musicians and presenting her own compositions as part of the repertory expected of a virtuoso.

The young Clara’s programmes included variations, opera-based fantasies and other display pieces, but her published works already ask more than fluent passagework. The Romance variée, Op. 3, transforms a melody through changing texture; Robert Schumann used its theme in his own Op. 5 Impromptus. Her A minor Piano Concerto began with the finale, for which Robert helped with orchestration, and acquired its complete three-movement form by 1835. She played it at the Gewandhaus under Mendelssohn that November. The middle movement withdraws from the orchestra into a duet between piano and solo cello before the finale returns.

1838–40A performer’s independence

In Vienna in 1838 she was named an imperial and royal chamber virtuosa, an exceptional honour for a young foreign woman. Her relationship with Robert, who had studied with her father, created a conflict Friedrich Wieck could not settle by professional management alone. He opposed their marriage and attempted to restrict their communication. Clara’s 1839 Paris visit without him was part of a difficult process of personal and financial separation. After court proceedings, she and Robert married at Schönefeld near Leipzig on 12 September 1840, the day before her twenty-first birthday.

Two opus numbersThe 1841 collection Zwölf Gedichte aus Rückerts Liebesfrühling combines Robert’s Op. 37 with Clara’s Op. 12. Her three contributions are independent compositions.

Artistic work and household requirements remained in conflict throughout the marriage. Clara bore eight children between 1841 and 1854; seven survived infancy. She taught, practised, performed and managed finances while Robert needed quiet for composition. Their diaries and letters record encouragement, mutual study and frustration. Clara’s songs for the joint Rückert collection demonstrate her own treatment of text: recurring verbal patterns can retain a recognisable musical shape while changes of harmony give the final stanza a different force.

Touring remained an economic and artistic necessity. Robert accompanied her to Russia in 1844, but her international reputation was then more established than his. After their return and his worsening health, the family moved to Dresden. The G minor Piano Trio, Op. 17, followed in 1846. Its four movements distinguish sonata argument, scherzo, slow song and contrapuntal finale. The piano supplies thematic entries and a changing harmonic surface, while violin and cello can answer, combine or sustain lines beyond the keyboard’s physical reach.

1853The last concentrated period of composition

In Düsseldorf, where Robert became music director in 1850, Clara continued performing and assisting with musical work. The year 1853 produced her Variations on a Theme by Robert Schumann, Op. 20, and two sets of Three Romances, Opp. 21 and 22, for piano and for violin with piano respectively. The violin pieces were dedicated to Joseph Joachim, an important performing partner over subsequent decades. Brahms met the couple that autumn. His later Op. 9 Variations use the same Robert Schumann theme as Clara’s Op. 20, placing three composers’ treatments of one musical object in direct comparison.

A shared themeClara’s Op. 20 takes its theme from Robert’s Bunte Blätter, Op. 99 No. 4, in F♯ minor. The sequence of variations changes figuration and contrapuntal density while retaining a recognisable harmonic framework.

Robert’s crisis in February 1854 and admission to Endenich transformed the family’s circumstances. Doctors largely prevented Clara from visiting him; she continued to give concerts and bore their youngest child, Felix, that June. Brahms helped the household and remained a close correspondent and musical adviser. After Robert’s death in 1856 she sustained the family through teaching and touring. Her own composition became intermittent.

The concert repertoryPerformance as editorial work

Her later programmes helped establish a durable place for Bach, Beethoven, Chopin, Robert Schumann and Brahms in public piano concerts. Her long career gave playing from memory and these repertory choices institutional force. Partnerships with Joachim placed chamber music beside solo repertory. Her editions of Robert’s music preserved practical knowledge while also making decisions that later scholarship has had to examine. The withholding of his Violin Concerto, in which Joachim and Brahms were also involved, shows that preservation could include exclusion as well as advocacy.

In 1878 she joined the Hoch Conservatoire in Frankfurt, teaching advanced pianists while her daughters assisted with other pupils. Students carried her approach to phrasing, tone and repertory into several countries. Family responsibilities continued through the deaths and illnesses of adult children and the support of grandchildren. Her last public performance took place in 1891, and she retired from the conservatoire the following year. She died in Frankfurt in May 1896 and was buried beside Robert in Bonn. Surviving letters, programmes, annotated scores and pupils’ recollections document the choices of a working musician across six decades; no sound recording preserves her playing.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Voicing

Keyboard figuration can carry an independent middle part. The Variations, Op. 20, require the pianist to distinguish the theme’s structural line from new moving voices. A uniform legato and dynamic level conceal the variation’s argument.

Vocal phrase

The romances and songs share a concern with sustained melody shaped by its harmony. Appoggiaturas and delayed resolutions determine where a line leans and where it can release; a singer’s breath or a pianist’s timing must make that difference audible.

Concerto intimacy

The A minor Concerto’s Romance withdraws from orchestral mass and introduces a solo cello beside the pianist. Its chamber texture marks a formal change between the outer movements.

Variation & dialogue

Op. 20 treats an existing theme as material for harmonic and contrapuntal work. The Trio’s finale likewise gives imitation a structural role. These are procedures she studied and practised with Robert, but their particular distributions of voices belong to her scores.

Rhythmic character

The C minor Scherzo, Op. 14, demands strongly articulated motion and rapid changes of register. Its attack differs from the broad line of a romance, revealing the range of a catalogue that also includes dances and public concert pieces.

Liebst du um Schönheit

From Liebesfrühling; Clara’s setting 1841
GermanLiebst du um Schönheit, O nicht mich liebe! Liebe die Sonne, Sie trägt ein gold’nes Haar! Liebst du um Jugend, O nicht mich liebe! Liebe den Frühling, Der jung ist jedes Jahr! Liebst du um Schätze, O nicht mich liebe! Liebe die Meerfrau, Sie hat viel Perlen klar! Liebst du um Liebe, O ja, mich liebe! Liebe mich immer, Dich lieb’ ich immerdar!
English · Contrapunctus translationIf beauty is why you love, do not choose me! Give your love to the sun: she wears golden hair. If youth is why you love, do not choose me! Give your love to spring: it is young again each year. If riches are why you love, do not choose me! Give your love to the mermaid: she owns many bright pearls. If love is why you love, then yes, choose me! Keep loving me always; my love for you will not end.

Clara’s Op. 12 No. 2 belongs to the 1841 collection she published with Robert. Three stanzas reject an external reason for love; the fourth changes ‘nicht’ to ‘ja’. That verbal alteration makes a small musical change carry a large formal consequence. Mahler set the same poem much later, in 1902: the shared text invites comparison of the two settings. Later connectionsJohannes BrahmsFanny DaviesAdelina de Lara

Her effect on Brahms embraces the variation theme they shared, performances, criticism and a lifetime of correspondence. Her pupils transmitted a practical tradition of voicing and repertory. Robert’s music also travelled through that teaching.
