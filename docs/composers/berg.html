Alban Berg

Born 9 February 1885, Vienna
Died 24 December 1935, Vienna

A composer of operas and coded chamber music who made twelve-tone rows accommodate triads, folk song and a Bach chorale.

Life

Alban Berg grew up in Vienna in a family supported by commerce. As a teenager he wrote songs, read literature and learned the piano without a conservatory training. His father died in 1900. In 1904 his brother Charley showed some of the songs to Arnold Schoenberg, who accepted him as a pupil. Lyrical phrases came readily; making them support a sustained instrumental structure was harder. Schoenberg’s lessons in harmony, counterpoint and form supplied the technical discipline behind the works that followed.

The Piano Sonata, Op. 1, completed in 1908, consists of a single movement. Its B minor is repeatedly obscured by whole-tone formations, augmented chords and sequences of fourths, but the tonic remains available at the close. Small melodic cells generate much of the argument. In the String Quartet, Op. 3, written in 1910, this concentration survives without an equivalent tonal frame. Schoenberg’s instruction in developing variation gave Berg’s long phrases more densely connected internal parts.

ViennaWords in the orchestra

Berg married Helene Nahowski in 1911. His artistic circle included the architect Adolf Loos, the writer Peter Altenberg and admirers of Karl Kraus’s attacks on journalistic language and public hypocrisy. Altenberg’s brief postcard texts became five orchestral songs in 1911–12. Their short verbal statements are surrounded by an orchestra far larger than their scale seems to require. Two songs were performed at Schoenberg’s notorious Vienna concert of 31 March 1913, which was interrupted by shouting and fighting. Public hostility was accompanied by Schoenberg’s own reservations about his pupil’s handling of musical form.

Forms under the dramaWozzeck has three acts of five scenes. The second act functions as a five-movement symphony; the third builds scenes around such restricted materials as one pitch or one rhythm.

In May 1914 Berg saw Georg Büchner’s unfinished play Woyzeck in Vienna and began planning an opera. The title Wozzeck came from the edition then in circulation. Military service during the First World War interrupted composition and sharpened his familiarity with barracks discipline. Büchner’s soldier is exploited by a captain and a doctor, humiliated by poverty and driven towards murder. The captain’s vocal contortions, the doctor’s obsessional music and Wozzeck’s compressed utterances place social relations inside the musical texture.

The opera was completed in 1922. Its passacaglia, inventions, suite movements and fugue work beneath the surface, stabilising a sequence of abrupt scenes and orchestral transitions. At Marie’s murder, the pitch B becomes an obsessive reference. After Wozzeck’s drowning, an extended D minor orchestral interlude brings the accumulated suffering into a recognisably tonal frame. The last scene returns to the children outside: Marie’s son continues his game while the other children run to see her body. The orchestra leaves this ending unconsoled.

Erich Kleiber conducted the premiere at the Berlin State Opera on 14 December 1925 after extensive preparation. The success made Berg the member of Schoenberg’s circle with a major foothold in the international theatre. Yet his loyalty to his teacher continued. The Chamber Concerto, completed in 1925 for Schoenberg’s fiftieth birthday, writes the three composers’ names into its introductory pitch material. Piano and violin take turns as principal soloist before the finale combines them, making the relationship between individuals audible through instrumentation as well as through private symbolism.

Private programmesThe quartet behind the dedication

During a visit to Prague in 1925, Berg became attached to Hanna Fuchs-Robettin. Both were married. The Lyric Suite, composed in 1925–26, was publicly dedicated to Alexander Zemlinsky but carried an additional programme for Hanna. Berg marked an annotated score with references to their relationship, their initials and numbers. The six movements alternate increasingly intense slow movements with fast ones. Twelve-tone construction governs parts of the work. Quotations from Zemlinsky and Wagner bring already-existing music into the quartet’s argument.

The hidden wordsGeorge Perle’s discovery of the annotated Lyric Suite score in 1977 established a concealed setting of Baudelaire, in Stefan George’s German, in the finale. Its vocal line can be restored, but the published quartet remains a complete instrumental work.

Berg began Lulu in 1929, combining Frank Wedekind’s Erdgeist and Die Büchse der Pandora. The opera follows its central character through relationships organised by money, sexual possession and social rank. A film interlude at the centre of the second act accompanies a musical palindrome: material runs towards a midpoint and then returns in reverse. Related rows link the characters. Jazz-band instruments and passages of theatrical dance music operate inside this tightly connected pitch structure.

1935A chorale inside a row

The American violinist Louis Krasner commissioned a concerto in 1935. The death that April of eighteen-year-old Manon Gropius, daughter of Alma Mahler and Walter Gropius, gave it its dedication, “To the memory of an angel”. Berg set Lulu aside and finished the concerto that summer. Its row begins with overlapping minor and major triads whose roots follow the violin’s open strings, G–D–A–E. Its final four notes ascend by whole tones, allowing the chorale Es ist genug, in Bach’s harmonisation, to enter as a structural consequence of the pitch material.

Berg died of septicaemia on Christmas Eve 1935, before hearing the concerto performed. Krasner gave its premiere in Barcelona in April 1936, conducted by Hermann Scherchen. Lulu existed in a continuous short score, but much of the third act lacked orchestration. A two-act version was first staged in Zurich in 1937; Friedrich Cerha’s completion of the orchestration reached the Paris Opéra in 1979 under Pierre Boulez. The difference between those versions is dramatic as well as musical: the completed final act makes the earlier relationships return in altered social circumstances and with fatal consequences.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Harmony

Berg chooses rows that admit familiar chords. The Violin Concerto’s G minor, D major, A minor and E major triads overlap within one ordered series. In Wozzeck, composed without twelve-tone technique, tonal islands have dramatic force within music free to move outside a key. D minor becomes a particular utterance within that shifting harmonic language.

Melody & voice

Wide vocal intervals coexist with repeated-note speech and sustained cantabile. A character’s social position can determine the kind of line available to them: the captain in Wozzeck ornaments his commands, while the soldier answers in shorter units. Berg preserves the expressive weight of an appoggiatura — a leaning dissonance — even when there is no conventional tonic to resolve into.

Form

A scene can be a passacaglia or an invention while remaining comprehensible as theatre. In Lulu, the central film music’s palindrome supports a larger network of returns and reversed fortunes. These structures make repetition and recognition possible without requiring the listener to identify a row or count a bar.

Orchestration

The Three Orchestral Pieces inherit Mahler’s crowded marches and unstable layers. Berg can then remove most of that apparatus for an exposed solo or chamber group. The saxophone in the Violin Concerto and Lulu is a distinctive voice within the texture, while the concerto’s clarinet choir gives the Bach harmonisation the separate sonority of a chorale.

Quotation & cipher

The Lyric Suite embeds A–B♭ for Alban Berg and B♮–F for Hanna Fuchs, using German B and H. Its quotations carry private associations, but also have audible intervallic and formal functions. The concerto’s Carinthian folk song and Bach chorale bring different historical kinds of melody into contact with its row.

  5. 1913–15

Documented encounter

Schoenberg painted his pupil in the year of the String Quartet, Op. 3. The tall, narrow canvas belongs to the teacher’s concentrated period of portrait painting; it depicts a working relationship that began with harmony lessons in 1904 and continued after Berg’s operatic success.

Documented useCharles Baudelaire

De profundis clamavi — opening quatrain

1857; text from the 1861 edition
FrenchJ’implore ta pitié, Toi, l’unique que j’aime, Du fond du gouffre obscur où mon cœur est tombé. C’est un univers morne à l’horizon plombé, Où nagent dans la nuit l’horreur et le blasphème ;
English · Contrapunctus translationI beg your pity, you, the only one I love, From the depth of the dark abyss into which my heart has fallen. It is a bleak universe beneath a leaden horizon, Where horror and blasphemy swim through the night;

The finale of the Lyric Suite conceals a setting of this poem in Stefan George’s German translation. Berg’s annotated copy for Hanna Fuchs-Robettin supplied the evidence discovered by George Perle in 1977. The French original appears here. Its address to one absent beloved explains the vocal conception behind the instrumental lament.

Later connectionsBenjamin BrittenGyörgy KurtágPierre BoulezHans Werner Henze

Berg’s operas offered later composers a way of retaining character, lyricism and theatrical timing within post-tonal music. Britten admired Wozzeck; Boulez conducted both operas and the premiere of Cerha’s completed Lulu. The coded intimacy of the chamber music also matters to Kurtág, whose dedications and short vocal forms make personal reference part of musical construction.
