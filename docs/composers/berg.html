Life

In 1904 Berg’s brother Charly secretly took some of Alban’s songs to Arnold Schoenberg. Their sister Smaragda had spotted an advertisement for lessons in harmony and counterpoint. Alban was nineteen, a bookseller’s son from Vienna, writing songs and working in the civil service. His father had died four years earlier; there was little money for tuition. Schoenberg accepted him all the same, though he could not pay the fee. Remembering their first meeting in a recording made in 1949, he described “a very tall youngster and extremely timid”, whose songs lay somewhere between Hugo Wolf and Brahms.

An inheritance in 1906 allowed Berg to leave the office. When his mother proposed the conservatoire, Schoenberg heard, he wept until she agreed he could stay with his teacher. Lessons lasted until 1910. The Piano Sonata, Op. 1, completed in 1908, shows Schoenberg’s developing variation at work: small phrases change and grow, through whole-tone passages to a final B minor. The String Quartet, Op. 3, shares its themes between two movements; its opening idea returns near the close.

1911–14Postcards and a birthday present

Berg married Helene Nahowski in May 1911. He knew Adolf Loos and Peter Altenberg, and read Karl Kraus’s attacks on Vienna’s newspapers and respectable morals. Altenberg’s postcard texts became five songs for a huge orchestra. Two were played under Schoenberg in Vienna on 31 March 1913, at a concert that broke up in uproar. Visiting Schoenberg in Berlin that June, Berg received a bruising criticism of the songs and his Four Pieces for Clarinet and Piano.

His teacher wanted something larger. Berg tried a symphony, then turned to the Three Orchestral Pieces: quiet percussion opens the Präludium, while the march’s climaxes recall Mahler’s Sixth. He sent these two pieces to Schoenberg for his fortieth birthday in September 1914, anxiously asking him to accept the good intention if the music fell short. Reigen, the waltz, still needed finishing.

1914–25The Captain’s barber

On 14 May 1914 Berg saw Büchner’s Woyzeck in Vienna and decided to make an opera of it. The edition he used called the soldier Wozzeck. A year later Berg was in uniform. Ill health brought him from army training to the War Ministry, where service continued until 1918. “There is a part of me in this figure too,” he wrote to Helene on 7 August that year: he too was “dependent on people I detest”. He worked on the opera during leave.

Fifteen scenesBerg arranged Wozzeck in three acts of five scenes. Act II follows the outline of a five-movement symphony, including a sonata movement and a scherzo.

Wozzeck earns extra money by shaving his Captain and submitting to the Doctor’s experiments. The Captain lectures him about morality while the razor is in his hand; the Doctor is delighted by symptoms of mental collapse. Berg gives this examination a passacaglia, variations on a recurring twelve-note theme. Elsewhere Marie sings their child a lullaby. Later, after her murder, an out-of-tune tavern piano plays a polka while Wozzeck tries to dance with blood on him.

The full score was finished in 1922. At Marie’s murder, B♮ haunts the scene, then swells through the orchestra after Wozzeck has cut her throat. When he drowns in the pond where he has tried to hide the knife, a great interlude gathers his music into D minor. Then children are playing outside Marie’s door. They hear that her body has been found and run off to look; her son carries on with his game before following them. The quavers keep moving.

Erich Kleiber conducted the première at the Berlin State Opera on 14 December 1925. Further productions spread through Europe, bringing Berg the royalties that would support Lulu. He had also completed the Chamber Concerto, a belated fiftieth-birthday gift for Schoenberg. Its opening spells out musical letters from the names of Schoenberg, Webern and Berg. The piano leads the first movement, the violin the second; in the finale their earlier music sounds together against thirteen wind instruments.

1925–26A score for Hanna

In Prague in 1925, Berg stayed with Herbert and Hanna Fuchs-Robettin while Zemlinsky conducted excerpts from Wozzeck. He fell in love with Hanna. Both were married, and the Lyric Suite he wrote afterwards kept two dedications: Zemlinsky’s on the published score, Hanna’s in a copy covered with private annotations. A–B♭ spells Alban Berg in German note names; B♮–F supplies Hanna Fuchs. In the third movement these four notes whisper through the strings. Across the six movements, the fast music grows faster and the slow music slower.

The annotated copyIn 1977 George Perle saw the score Berg had marked for Hanna, preserved by her daughter Dorothea. The lovers’ numbers, 23 and 10, enter its bar counts and metronome markings.

The final movement carries the words of Baudelaire’s De profundis clamavi, in Stefan George’s German translation, concealed beneath the instrumental lines. The speaker begs the beloved for pity from the bottom of a dark abyss. Berg also borrowed from Zemlinsky’s Lyric Symphony and Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde. In the second movement, the Tristan chord descends over plucked cello Cs: “Do-Do”, the nickname of Hanna’s daughter, written into the quartet.

1929–35Lulu’s portrait

For his second opera Berg joined Wedekind’s Erdgeist to Die Büchse der Pandora, which he had seen in Karl Kraus’s production in 1905. Lulu’s first scene takes place in a painter’s studio; her portrait follows her through successive households to a London garret. Men marry and bargain over her. At the centre of Act II a silent film shows her imprisonment and escape, accompanied by music that reverses around its midpoint. The singers who played her husbands return as her clients in the final scene. Dr Schön’s baritone becomes Jack the Ripper’s.

In November 1934 Kleiber conducted the Lulu symphonic excerpts in Berlin, amid growing Nazi hostility to Berg’s music. German performances were disappearing, taking his income with them. The American violinist Louis Krasner’s commission arrived early in 1935, while Berg was still orchestrating the opera. Then, on 22 April, Manon Gropius died of polio, aged eighteen. She was Alma Mahler’s daughter by the architect Walter Gropius. Berg put Lulu aside and wrote on the concerto’s title page: “To the memory of an angel”.

1935Four open strings

That June Krasner visited Berg at his house by the Wörthersee and improvised for hours, letting the composer learn his playing. The concerto begins with the violin touching its open strings, G–D–A–E. Those notes support the overlapping triads in its twelve-note row. On 16 July Berg told Krasner he had finished the composition the previous day. “The work was giving me more and more pleasure,” he wrote.

The choraleThe row’s last four notes rise by whole tones. They fit the opening of Es ist genug, the hymn Berg found in Bach’s Cantata BWV 60 after work on the concerto was already well advanced.

The first movement includes a Carinthian folk song, passed between horn, solo violin and trumpets. The second begins violently, drives through a cadenza and breaks into the quiet of the chorale. During its variations, orchestral violins join the soloist one by one; Berg asks the soloist to lead them visibly as well as audibly. He finished the orchestration in August and died of septicaemia on Christmas Eve. Krasner gave the première in Barcelona in April 1936, with Hermann Scherchen conducting.

Lulu lay complete in short score, with much of Act III still awaiting orchestration. Zurich staged the two finished acts in 1937. Friedrich Cerha’s completion finally reached the Paris Opéra in February 1979, with Pierre Boulez conducting and Teresa Stratas as Lulu. The portrait could now travel all the way to the London room, where Countess Geschwitz sings the last words after Jack has stabbed her.

The Sound

♮Harmony

The Violin Concerto’s twelve-note row contains four familiar chords: G minor, D major, A minor and E major. Each starts on one of the violin’s open strings, with neighbouring triads sharing a note. A player tuning the instrument already touches the foundations of the piece. At the other end of the row, four notes climbing through three whole tones prepare the opening of Bach’s chorale.

𝅘𝅥Singing and speech

The Captain in Wozzeck can turn a lecture on morality into falsetto; Wozzeck answers his orders on a repeated note. Marie’s lullaby brings open fourths and fifths into their cramped room. Berg moves between measured speech and full singing as the scene demands, letting a single character change vocal manner within a conversation. The Captain’s voice has time to wander; the soldier has work to finish.

𝄋Forms on stage

The Doctor examines Wozzeck over a passacaglia: a recurring theme subjected to twenty-one variations. In Lulu, the film of imprisonment and escape has a different device, a palindrome that runs backwards after its central piano arpeggio. The cell door closes and opens again. Lulu and the people around her also have related twelve-note rows, carrying their connections into the pitches they sing.

𝄞The orchestra

The Three Orchestral Pieces begin with percussion scarcely above silence, before violins and bassoon bring a melody into focus. By the final march the ensemble needs six horns and a great hammer. Berg can reduce his forces just as decisively: the Violin Concerto opens with harp and clarinets around the soloist’s bare strings, and its last pages recall that tentative beginning.

𝄪Borrowed music and initials

In the Lyric Suite, A–B♭ and B♮–F join Alban Berg’s initials to Hanna Fuchs’s; German notation calls B♭ “B” and B♮ “H”. These notes are especially exposed in the third movement’s quiet opening. Other people enter through borrowed tunes: Zemlinsky’s Lyric Symphony appears in the quartet, while a Carinthian folk song passes from horn to violin in the concerto.

𝄾Dance music

In Lulu’s dressing-room scene, an offstage band plays ragtime with banjo and sousaphone; an English waltz follows. The dance band gradually merges with the pit orchestra. Wozzeck puts an out-of-tune piano on stage for its tavern music, inside a largely atonal score. In the concert hall, Berg adds an alto saxophone to the Violin Concerto, sharing a player with the third clarinet.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1907–08
    Piano Sonata in B minorOp. 1Piano
    A single sonata movement whose opening phrases supply ideas for both hands throughout. Whole-tone passages disturb B minor, which returns in the closing bars.
  2. 1910
    String QuartetOp. 3Two violins, viola and cello
    Berg’s last work as Schoenberg’s pupil begins with a six-note figure in second violin and viola. The opening theme returns near the end of the second movement.
  3. 1911–12
    Five Orchestral Songs on Picture-Postcard Texts by Peter AltenbergOp. 4Voice and large orchestra
    Altenberg’s tiny postcard texts receive a full orchestral setting. Schoenberg conducted two of the songs at the Vienna concert that collapsed into uproar on 31 March 1913.
  4. 1913
    Four Pieces for Clarinet and PianoOp. 5Clarinet and piano
    Four miniatures dedicated to Schoenberg and his Society for Private Musical Performances. The society gave their première in Vienna in October 1919.
  5. 1913–15
    Three Orchestral PiecesOp. 6Large orchestra
    Quiet percussion opens the Präludium; Reigen turns the waltz unsteady, and Marsch brings the climaxes of an enormous orchestra. Berg sent the first completed pieces to Schoenberg for his fortieth birthday.
  6. 1914–22
    WozzeckOp. 7Opera in three acts; voices, orchestra and stage ensembles
    Büchner’s soldier shaves the Captain, sells his body to the Doctor’s experiments and murders Marie. A D minor interlude mourns him before the children resume their games.
  7. 1923–25
    Chamber ConcertoPiano, violin and thirteen wind instruments
    The piano’s variations and the violin’s Adagio are superimposed in the finale. Musical spellings of Schoenberg, Webern and Berg introduce this birthday present to the teacher.
  8. 1925–26
    Lyric SuiteString quartet
    Six movements whose fast tempos accelerate across the set while the slow ones broaden. Berg’s copy for Hanna Fuchs-Robettin reveals their initials and the Baudelaire poem hidden in the finale.
  9. 1929–35
    LuluOpera in three acts; third-act orchestration completed by Friedrich Cerha
    Lulu’s portrait travels from the painter’s studio to her last room in London. A palindromic film interlude divides her rise from her fall; the husbands’ singers return as her clients.
  10. 1935
    Violin ConcertoViolin and orchestra
    Written for Louis Krasner in memory of Manon Gropius, with two movements divided into four sections. A Carinthian folk song and Bach’s harmonisation of Es ist genug emerge from the twelve-note writing.

Sister Arts

Schoenberg painted Berg in 1910, the year his pupil finished the String Quartet, Op. 3, and his formal lessons. The canvas is almost 1.8 metres high, giving the tall, leaning figure room to stand. In a recorded reminiscence in 1949, Schoenberg still recalled the nineteen-year-old who had first come to him as “extremely timid”.

Charles Baudelaire

De profundis clamavi — opening quatrain

1857; text from the 1861 edition
FrenchJ’implore ta pitié, Toi, l’unique que j’aime, Du fond du gouffre obscur où mon cœur est tombé. C’est un univers morne à l’horizon plombé, Où nagent dans la nuit l’horreur et le blasphème ;
Our translationI implore your pity, You, the only one I love, From the depths of the dark gulf where my heart has fallen. It is a dismal universe with a leaden horizon, Where horror and blasphemy swim in the night;

Baudelaire’s speaker calls to the beloved from a dark gulf. Berg laid Stefan George’s German translation beneath the finale of the Lyric Suite, and wrote the words into the score he gave Hanna Fuchs-Robettin. The quartet’s four players carry this private lament; performances with a singer make the concealed words audible.

Lineage

Drew fromArnold SchoenbergGustav MahlerRichard WagnerAlexander ZemlinskyGeorg Büchner
Berg
Passed toBenjamin BrittenGyörgy KurtágPierre BoulezHans Werner Henze

Schoenberg taught Berg to build a movement from small musical ideas; Mahler’s waltzes and march climaxes return in the Three Orchestral Pieces. Britten wanted to study with Berg, but his parents refused permission. Boulez served the music from the conductor’s desk, bringing Cerha’s completed Lulu to the Paris stage in 1979 with Teresa Stratas in the title role.
