Life

Debussy was born above his parents’ china shop in Saint-Germain-en-Laye, outside Paris. Neither parent was a professional musician. His father’s involvement in the Paris Commune brought imprisonment in 1871; through a connection made during that imprisonment, the boy came to the piano teacher Antoinette Mauté, Verlaine’s mother-in-law. He entered the Paris Conservatoire in 1872, aged ten. Antoine Marmontel trained him as a pianist; Ernest Guiraud later taught him composition. His resistance to harmonic rules developed inside this education: he could play, analyse and compete successfully in the system he came to distrust.

From 1880 he spent summers working for Nadezhda von Meck, Tchaikovsky’s patron, playing chamber music and teaching her children. Travel with her household took him to Italy and Russia. In Paris he accompanied the soprano Marie Vasnier, with whom he had an affair, and wrote songs for her flexible high voice. In their accompaniments, the piano’s register, resonance and passing harmonic changes can supply a second interpretation of the words. The early songs also show how thoroughly he knew the French vocal tradition he would alter.

1884–89Rome, Bayreuth and the exhibition

He won the Prix de Rome in 1884 with the cantata L’Enfant prodigue. Residence at the Villa Medici followed, but he disliked the obligation to send official compositions back to Paris and returned before completing the prescribed stay. The choral La Damoiselle élue, on a French translation of Dante Gabriel Rossetti, was one of the works connected with these requirements. Its female voices, divided orchestral textures and suspended harmonies indicate a different ambition from the prize cantata’s operatic rhetoric. An institutional exercise had become a place to test the weight of a sound held still.

Debussy attended Bayreuth in 1888 and 1889. Tristan and Parsifal offered chromatic harmony, orchestral continuity and a theatre in which a musical idea could remember a character’s unspoken thoughts. His Cinq Poèmes de Charles Baudelaire retain the pressure of that encounter. He later opposed Wagnerism while retaining lessons from this apprenticeship. He could redirect the listener’s expectation by changing the collection of available notes, the rhythmic layer or the instrumental colour on which attention depended.

Gamelan and the pianoA gamelan is an ensemble with its own tunings, instruments and performance practices. Its tunings differ from those of Debussy’s equal-tempered piano. His response involved layered patterns, resonance and pacing as well as pentatonic notes.

At the 1889 Paris Exposition he heard a Javanese gamelan and Vietnamese theatre. These performances took place within a colonial exhibition. For Debussy they demonstrated ways of organising musical time beyond European symphonic development. The piano piece Pagodes, written fourteen years later, superimposes repeated figures in different registers and makes low notes function like structural gongs. Its pentatonic material, the independent speeds of the layers and the resonance left between attacks all carry the connection.

Poets and playersA flute before a key

Back in Paris he entered circles around Stéphane Mallarmé and Pierre Louÿs and studied Russian scores, particularly Mussorgsky’s Boris Godunov. Mallarmé’s poem L’Après-midi d’un faune gave him a character trying to distinguish remembered desire from invention. The resulting orchestral prelude, first performed in December 1894 under Gustave Doret, begins with an unaccompanied flute. Its opening C♯ descends towards G, spanning a tritone before returning; without a bass, no single chord tells us how to hear the line. When harmony arrives it supplies one interpretation among several.

The String Quartet of 1893 builds its four movements around a returning idea, altered in rhythm, harmony and instrumentation. The second movement’s pizzicato makes the strings behave as plucked percussion; the slow movement depends on sustained, divided attention between the instruments. This is cyclic thinking, familiar in French music from César Franck, with timbral changes built into the thematic transformations. A listener can recognise an idea while having to reconsider what kind of musical event it has become.

In the orchestral Nocturnes, completed in 1899, three movements examine different kinds of motion. Nuages puts a recurrent cor anglais figure against slowly shifting sonorities; Fêtes lets a distant procession approach and pass through an already active scene. Sirènes adds a wordless women’s chorus to the orchestra, retaining breath and vowel colour. The title refers to pictorial effects, with Whistler’s painted nocturnes as a point of reference.

The operaFrench speech in an orchestra

Debussy began Pelléas et Mélisande in 1893 after Maeterlinck’s play and continued revising it up to its 1902 premiere at the Opéra-Comique. André Messager conducted; Mary Garden sang Mélisande. The five acts preserve the play’s prose and its evasions. Characters ask practical questions and receive incomplete answers; the important event may have happened before the scene began. Debussy follows speech with a largely syllabic vocal line. Repeated notes can carry a sentence while the orchestra alters the emotional information around it.

Conversation and interludeThe orchestral interludes can expand violently. They connect scenes of vocal conversation, where syllables must remain audible.

The work’s recurring motifs change with their dramatic surroundings. Golaud’s interrogation, Mélisande’s hair falling from the tower and the cavern scene depend on differences of register, pace and orchestral density. The opera made Debussy publicly important. He worked intermittently on Poe’s The Fall of the House of Usher and other theatre projects, leaving them in fragments.

1903–13Sea, keyboard and movement

Estampes in 1903 brought the piano pieces Pagodes, La soirée dans Grenade and Jardins sous la pluie together as three differently constructed places. The Spanish piece uses a habanera rhythm; the last transforms French nursery songs through rapid figuration. The two books of Images extend this work with resonance and simultaneous layers. In Reflets dans l’eau, the figuration changes register and density, and a small initial idea survives the transformations. Hommage à Rameau answers an older French keyboard tradition with a slow dance.

During the composition of La Mer, Debussy’s domestic life changed publicly. He had married Lilly Texier in 1899; in 1904 he left her for Emma Bardac. Lilly survived a suicide attempt. Debussy and Emma’s daughter Claude-Emma, called Chouchou, was born in 1905, and the couple married in 1908. Friends took sides, and the scandal formed part of the hostile atmosphere around the first performance of La Mer in October 1905. The score’s motives and rhythmic processes cross the boundaries of its three movements.

The subtitle calls these movements ‘three symphonic sketches’. The first, De l’aube à midi sur la mer, accumulates motion and reaches a broad brass conclusion; Jeux de vagues keeps separating and recombining short figures; the final dialogue of wind and sea recalls material heard earlier. Debussy chose a version of Hokusai’s wave for the published score’s cover. Recurrent intervals and changes of rhythmic scale hold the orchestral sequence together.

The twenty-four piano Préludes, published in two books in 1910 and 1913, place their titles at the end of each piece. The listener meets a musical construction before receiving its verbal suggestion. Voiles contrasts a predominantly whole-tone field with a brief pentatonic passage; La cathédrale engloutie expands parallel chords into the register and resonance of bells. Other pieces admit popular entertainers, cakewalk rhythms and deliberate comedy. In Minstrels, pauses and displaced accents are as carefully composed as the harmonies of the more frequently anthologised slow preludes.

An ambiguous titleVoiles can mean sails or veils. Debussy left the word’s ambiguity intact. The distribution of intervals changes when the pentatonic passage interrupts the whole-tone material.

Jeux, first danced by the Ballets Russes in May 1913, takes a lost tennis ball and three players as the premise for an orchestral work of repeated interruptions. Small figures are constantly rephrased, while tempo and instrumental combinations change before a conventional theme has time to settle. Nijinsky choreographed it; two weeks later the premiere of Stravinsky’s The Rite of Spring displaced it in public attention. Later composers, especially Boulez, found in Jeux a model of continuity made from local changes.

The war yearsThree sonatas out of six

The First World War and worsening cancer restricted his activity, but 1915 produced the twelve piano Études, En blanc et noir for two pianos, the Cello Sonata and the Sonata for flute, viola and harp. The studies begin from specific pianistic problems — thirds, octaves, repeated notes, opposing sonorities — and turn those restrictions into form. He planned six sonatas and identified himself on their title pages as a French musician. References to Couperin and Rameau coexist with abrupt changes of texture and an economy unlike the expansive nineteenth-century sonata.

Only three sonatas were completed. Debussy played the piano part in the Violin Sonata’s premiere with Gaston Poulet in May 1917; its finale repeatedly interrupts and restarts its motion. He died in Paris on 25 March 1918 while the city was under German bombardment. His last completed chamber works make articulation, register, silence and the spacing of intervals carry tasks once assigned principally to thematic development.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Pitch collections

Whole-tone, pentatonic, modal and chromatic material coexist. A whole-tone scale has no semitones and no perfect fifths; it weakens the usual leading-note and dominant relationships. Debussy can make the change from one collection to another into an event, as in Voiles.

Chords & voice-leading

Sevenths and ninths can linger as sonorities in their own right. Parallel movement can preserve a chord’s spacing while moving the complete sonority, a procedure often called planing. Independent bass notes, held pitches and contrary motion continually change the relation between chord and line.

Orchestration

Register and instrumental identity separate layers that would blur in a piano reduction. The opening flute of Faune, the cor anglais in Nuages and the wordless chorus in Sirènes are structural decisions. In La Mer, strings divide into numerous parts while winds or brass supply a differently paced foreground.

Time & form

Ostinatos can hold one rate of motion while another layer accelerates or lengthens its phrases. In Jeux, small repetitions and changes of tempo produce continuity. The quartet and La Mer transform their returning material, making memory central to the form.

Piano

The sustaining pedal preserves a low harmonic foundation beneath figures played elsewhere on the keyboard. Different registers can suggest different instruments or speeds. In Pagodes, gong-like bass notes, a middle-register pattern and an upper line must remain distinguishable; indiscriminate pedal blurs these layers.

Words & theatre

Pelléas treats French prose as measured speech: repeated pitches, short syllabic gestures and selective melisma leave the words intelligible. The orchestra can remember or contradict the conversation. The songs range from Verlaine’s metrical verse to Louÿs’s prose poems.

Sister arts

Objects and texts, with the nature of each musical connection made explicit.

Documented use

Debussy chose an adaptation of Hokusai’s wave for the cover of Durand’s 1905 edition of La Mer. The printed score isolates and stylises the wave from the view shown here. The score’s subtitle remains ‘three symphonic sketches’.

Shared context

Whistler’s series of painted Nocturnes supplied Debussy with a model for the title of his orchestral triptych. Whistler had borrowed a musical genre-name to direct attention towards arrangements of colour; Debussy took it back with that pictorial implication. This Thames view represents the series.

Documented useStéphane Mallarmé

L’Après-midi d’un faune — opening

1876
FrenchCes nymphes, je les veux perpétuer. Si clair, Leur incarnat léger, qu’il voltige dans l’air Assoupi de sommeils touffus. Aimai-je un rêve ?
English · Contrapunctus translationThese nymphs, I want to perpetuate them. So clear, Their light flesh-pink, that it flutters in the air Drowsy with thickets of sleep. Did I love a dream?

The faun’s memory blurs encounter and invention. In Debussy’s Prélude, the returning flute phrase acquires changing harmonic contexts. Mallarmé attended the 1894 premiere and wrote his approval to Debussy afterwards. The excerpt preserves the opening’s interrupted syntax.

Editorial comparison

The title of Monet’s Le Havre harbour painting helped give Impressionism its name after the 1874 exhibition. Debussy was repeatedly placed under that label. Compare the few dark boats that establish distance against the harbour’s coloured marks with the separation of rhythmic layers in La Mer.

Lineage

Later connectionsMaurice RavelOlivier MessiaenPierre BoulezBéla BartókTōru Takemitsu

Ravel was a contemporary with a distinct technique. Messiaen inherited the possibility of making a pitch collection and its colour structural; Boulez studied the changing continuity of Jeux. Bartók’s encounter with Debussy helped him hear alternatives to German chromatic harmony. Tōru Takemitsu later treated resonance and instrumental spacing as compositional material in his engagement with Debussy.
