22.1Life

Fanny Mendelssohn was born in Hamburg in 1805, the eldest child of Abraham and Lea Mendelssohn. The family moved to Berlin in 1811. Her grandfather, the philosopher Moses Mendelssohn, had argued for Jewish civil equality; her parents later had their children baptised as Christians, and the family adopted the additional surname Bartholdy. Fanny and Felix received closely comparable musical training. Their mother began their piano lessons, Ludwig Berger developed their playing, and Carl Friedrich Zelter taught composition. Bach’s counterpoint belonged to the household’s practical vocabulary from the start.

At thirteen Fanny could play the twenty-four preludes of the first book of The Well-Tempered Clavier from memory. The family’s musical gatherings allowed both siblings to hear their pieces performed, discuss revisions and encounter established musicians. Their prospects nevertheless diverged. In an 1820 letter Abraham told Fanny that music might become Felix’s profession but should remain an ornament of her life. The restriction reflected the position of a wealthy daughter. Her manuscripts include sonatas, fugues and large vocal works alongside the songs that later became her best-known publications.

The name on the title pageSix of Fanny’s songs appeared in Felix’s Opp. 8 and 9 under his name. When Queen Victoria chose Italien to sing during his 1842 visit, Felix explained that his sister had composed it.

BerlinA household with an audience

In 1829 she married Wilhelm Hensel, a painter who supported her composing and contributed drawings to her manuscripts. Their son, Sebastian, was born in 1830. At the family house in Leipziger Straße she developed the Sunday concerts, or Sonntagsmusiken, into a sustained musical institution. She rehearsed performers, played piano and directed ensembles; invited listeners included artists, visiting musicians and members of Berlin’s educated society. These ‘domestic’ events combined a private address and patronage with demanding music and serious preparation. They gave her an audience without the professional independence available to Felix.

The works of the early 1830s show how far that activity extended beyond short piano pieces. Lobgesang sets sacred words for soloists, chorus and orchestra; the Overture in C major requires an orchestra without singers. Her E♭ major String Quartet of 1834 begins with an Adagio rather than a conventional sonata Allegro. Its succession of slow introduction, quick movement, Romanze and finale gives expressive contrast a leading role. All four instruments frequently exchange the foreground.

1839–41Italy and the calendar

The Hensels travelled to Italy in 1839–40. In Rome, Fanny met the young Charles Gounod, who admired her playing and introduced another kind of professional response to her music. She played German repertory for the French musicians at the Villa Medici, including Bach and Beethoven. Travel also brought a temporary release from the fixed obligations of the Berlin household. Her diary records places, encounters and musical experiences. The piano cycle she wrote after her return makes time and memory audible.

A joint manuscriptDas Jahr comprises twelve months and a postlude. Its illustrated fair copy joins Fanny’s notation to Wilhelm’s drawings and literary mottoes: the object is both a score for performance and a family album.

Das Jahr, composed in 1841, organises twelve months into a large cycle through contrasting tempos, transitions, returns and chorale references. March draws on the Easter hymn Christ ist erstanden; December introduces Vom Himmel hoch. Such quotations place the private year within a shared religious calendar. Rapid figuration can occupy both hands while a slower melodic strand must be projected from inside it. The pianist has to distinguish structural voices by touch and duration.

PublicationA short public career

Felix remained her closest musical correspondent, but his advice about publication was cautious and sometimes discouraging. They exchanged detailed criticism and depended on each other’s judgement while occupying unequal social positions. In 1846 Fanny decided to publish under her own name. Songs became Op. 1, piano pieces Op. 2, and further collections followed. The Gartenlieder, Op. 3, translate the social practice of ensemble singing into six unaccompanied mixed-voice songs; their parts require independent singers.

The D minor Piano Trio, completed in 1847, combines a turbulent opening Allegro, a slower lyrical movement, a short movement explicitly called Lied and an extended finale. The piano’s figuration and the strings’ sustained lines create different rates of movement within one pulse. On 14 May 1847 Hensel suffered a stroke while rehearsing music for a Sunday concert and died that day, aged forty-one. Felix died less than six months later. Her trio appeared posthumously as Op. 11 in 1850, but much of her music remained in manuscript for generations. Modern editions have made the distinction between the handful of published opus numbers and the scale of her actual working catalogue impossible to ignore.

22.2The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄢Piano voices

The accompaniment has voices of its own. In the piano songs and Das Jahr, sustained melodic notes, inner counter-lines and figuration have separate phrase lengths. Pedalling must support the longer notes while keeping their chromatic neighbours distinct.

♭Harmonic direction

Chromatic bass motion and altered inner parts can destabilise an otherwise singable phrase. The E♭ major Quartet repeatedly sets the nominal home key against darker regions; its opening Adagio postpones the kind of decisive tonic statement expected from a first-movement Allegro.

𝅘𝅥Song & text

Goethe, Heine, Eichendorff and Rückert supply different metres and speaking voices. In Nachtwanderer, piano movement continues beneath the singer’s description of silence, keeping the nocturnal landscape unsettled.

𝄋Cyclic construction

Das Jahr binds separate pieces through transitions, recurrence and the ordering of keys. Its thirteenth piece, the postlude, brings the calendar to a formal conclusion.

𝄡Ensemble conversation

The Quartet, Trio and Gartenlieder distribute responsibility among performers. The pianist’s capacity to sustain activity underneath a string melody is especially important in the Trio: the three players sustain an equal partnership through contrasting textures.

22.3Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1828
    Easter Sonata in A majorPiano
    A four-movement sonata long misattributed to Felix; its contrapuntal writing and religious associations belong to Fanny’s early large-scale work.
  2. 1831
    LobgesangSoloists, chorus and orchestra
    A cantata associated with the birth of her son, Sebastian; Felix later used the same name for his symphony-cantata.
  3. 1832
    Overture in C majorOrchestra
    A slow introduction opens into an energetic Allegro; evidence of orchestral composition beyond the songs and piano collections.
  4. 1834
    String Quartet in E♭ majorTwo violins, viola and cello
    Four movements beginning with an Adagio; the Romanze and finale make a different balance from the conventional sonata-first quartet.
  5. 1841
    Das JahrPiano
    Twelve months and a postlude; chorales, transitions and recurring material turn a calendar into a continuous large-scale design.
  6. 1846
    GartenliederOp. 3Unaccompanied mixed voices
    Six part-songs published in 1847, bringing the cultivation of a private vocal ensemble into print.
  7. 1846 (published)
    Vier Lieder für das PianoforteOp. 2Piano
    Four songs for piano: the title asks the performer to distinguish a singing voice from the instrument’s accompanying activity.
  8. 1847
    Piano Trio in D minorOp. 11Piano, violin and cello
    A substantial four-movement work, published in 1850; the third movement is a short Lied before the expanded finale.
  9. 1847 (published)
    Sechs LiederOp. 7Voice and piano
    Includes the Eichendorff setting Nachtwanderer; the opus number records publication.

22.5Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Wilhelm drew Fanny in the year they married, with a floral wreath and carefully articulated dress. His drawings later shared the pages of the illustrated manuscript of Das Jahr. Their collaboration belongs to the material history of the music.

Joseph von Eichendorff

Nachts · set as Nachtwanderer

Published 1826; Hensel’s setting published 1847
GermanIch wandre durch die stille Nacht, Da schleicht der Mond so heimlich sacht Oft aus der dunkeln Wolkenhülle, Und hin und her im Tal, Erwacht die Nachtigall Dann wieder alles grau und stille. O wunderbarer Nachtgesang, Von fern im Land der Ströme Gang, Leis Schauern in den dunkeln Bäumen — Irrst die Gedanken mir, Mein wirres Singen hier Ist wie ein Rufen nur aus Träumen.
Our translationI walk through the silent night; the moon steals softly, secretly, now and again from its dark covering of cloud. Here and there in the valley the nightingale wakes — then all is grey and still once more. O strange song of the night, the rivers’ movement far across the land, a faint trembling in the dark trees — you send my thoughts astray; my tangled singing here is only like a call out of dreams.

Hensel’s Op. 7 No. 1 gives the poem a piano part whose continuing motion complicates the repeated claims of stillness. The final ‘Rufen’ is a call from dreams. The sung German text supplies a precise point of comparison with Robert Schumann’s different Eichendorff settings.
