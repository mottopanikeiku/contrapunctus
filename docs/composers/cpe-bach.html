ILife

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach was the second surviving son of Johann Sebastian Bach and Maria Barbara Bach. He was born in Weimar in 1714; Georg Philipp Telemann was one of his godfathers. The family moved to Cöthen in 1717 and Leipzig in 1723, where his father became Thomaskantor. Emanuel learned the keyboard and composition at home, then studied law at Leipzig and Frankfurt an der Oder. Music, rather than the law, supplied his profession: by 1738 he had entered the circle of the Prussian crown prince Frederick, who became king in 1740.

BerlinThe accompanist and the soloist

Frederick played the flute, composed and maintained an orchestra whose leading musicians included Johann Joachim Quantz and the brothers Graun. Bach’s employment was chiefly as a keyboard accompanist, not as the court’s principal composer. The king’s preference for an Italianate melodic style did not determine what Bach published under his own name. His six ‘Prussian’ keyboard sonatas of 1742 and six ‘Württemberg’ sonatas of 1744 put contrasting textures, unexpected rests and highly ornamented slow movements inside the three-movement sonata. The player had to manage changes of character as precisely as changes of fingering.

EmpfindsamkeitThe German term means sensibility or susceptibility to feeling. In music it describes an expressive practice, not a fixed list of forms: interrupted phrases, dissonant appoggiaturas and sudden changes of register can make a melody behave like speech.

Bach’s preferred resources included the clavichord, in which a metal tangent strikes a string and remains in contact with it. Pressure on the key can alter the sustained sound; the instrument can even produce a small vibrato, called Bebung. Its low volume suits a room rather than a concert hall. On it, an accented dissonance and its quieter resolution become physical events under the finger. Bach also wrote for harpsichord and the developing fortepiano: the word Clavier in his publications does not identify one instrument.

His Versuch über die wahre Art das Clavier zu spielen appeared in two parts, in 1753 and 1762. The first discusses fingering, ornaments and performance; the second deals with accompaniment and improvisation. It addresses the working musician who must interpret a small ornamental sign, realise a figured bass and invent a fantasia. Bach treats expression as a practical discipline. The performer must recognise the affect of a passage and communicate it through timing, articulation and touch, rather than apply an unvarying legato to everything on the page.

HamburgChurches and paying listeners

In 1768 Bach succeeded Telemann as music director of Hamburg’s principal churches and cantor at the Johanneum. The move replaced service to one monarch with responsibilities to an urban institution. He organised church performances, supplied Passion music, taught and gave public concerts. His sacred repertory included adaptations of other composers’ music as well as his own pieces; the office demanded a usable annual programme, not a succession of entirely new scores. The oratorio Die Auferstehung und Himmelfahrt Jesu, on Karl Wilhelm Ramler’s poetry, belonged to the concert life as well as the religious culture of the city.

A commission without restraintBaron Gottfried van Swieten commissioned the six string symphonies Wq 182 in 1773 and encouraged Bach not to restrict himself out of concern for the players’ difficulties. Their leaps, unison attacks and abrupt harmonic turns exploit that permission.

The Hamburg symphonies are not keyboard fantasias enlarged mechanically for strings. They use the collective attack of an ensemble: a rapid unison can break into separated parts, or a slow movement can begin without the reassurance of a settled new key. The four orchestral symphonies Wq 183 give wind instruments more independent work. Bach’s Heilig, for two choirs and orchestral groups, makes physical separation part of the composition; an angelic acclamation and a human response occupy different musical spaces.

He remained a publisher and businessman as well as a salaried church musician. From 1779 the collections für Kenner und Liebhaber — for connoisseurs and amateurs — brought sonatas, rondos and fantasias to subscribers. The titles identify overlapping audiences rather than a simple division into difficult and easy music. A rondo might return to its refrain after an excursion into an unexpectedly distant key; a fantasia might suspend bar-lines and move through recitative-like gestures before finding a cadence.

InheritanceTwo generations of pupils at a distance

Haydn told his early biographers that he had studied Emanuel Bach’s sonatas closely and owed him a great deal. Beethoven, writing to Breitkopf & Härtel in 1809, requested more of Bach’s keyboard music for both enjoyment and study. Neither connection requires treating Emanuel as an incomplete version of the later Viennese composers. His deliberately unstable phrase lengths and changes of affect remain different from Haydn’s games with regularity or Beethoven’s cumulative development. He also guarded the earlier Bach inheritance, preserving manuscripts and performing music by his father. At his death in Hamburg in 1788, he left both a performing tradition documented in print and a body of music that refuses to keep one tone of voice for long.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄾Rhetoric

Rests are punctuation. A phrase can stop before its cadence, restart in another register or answer a lyrical idea with an unaccompanied outburst. In the free fantasias the absence of regular bar-lines allows harmonic rhythm to follow an imagined speaker rather than a dance step.

♯Harmony

Appoggiaturas put the dissonant note on the beat and delay the consonance that explains it. Diminished-seventh chords offer several possible exits; Bach can use one to turn away from the key a listener has just begun to recognise. The surprise depends on an audible tonal expectation, not on the abandonment of key.

𝆏Keyboard touch

The clavichord permits changes of pressure after the attack, unlike the harpsichord. The Versuch distinguishes degrees of separation between notes and the expressive weight of ornaments. A modern piano can project this music, but its long sustain can obscure the rests and short articulations that organise a phrase.

𝄋Form

Sonatas, rondos and fantasias coexist. In a rondo the familiar refrain supplies a reference point against which a remote episode sounds more remote; in a fantasia the succession of affects can determine the order of sections. Even a repeated passage may be varied rather than reproduced literally.

𝄢Ensemble writing

The string symphonies Wq 182 turn leaps and sudden dynamics into ensemble problems: the players must change direction together. In the later Wq 183 symphonies, wind parts contribute distinctive lines rather than merely reinforce string harmony. Heilig distributes its answering choirs in space.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1742
    Six Prussian SonatasWq 48Keyboard
    Published with a dedication to Frederick II. Three-movement designs whose slow movements make ornamentation and changing affect central to the argument.
  2. 1744
    Six Württemberg SonatasWq 49Keyboard
    The set extends the range of keys and rhetorical contrasts of the Prussian collection; not simply salon miniatures.
  3. 1750
    Cello Concerto in A minorWq 170Solo cello, strings and continuo
    Angular orchestral gestures surround a solo part that alternates athletic passagework and sustained melodic declamation.
  4. 1753
    Fantasia in C minorWq 63/6, third movementKeyboard
    The final movement of the sixth Probestück accompanying the first part of the Versuch. A practical demonstration of changing affect and free delivery.
  5. 1758
    Geistliche Oden und LiederWq 194Voice and keyboard; poems by C. F. Gellert
    Fifty-four devotional songs, designed for domestic use as well as attentive musical study. The keyboard writing is not restricted to neutral chordal support.
  6. 1773
    Six String SymphoniesWq 182Strings and continuo
    Written for Baron van Swieten. Unison attacks, wide intervals and unexpected junctions between movements make small forces sound volatile.
  7. 1774; revised 1778
    Die Auferstehung und Himmelfahrt JesuWq 240Soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Ramler’s Resurrection and Ascension oratorio, with reflective arias, narrative recitatives and large public choruses.
  8. 1775–76
    Four Orchestral SymphoniesWq 183Orchestra with independent wind parts
    Published as symphonies with twelve obbligato parts. The D major opening makes a repeated note and dislocated accents into a large-scale provocation.
  9. 1776
    HeiligWq 217Alto, two choirs and orchestral groups
    An introductory arietta leads to an antiphonal setting of the Sanctus in German. The contrast between angelic and human choirs is architectural as well as harmonic.
  10. 1787
    Fantasia in F♯ minorWq 67Keyboard
    A late free fantasia whose passages of measured motion alternate with recitative-like writing. A related version with violin bears the title C. P. E. Bachs Empfindungen.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Menzel reconstructs the court where Bach earned his salary: Frederick plays the flute, and Bach is seated at the keyboard. Painted a century after the events, this is historical theatre, not an eyewitness record. Its arrangement nevertheless makes the employment relationship visible: the accompanist watches the royal soloist, while the listeners occupy the room around them. The picture should not be mistaken for a portrait of Bach improvising his own fantasias.

Christian Fürchtegott Gellert

Die Ehre Gottes aus der Natur — first stanza

1757
GermanDie Himmel rühmen des Ewigen Ehre, Ihr Schall pflanzt seinen Namen fort. Ihn rühmt der Erdkreis, ihn preisen die Meere; Vernimm, o Mensch, ihr göttlich Wort!
Our translationThe heavens proclaim the Eternal’s honour; their sound carries his name onwards. The earth proclaims him, the seas praise him; hear, human being, their divine word!

Bach set Gellert’s Geistliche Oden und Lieder in 1758; Beethoven returned to six of the same poet’s religious texts in Op. 48. Gellert’s short clauses and scriptural imagery offer a different task from operatic recitative: clear delivery for a domestic singer without making the accompaniment inert. Text: the 1757 collection.

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach

Versuch — the performer’s feeling

1753, Part I, chapter 3, §13
German proseIndem ein Musickus nicht anders rühren kan, er sey dann selbst gerührt; so muß er nothwendig sich selbst in alle Affecten setzen können, welche er bey seinen Zuhörern erregen will; er giebt ihnen seine Empfindungen zu verstehen und bewegt sie solchergestalt am besten zur Mit-Empfindung.
Our translationSince a musician cannot move others unless he is himself moved, he must necessarily be able to put himself into all the states of feeling that he wishes to arouse in his listeners; he makes his feelings understood by them and in this way moves them most effectively to share those feelings.

The prose belongs to the eighteenth-century discussion of acting and sensibility as much as to keyboard instruction. Bach’s next sentences distinguish languid or sorrowful passages from forceful or cheerful ones: the performer must change, not display a single permanent expression. The digital Versuch supplies the passage in its original context.

Haydn’s recollections and Beethoven’s 1809 request for more keyboard works document study, not merely a resemblance noticed later. The Versuch transmitted a performer’s grammar of ornaments, accompaniment and expression across the change from harpsichord and clavichord to piano.
