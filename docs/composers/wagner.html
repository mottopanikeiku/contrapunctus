RichardWagner

Born 22 May 1813, LeipzigDied 13 February 1883, Venice

An opera composer who made the orchestra remember what the characters conceal, and built a theatre for the result.

ILife

Wagner grew up among actors, singers and theatrical households in Leipzig and Dresden. His father died in 1813; his mother subsequently married the actor and painter Ludwig Geyer. Literature preceded serious musical training: as a schoolboy Wagner attempted a tragedy before he had mastered composition. Beethoven’s symphonies and Weber’s operas supplied different models, one for the weight of an orchestral argument, the other for a German theatre of supernatural events and sharply characterised instruments. Lessons with Theodor Weinlig in 1831 gave him a grounding in counterpoint. The later claim to have rebuilt opera came from someone who first learned its routines as a working theatre musician.

He held posts in Würzburg, Magdeburg, Königsberg and Riga, married the actress Minna Planer in 1836, and repeatedly found himself in debt. His early operas tried incompatible languages: Die Feen follows German Romantic opera, while Das Liebesverbot, after Shakespeare’s Measure for Measure, adopts a more Italianate manner. In 1839 the Wagners fled Riga and travelled by sea to London, then to Paris. There he copied, arranged and wrote journalism while trying to enter the operatic establishment. Meyerbeer, whom Wagner would later attack, helped him obtain a hearing for Rienzi. The grand-opera machinery of public ceremony, political catastrophe and large chorus was something Wagner had practised, not merely rejected.

DresdenOpera and revolution

Rienzi succeeded in Dresden in 1842, bringing an appointment as royal Kapellmeister the following year. Der fliegende Holländer, also first staged there, concentrated its supernatural plot around recurrent musical ideas and Senta’s ballad. The ballad is not an incidental number: it tells the legend that the opera subsequently makes real. Tannhäuser opposed the Venusberg to the world of pilgrimage and song contest, while Lohengrin made the demand for a name destroy the trust on which its marriage depended. All three still contain identifiable numbers, choruses and closed scenes. Their later revisions show Wagner testing the relation between those forms and continuous drama.

In Dresden he also entered revolutionary politics. During the uprising of May 1849 he associated with the anarchist Mikhail Bakunin and helped the insurgents; a warrant forced him to flee Saxony. He reached Switzerland through the assistance of Liszt, who conducted Lohengrin in Weimar in 1850. Wagner could not attend. Exile interrupted his conducting income and separated the making of a work from the practical experience of hearing it. For several years he wrote theoretical prose and the poems for an enormous cycle before returning to sustained musical composition.

The polemical recordDas Judenthum in der Musik appeared under the pseudonym K. Freigedank in 1850 and in an expanded, signed version in 1869. Its attacks on Jewish musicians, including Mendelssohn and Meyerbeer, are Wagner’s own published antisemitism, not an invention of later admirers.

The Zurich essays include The Artwork of the Future and Opera and Drama. They propose a theatre in which verse, action and music cooperate rather than serve a sequence of star turns. The practice did not always obey the prescription: Wagner’s orchestras often generate meanings that the spoken proposition cannot contain. His cultural politics were equally consequential. The antisemitic essays made exclusion part of his account of German art. His later friendship with individual Jewish musicians does not cancel those texts; nor does recognising their virulence require treating every chromatic chord as a coded political statement.

The RingA drama written backwards, composed forwards

He first planned a drama about Siegfried’s death, then supplied the earlier events needed to explain it. The result was a preliminary evening, Das Rheingold, followed by Die Walküre, Siegfried and Götterdämmerung. The libretto was assembled substantially backwards; the music was composed in dramatic order, beginning with Rheingold in 1853. Its opening holds an E♭ harmony for 136 bars while the texture grows from a low foundation into moving arpeggios. A world is established by adding register, instruments and rhythmic motion before the harmony begins to move.

The cycle turns possession into a musical problem. Gold becomes a ring, a ring becomes power, and contracts that supposedly guarantee order entangle their makers. Recurrent motifs connect objects, actions and memories, but their harmony and orchestration change as knowledge changes. Wotan’s Act II narration in Die Walküre can therefore recall earlier events without merely repeating their stage action. The orchestra knows relationships that a character may not yet understand. The term leitmotif is useful provided it does not reduce these mutable phrases to labels in a catalogue.

Wagner stopped work on Siegfried in 1857, after its second act, and did not resume the third until 1869. The interruption contains two radically different operas. Tristan und Isolde, completed in 1859, turns waiting, remembering and the desire to escape daylight into prolonged harmonic tension. Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg, completed in 1867, places new composition inside a comic civic world of craft rules, apprentices, rivalry and public judgement. Its counterpoint allows the stage to become disorderly without the score losing control: the Act II riot is organised through accumulating musical layers.

TristanA dominant without its expected answer

Arthur Schopenhauer’s philosophy, encountered in 1854, offered Wagner a language for desire that renews itself instead of reaching satisfaction. His attachment to Mathilde Wesendonck, whose husband Otto supported him in Zurich, provided a more immediate emotional circumstance. Five settings of Mathilde’s poems were composed alongside Tristan; Wagner explicitly called two of them studies for the opera. None of this makes the score a diary with orchestration. Its decisive mechanism is technical: a dissonance seems to approach a cadence, then becomes the beginning of another phrase, or resolves to a chord that itself requires resolution.

Four pitchesThe first “Tristan chord” is F–B–D♯–G♯. The opening phrase moves towards E7, not a settled A minor tonic. Passing notes and appoggiaturas complicate the route; the harmonic destination is itself an unstable dominant seventh.

The first chord had precedents; the novelty lies in its context and the scale of its consequences. A rising chromatic line makes each arrival provisional, while rests divide the initial phrases without completing them. Act III stretches this behaviour through Tristan’s delirium, where remembered motifs fail to recover the person they invoke. The final B major close belongs to Isolde’s transfiguration after Tristan’s death, not to an ordinary reconciliation enacted by the lovers. Debussy, Mahler and Schoenberg heard different possibilities in this combination of voice-leading and suspended dramatic time.

A king and a theatreMunich, Tribschen, Bayreuth

In 1864 the young Ludwig II of Bavaria summoned Wagner to Munich, paid debts and supported productions. Tristan finally reached the stage there in 1865 under Hans von Bülow; Meistersinger followed in 1868. Wagner’s affair with Bülow’s wife Cosima, Liszt’s daughter, made this musical alliance personally destructive. Their children Isolde, Eva and Siegfried were born before the Bülows’ divorce; Wagner and Cosima married in 1870. At their home in Tribschen, near Lucerne, the small ensemble of the Siegfried Idyll played on the staircase on Christmas morning that year. Its domestic scale sits beside, rather than outside, the Ring’s musical language.

Wagner wanted a festival theatre where the public attended the whole cycle and could not see the orchestra as a competing spectacle. Bayreuth’s Festspielhaus placed the players in a covered, recessed pit and dispensed with the tiers of private boxes usual in an opera house. Donations and Ludwig’s assistance enabled its opening with the complete Ring in August 1876. The festival incurred heavy debts, and its stage machinery did not always fulfil the composer’s demands. Nevertheless, the altered balance between stage and hidden orchestra became part of the works’ performance history, not simply a container for them.

Parsifal, first performed at Bayreuth in 1882, replaces the Ring’s repeated contests over power with ritual, failed compassion and the recovery of a wounded community. Its orchestral transformations connect scenes that would otherwise require conventional changes of scenery; bells, brass and widely spaced strings make distance audible. Wagner died in Venice the following February. Cosima later assumed control of Bayreuth, preserving a particular interpretation of his theatrical intentions while turning them into an institutional orthodoxy. The festival’s subsequent nationalist and Nazi affiliations belong to its documented history, alongside the composer’s own earlier political writings. The scores have meanwhile sustained stagings that argue with that inheritance rather than reproduce it.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞The remembering orchestra

A motif can refer backwards while the singer speaks in the present. In the Ring, changes of harmony, register and instrumental colour alter a motif’s implication; it is not a fixed musical equivalent of a noun. Listen for a familiar contour in an unfamiliar bass or instrument before consulting a list of motif names.

♯Chromatic voice-leading

Individual notes move by semitone while other notes remain held, producing chords that can be interpreted in more than one key. Tristan repeatedly reaches a dominant without delivering the expected tonic. The effect depends on still hearing the possibility of a cadence: it is extended tonal syntax, not an absence of tonal reference.

𝅘𝅥Declamation

German word stress governs many vocal phrases, especially the alliterative verse of the Ring. Long orchestral lines may continue under short, uneven sung units. Conversely, the measured song forms in Meistersinger are audible social rules: Walther’s song is judged because its phrase structure can be heard against the guild’s expectations.

𝄢Brass and depth

The Ring’s brass extends from trumpets and bass trumpet to trombones, contrabass tuba and the horn-family instruments now called Wagner tubas. These provide different attacks and blends, not simply additional loudness. A sustained brass harmony can remain behind moving strings, giving the ear foreground and background without a change of stage position.

𝄋Long spans

Local phrases are joined through orchestral transitions, returning motifs and postponed cadences. Wagner nevertheless uses sharp sectional contrasts: the Tristan Prelude’s opening silences, the closed ballad in Holländer, the songs and chorales of Meistersinger. Continuous drama does not mean an uninterrupted surface of equally dense music.

𝄆Counterpoint and comedy

The Meistersinger Prelude combines themes already heard separately, making their eventual coexistence a musical event before the plot begins. The Act II street fight builds crowded action from imitation and overlapping entries. Wagner’s contrapuntal training is most exposed here, where instrumental lines can remain lucid while the characters misunderstand one another.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1841
    Der fliegende HolländerWWV 63Opera in three acts; soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Premiered in Dresden in 1843. Storm music, Senta’s ballad and the contrast between living and ghostly sailors bind the legend to recurring musical material; the ending was subsequently revised.
  2. 1845
    TannhäuserWWV 70Opera in three acts
    The Dresden original and the revisions associated with Paris in 1861 differ substantially, especially in the Venusberg music. Pilgrimage, sensuality and formal song competition occupy audibly different musical spaces.
  3. 1848
    LohengrinWWV 75Opera in three acts
    Premiered by Liszt in Weimar in 1850. The Prelude begins in a divided, high violin register; public ceremony and private mistrust eventually destroy the marriage at the centre of the plot.
  4. 1854
    Das RheingoldWWV 86AMusic drama in one continuous evening; large orchestra
    The preliminary evening of the Ring. First staged separately in Munich in 1869, against Wagner’s preference for a complete cycle. The opening E♭ field accumulates motion before introducing harmonic change.
  5. 1856
    Die WalküreWWV 86BMusic drama in three acts
    Premiered separately in Munich in 1870. The first act builds from a storm and a stranger’s arrival to the recognition of Siegmund and Sieglinde; Wotan’s farewell in Act III makes remembered motifs carry the cost of his decision.
  6. 1857–58
    Wesendonck-LiederWWV 91Voice and piano
    Five settings of Mathilde Wesendonck. Im Treibhaus and Träume are designated studies for Tristan. The familiar orchestral cycle largely uses Felix Mottl’s later scoring; Wagner himself orchestrated Träume.
  7. 1859
    Tristan und IsoldeWWV 90Opera in three acts
    First performed in Munich in 1865. The Prelude’s chromatic voice-leading becomes the opera’s method of postponement; the cor anglais shepherd’s tune in Act III provides an exposed contrast to the crowded orchestral memories.
  8. 1867
    Die Meistersinger von NürnbergWWV 96Opera in three acts; soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Premiered in Munich in 1868. Song, counterpoint and spoken inflection are made into subjects of the plot. Sachs’s reflections on delusion do not erase the exclusionary nationalism of his final address.
  9. 1870
    Siegfried IdyllWWV 103Small orchestra
    First played privately at Tribschen on Christmas morning. Music shared with Siegfried is recast in chamber proportions; the later published version permits larger string forces.
  10. 1871
    SiegfriedWWV 86CMusic drama in three acts
    Its composition was interrupted for twelve years between Acts II and III. Forging, forest listening and the awakening of Brünnhilde require different kinds of orchestral motion; the completed work was premiered at Bayreuth in 1876.
  11. 1874
    GötterdämmerungWWV 86DPrologue and three acts; soloists, chorus and large orchestra
    The cycle’s conclusion, first heard in 1876. The Funeral March recalls a biography through motifs; the final orchestral pages juxtapose memories whose dramatic owners have died or lost their power.
  12. 1882
    ParsifalWWV 111Stage consecration festival play in three acts
    First performed at Bayreuth under Hermann Levi. Ceremonial repetition, compressed chromatic episodes and the transformation music give the three acts different experiences of time.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Rackham’s illustration belongs to an edition of Wagner’s own dramas, not to an independently assembled Norse mythology. The swimming figures and curling water answer the beginning of Rheingold, where the stage is submerged and the orchestra is still emerging from its sustained E♭. The book makes visible the physical setting from which the gold is stolen; Wagner’s later motifs keep recalling that loss after the scene has changed.

A parallel treatment of the medieval legend, not a documented source for Wagner. Leighton exhibited the painting at the Royal Academy in 1907, decades after the opera. Its historical costume fixes the lovers in a visible medieval setting; Wagner’s second act strips much of that social world away through darkness and the lovers’ argument against “day”. The difference between the image’s legible bodies and the music’s unsettled harmony is the useful comparison.

Mathilde Wesendonck

Im Treibhaus — opening stanzas

1858
GermanHochgewölbte Blätterkronen, Baldachine von Smaragd: Kinder ihr aus fernen Zonen, Saget mir, warum ihr klagt? Schweigend neiget ihr die Zweige, Malet Zeichen in die Luft; Und der Leiden stummer Zeuge Steiget aufwärts, süßer Duft.
Our translationHigh-vaulted crowns of leaves, canopies of emerald: children from distant regions, tell me why you lament. Silently you bend your branches, trace signs into the air; and the mute witness of suffering rises upward, sweet fragrance.

Wagner labelled this song a study for Tristan und Isolde; its material is connected with the desolate Prelude to Act III. The greenhouse gives the poem a precise condition: living things have been transported from their native climate and cannot return. The first two of six stanzas are quoted here; the complete German text carries the comparison into human exile.

Richard Wagner

Hans Sachs’s Wahn monologue — opening

Libretto completed 1862; opera completed 1867
GermanWahn! Wahn! Überall Wahn! Wohin ich forschend blick’ in Stadt- und Weltchronik, den Grund mir aufzufinden, warum gar bis aufs Blut die Leut’ sich quälen und schinden in unnütz toller Wut?
Our translationDelusion! Delusion! Delusion everywhere! Wherever I look in inquiry in the chronicles of town and world, to find the reason why, even to the shedding of blood, people torment and flay one another in useless, frantic rage.

In Act III of Die Meistersinger, the cobbler-poet reads the previous night’s street fight as an instance of recurrent human delusion. The orchestral recollections make his monologue an interpretation of music the audience has already heard. His decision to redirect this irrational energy into the song contest links the work’s comic action to its argument about making art.

VIListen

Recordings worth seeking out.

Wagner
Passed tobrucknerGustav MahlerRichard StraussClaude DebussyArnold SchoenbergAlban Berg

Bruckner borrowed brass colours and harmonic resources without adopting Wagner’s theatrical forms. Mahler learned from both the scores and the practical demands of conducting them. Debussy and Schoenberg began from Wagnerian chromaticism and pursued incompatible solutions to its cadential pressure: one often suspending functional direction, the other intensifying it until a tonic could no longer organise the whole.
