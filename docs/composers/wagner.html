Life

In 1839 Richard Wagner and his wife Minna fled Riga ahead of their creditors and took a storm-battered ship to London. He was twenty-six, an opera conductor with two completed operas and no success worth mentioning. The sea passage stayed with him. A story by Heinrich Heine about a captain condemned to sail for ever became Der fliegende Holländer; its overture begins over a bare fifth, with horns and bassoons calling across the strings’ storm. The Wagners went on to Paris, where he made arrangements for publishers and tried to persuade somebody to stage his music.

He had grown up inside the theatre. Born in Leipzig in 1813, he lost his father as an infant and moved with his mother into the household of Ludwig Geyer, an actor and painter in Dresden. Wagner wanted to write plays before he knew how to write music. Weber’s Der Freischütz and Beethoven’s symphonies supplied the sounds; Theodor Weinlig supplied counterpoint lessons in 1831. Jobs followed in Würzburg, Magdeburg, Königsberg and Riga. Die Feen remained unperformed, and Das Liebesverbot, his operatic version of Measure for Measure, collapsed after one performance in 1836. That year he married Minna, an actress.

1842–49A court conductor at the barricades

Meyerbeer helped get Rienzi accepted in Dresden. Its success in October 1842 won Wagner a court appointment the following year, and Dresden heard both Holländer and Tannhäuser. Senta’s ballad tells the Dutchman’s story before he walks into her house; the pilgrims’ chorus in Tannhäuser keeps returning against the music of Venus. These are operas with arias, choruses and recognisable stopping places, already full of phrases that remember an earlier scene. In Lohengrin, completed in 1848, the bride destroys her marriage by asking the one question her husband has forbidden: his name.

Wagner’s Dresden friends included the anarchist Mikhail Bakunin. He helped the insurgents during the May uprising of 1849, then fled when the revolt failed and a warrant was issued for his arrest. Liszt helped him escape and conducted Lohengrin in Weimar in 1850. Its composer was in Switzerland. In Zurich he wrote The Artwork of the Future and Opera and Drama, proposing a theatre in which poetry, acting and music worked together, and began planning the performances for which no theatre yet existed.

He also published Das Judenthum in der Musik in 1850, attacking Jewish musicians, including Mendelssohn and the Meyerbeer who had helped him. He claimed that Jews could only imitate the art of the nations among whom they lived. The essay first appeared under the pseudonym K. Freigedank; in 1869 he enlarged it and put his name on it. His antisemitism was a public campaign, renewed after he had secured fame and royal patronage.

1848–74Beginning at the end

The copyist’s penWagner asked Liszt to find someone who could turn his pencil drafts into a fair score. On 14 June 1854 he withdrew the request: Mathilde Wesendonck had given him a gold pen. “I must write neat scores for as long as I live on earth!”

The Ring began as a drama about Siegfried’s death. Wagner kept discovering that he needed another opera to explain what had happened before it. By 1852 he had the texts for four evenings: Das Rheingold, Die Walküre, Siegfried and Götterdämmerung. He composed the music forwards, starting Rheingold in 1853. A low E♭ in the double basses becomes an entire river: horns enter successively, strings begin to flow, and the harmony stays on E♭ for 136 bars.

Alberich steals the gold; Wotan steals the ring made from it; every bargain leaves somebody unpaid. The orchestra remembers the transactions. In Act II of Die Walküre, Wotan tells Brünnhilde how he reached this impasse, and music from the earlier drama rises beneath his account. A leitmotif can belong to a sword, a promise or a person, then change its meaning when another character hears it. By Siegfried’s funeral march, the orchestra can tell his ancestry and his life without a sung word.

1857–65The impossible love opera

Wagner put Siegfried aside in 1857, after Act II. He had discovered Schopenhauer in 1854 and fallen in love with Mathilde Wesendonck, whose husband Otto helped support him. The philosopher’s account of desire and the poet’s proximity fed Tristan und Isolde. Wagner set five of Mathilde’s poems in 1857–58, calling Im Treibhaus and Träume studies for the opera. Minna intercepted a letter to Mathilde in 1858; Wagner left Zurich, finishing Tristan the following year.

Four notesThe first Tristan chord contains F–B–D♯–G♯. It moves towards E7, which itself wants to resolve to A minor. Wagner leaves that next step unanswered.

The Prelude begins with cellos, a wind chord and an oboe climbing by semitones. Then silence. The next phrase tries again, higher. This refusal to settle becomes the lovers’ whole existence: daylight separates them, night promises a union it cannot sustain, and Act III makes Tristan wait for Isolde through a delirium of remembered music. In April 1859 Wagner wrote to Mathilde: “Only mediocre performances can save me! Completely good ones must drive people mad.” The opera ends in B major after Tristan has died.

Rescue arrived in the form of an eighteen-year-old king. Ludwig II summoned Wagner to Munich in 1864 and paid his debts. Hans von Bülow conducted the first Tristan there on 10 June 1865. Two months earlier Bülow’s wife Cosima, Liszt’s daughter, had borne Wagner a daughter named Isolde. The affair scandalised Munich; Ludwig was forced to send his composer away. Wagner settled at Tribschen beside Lake Lucerne.

1867–70A comedy and a staircase

At Tribschen he completed Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg, first performed in Munich in 1868. The cobbler Hans Sachs helps a young singer win a bride and a hearing from the town’s rule-bound guild. Its orchestra can organise a street riot as counterpoint and make a five-voice ensemble stand perfectly still. Sachs’s last speech turns from generosity towards the young into a warning against foreign influence on German art. The comedy ends with a national claim.

Wagner resumed Siegfried in 1869. He and Cosima married in August 1870, after the births of Eva and Siegfried as well as Isolde. On Christmas morning a small group of players assembled on the staircase of their house and woke Cosima with the Siegfried Idyll, a birthday surprise. Music shared with the opera filled a family home. Four years later Wagner finished Götterdämmerung.

1876–83The theatre on the hill

Out of sightBayreuth’s orchestra sits below the stage in a recessed, covered pit. The auditorium’s rising rows replace the customary tiers of side boxes; the audience sees the drama without seeing the players.

The complete Ring opened at Bayreuth in August 1876, Hans Richter conducting. Subscriptions had failed to pay for the theatre; another loan from Ludwig had saved it. Three cycles attracted an international audience and left an enormous deficit. Wagner’s theatrical demands tested the machinery as severely as his scores tested the musicians. For the Rhinemaidens, the singers had to climb onto tall contraptions that made them appear to swim.

Parsifal brought the festival back in 1882, under Hermann Levi. Its wounded king, Amfortas, suffers through the ceremony he must perform; bells and brass accompany the passage into the Grail hall. Wagner died in Venice on 13 February 1883 and was buried in the garden of Wahnfried, his Bayreuth house. Cosima took charge of the festival after him. Under later family leadership Bayreuth became a platform for Nazi culture. Hitler first visited the Wagner family in 1923 and was a regular festival guest from 1933 to 1940.

The Sound

𝄞The orchestra remembers

Wagner lets an instrumental phrase remember something the singer has left out. During Wotan’s account of his troubles in Act II of Die Walküre, motifs from Rheingold return beneath him. At Siegfried’s funeral, the orchestra recalls his family and his sword. The same tune can sound proud on brass, vulnerable in strings or ominous over a changed bass.

♯Semitones

A single moving note can keep a chord unsettled. In the Tristan Prelude, the oboe climbs G♯–A–A♯–B while the parts beneath it find new dissonances. The opening reaches E7, a chord that points towards A minor, and stops short of that arrival. Successive phrases rise from the silence and begin the same urgent business again.

𝅘𝅥Words and breath

The Ring’s alliterative German gives singers hard consonants to bite on and short phrases that can cut across an orchestral line. Meistersinger puts the rules of verse on stage: Walther’s song is examined, rejected and eventually taught into shape. Wagner can make us hear the difference between a guild’s correct exercise and a young man finding something to say.

𝄢Brass and anvils

The Ring enlarges the brass family with bass trumpet, contrabass tuba and Wagner tubas, played by horn players. Their blended chords give Valhalla its imposing entrance. Nibelheim has another sound altogether: eighteen anvils hammer underground. Six harps help illuminate the gold. These instruments belong to particular places and actions before they become part of a concert-hall spectacle.

𝄋Holding a chord

Rheingold begins with 136 bars on E♭. The double basses hold the root while successive horn entries unfold the chord; quicker figures spread through the strings. Almost everything changes except the harmony. Tristan does the reverse, making tiny harmonic changes carry enormous consequences while the lovers remain together in the same place, trying to prolong the night.

𝄆Comic counterpoint

In the Meistersinger Prelude, tunes introduced separately eventually fit together. Wagner lets us hear the reconciliation before anyone has quarrelled. At the end of Act II, the town’s street fight grows through overlapping entries and imitations, with each new arrival adding to the confusion. Then the crowd disperses and the Nightwatchman resumes his rounds. The orchestra has kept its head throughout.

Sister Arts

Rackham drew these swimmers for Margaret Armour’s English translation of Wagner’s dramas, published in 1910. Their hair and limbs curl with the water that fills the opening scene of Rheingold. A book illustrator could let them float freely; the first Bayreuth singers had to clamber onto tall swimming machines.

Leighton exhibited his lovers at the Royal Academy in 1907, and this reproduction appeared in that year’s exhibition book. Their medieval clothes place them in the chivalric world Wagner’s lovers long to escape. In the opera’s second act, Isolde puts out the torch that separates her from Tristan; their meeting depends on darkness.

Mathilde Wesendonck

Im Treibhaus — opening stanzas

1858
GermanHochgewölbte Blätterkronen, Baldachine von Smaragd: Kinder ihr aus fernen Zonen, Saget mir, warum ihr klagt? Schweigend neiget ihr die Zweige, Malet Zeichen in die Luft; Und der Leiden stummer Zeuge Steiget aufwärts, süßer Duft.
Our translationHigh-vaulted crowns of leaves, canopies of emerald: children from distant regions, tell me why you lament. Silently you bend your branches, trace signs into the air; and the mute witness of suffering rises upward, sweet fragrance.

Mathilde’s plants live far from the countries where they grew. Wagner was living in a cottage on her husband’s Zurich estate when he wrote the song; he marked it as a study for Tristan, whose third-act Prelude takes up its desolate music. These are the first two of six stanzas; the complete German poem turns the greenhouse into a place of human exile.

Richard Wagner

Hans Sachs’s Wahn monologue — opening

Libretto completed 1862; opera completed 1867
GermanWahn! Wahn! Überall Wahn! Wohin ich forschend blick’ in Stadt- und Weltchronik, den Grund mir aufzufinden, warum gar bis aufs Blut die Leut’ sich quälen und schinden in unnütz toller Wut?
Our translationDelusion! Delusion! Delusion everywhere! Wherever I look in inquiry in the chronicles of town and world, to find the reason why, even to the shedding of blood, people torment and flay one another in useless, frantic rage.

Morning after the street fight: Sachs sits with his book and tries to account for the neighbours’ behaviour. His word Wahn covers delusion, folly and madness. He decides to put that unruly energy to work in the song contest, then helps Walther turn a dream into a song the judges can hear.

Passed toAnton BrucknerGustav MahlerRichard StraussClaude DebussyArnold SchoenbergAlban Berg

Mahler learnt Wagner from the conductor’s desk, balancing these orchestras against singers night after night. Bruckner brought Wagner tubas into the symphony. Debussy went to Bayreuth, then quoted Tristan in Golliwogg’s Cake-walk and made fun of it. Schoenberg carried the same chromatic pressure into music that eventually dispensed with a home key.
