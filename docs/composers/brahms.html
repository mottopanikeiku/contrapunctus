Life

In the autumn of 1853 a twenty-year-old pianist from Hamburg arrived at the Schumanns’ home in Düsseldorf with an introduction from Joseph Joachim. Johannes Brahms played his own music to them. Robert Schumann heard in him a composer of the future and announced him that October in an article called Neue Bahnen (New Paths). Publishers became interested. Brahms was now expected to fulfil a public prophecy before most of the public had heard any of his music. His piano sonatas were large-scale works. The symphonies Schumann expected took much longer to arrive.

Brahms was born in Hamburg in 1833. His father, Johann Jakob, earned his living playing instruments including the horn and double bass. His mother, Christiane Nissen, was a seamstress. He studied piano with Otto Cossel and then piano and composition with Eduard Marxsen, who grounded him in Bach, Mozart and Beethoven. He played, arranged and accompanied for money. A tour with the Hungarian violinist Ede Reményi in 1853 brought him to Joachim, and Joachim sent him on to Düsseldorf.

1854–⁠61Clara and the First Piano Concerto

Schumann attempted suicide in February 1854 and entered the asylum at Endenich. Brahms went to help Clara with the children and the household’s practical affairs while she continued earning money at the piano. Robert died in 1856. Brahms wrote to Clara with an intensity he seldom showed in public; their friendship lasted more than forty years. She read his drafts, performed his music and told him what she thought of it. She did not always approve.

The First Piano Concerto began as music for two pianos, passed through an attempt at a symphony and was completed in D minor with a long, turbulent orchestral opening. The piano enters quietly. Brahms played the première in Hanover in January 1859, with Joachim conducting. At Leipzig five days later he was hissed. For much of the long first movement the soloist plays within the orchestral texture, and several of the hardest passages are not written for display.

He also worked with singers at the Detmold court and with a women’s choir in Hamburg. In the Serenades and the First String Sextet he tried out orchestral and chamber combinations. In 1861 he took an aria from Handel’s B♭ keyboard suite and wrote twenty-five variations and a fugue on it. The variations turn the theme into a march, an ornamented line and a heavy chordal statement, and the pianist’s figuration changes from one variation to the next.

Clara’s objectionThe F minor Piano Quintet began as a string quintet, then became a sonata for two pianos. Clara admired the music but thought the pianos lost too many of its ideas. Brahms tried again, combining piano and string quartet.

Brahms revised his work with the help of friends. Joachim tried Brahms’s string parts; the surgeon and amateur musician Theodor Billroth read his chamber music; Clara played through new work. The F minor Quintet reached its final combination in 1864 after advice from Clara, Joachim and Hermann Levi. Brahms destroyed many discarded scores. He let the two-piano version of the quintet, one of the alternatives he considered, survive.

ViennaVienna posts and Ein deutsches Requiem

He first visited Vienna in 1862 and made it his principal home after hopes of a leading appointment in Hamburg came to nothing. He directed the Singakademie in 1863–⁠64 and the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde’s concerts from 1872 to 1875. Publishing and performing eventually paid well enough for him to give up regular conducting. He collected manuscripts and early editions, studied Renaissance music and edited older works.

Critics cast him as Wagner’s opposite. In 1860 Brahms had helped Joachim draft a declaration against the New German School. It reached print before they had gathered the intended signatures, and it was ridiculed. After that he kept out of such campaigns. He knew and admired Wagner’s music, though he never wrote an opera.

His mother died in 1865, while Ein deutsches Requiem was taking shape. Brahms chose his text from Luther’s Bible, beginning with a blessing for those who mourn and ending with one for the dead. Before the Bremen performance, the conductor Carl Reinthaler objected that the text omitted Christ’s redemptive death. On 9 October 1867 Brahms replied: “I would gladly leave out ‘German’ too and simply put ‘human’.” He did not change the text.

On Good Friday 1868 Brahms conducted six movements in Bremen Cathedral, with Clara in the audience. The success made his name far beyond the circle that already knew his chamber music. He added a movement for soprano and chorus, whose words promise comfort like a mother’s, and the complete seven-movement work was heard in Leipzig in 1869. The choral writing runs from Denn alles Fleisch to Wie lieblich sind deine Wohnungen.

1876–⁠85Four symphonies

The First Symphony reached Karlsruhe in November 1876. It begins with timpani beating beneath lines that move chromatically in opposite directions. In the finale, a horn call enters over the C minor music; Brahms had sent that tune to Clara as a birthday greeting from the Alps in 1868. A brass chorale follows, then the C major melody that resembles Beethoven’s Ninth. He began the Second Symphony the next summer at Pörtschach, beside the Wörthersee.

A publisher’s warningOn 22 November 1877 Brahms told Fritz Simrock that the Second Symphony was unbearably melancholy: “The score must appear with a mourning border.” The symphony is in D major and ends with the brass playing loud.

Early in the Second’s first movement, trombones and tuba enter, and the second theme, which has the shape of a lullaby, arrives with minor-key harmony beneath it. Brahms gives the tune a stable melodic line and puts unsettled harmony in the accompaniment. In the Violin Concerto of 1878, written with Joachim’s advice, he gives the slow movement’s first melody to an oboe. The Second Piano Concerto, completed in 1881, has four movements and another borrowed soloist. A solo cello plays the opening of the slow movement while the piano is silent.

F–A–FThe motto is traditionally read as frei aber froh, “free but happy”, answering Joachim’s frei aber einsam, “free but lonely”. At the start of the Third Symphony Brahms lowers the middle note to A♭, putting a minor third inside F major.

All four movements of the Third Symphony, completed in 1883, end quietly. Near the end of the finale the first movement’s opening returns. The Fourth was written in 1884–⁠85. Its first tune is made from a chain of thirds, some inverted as rising sixths. For the finale Brahms took an eight-bar progression from the closing chorus of Bach’s cantata Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich, BWV 150, altered it and built a sequence of variations on it. The last pages are in E minor.

His independence now allowed him to help others. Through the Austrian state stipend scheme he encountered Dvořák’s scores, supported the younger man and recommended him to Simrock. His own Hungarian Dances, many based on tunes already circulating, sold far beyond the audiences for his symphonies.

1891–⁠97Mühlfeld and the late works

Brahms had considered retiring from composition in 1890. After hearing Richard Mühlfeld of the Meiningen orchestra he wrote a Clarinet Trio and a Clarinet Quintet in 1891, then two sonatas in 1894. In the slow movement of the quintet the clarinet plays an elaborate solo passage over muted strings; at the end of the finale the music with which the work began returns. The piano collections Opp. 116–⁠119 belong to the same late period. They are short pieces, with restless rhythms and melodies in the middle voices.

Clara died in May 1896. Brahms had completed his Four Serious Songs that spring, setting biblical words about death and love, and his final organ preludes were based on Lutheran chorales. In March 1897 he attended a performance of the Fourth Symphony in Vienna. He died on 3 April. The books and scores in his library went to musicians.

The sound

𝄋A tune built from thirds

The Fourth Symphony opens B–G, then E–C, falling thirds separated by upward leaps. The same interval supplies the notes for the rest of the melody. Brahms lengthened the thirds, changed their rhythm or moved them into another part, and the connection to the opening remains audible. Schoenberg called this process developing variation, in which a theme is already being developed during its first statement.

𝅘𝅥Placement of the downbeat

Brahms can set three beats against two, or carry a melody across a bar line while the accompaniment keeps the metre underneath. The opening horn phrase of the Second Symphony obscures where a bar begins. A hemiola works in a related way, with two bars of three crotchets regrouped as three groups of two.

𝄢The bass line

In the finale of the Fourth Symphony an eight-bar progression returns again and again while the orchestra changes what happens around it. At times the tune is plainly audible; at others a flute solo draws attention away from the progression beneath. The Handel Variations for piano also keep the bass in view. Even where the surface becomes elaborate, much of the original theme remains beneath it.

♭Major and minor

The Third Symphony opens in F major with the motto F–A♭–F, whose middle note comes from the minor scale. This clash between major and minor recurs in Brahms. In the Second Symphony, the second theme, which resembles a lullaby, begins in F♯ minor. The melody is easy to sing, but the accompanying chords are not settled.

𝄞Another player’s solo

A horn opens the Second Piano Concerto, and the piano answers. In its slow movement a solo cello plays the first long melody. In the Adagio of the Violin Concerto an oboe plays the melody before the solo violin enters. In several of his concertos Brahms wrote passages in which the soloist accompanies other instruments.

𝄆Returning openings

The Clarinet Quintet ends with a theme and five variations. Near the end, music from the first movement returns. The Third Symphony also returns to its opening material in its final pages, played softly.

Sister arts

After Feuerbach died in 1880, Brahms wrote Nänie in his memory and dedicated it to the painter’s stepmother Henriette. Schiller’s poem mourns figures from Greek myth, a world Feuerbach painted many times. The poem names grieving mothers and lost children, and this painting shows a woman on the shore.

Klinger kept a piano in his studio and gave his print cycles opus numbers, as composers numbered their works. His Brahmsphantasie sets prints alongside five songs and the Schicksalslied, and Brahms received the finished book in 1894. Its images depict dreams and encounters drawn from the music, among them this meeting with Death. Wesleyan’s account reproduces the book and traces the friendship between the two artists.

Friedrich Schiller

Nänie

1800; set by Brahms in 1880–⁠81
GermanAuch das Schöne muß sterben! Das Menschen und Götter bezwinget, Nicht die eherne Brust rührt es des stygischen Zeus. Einmal nur erweichte die Liebe den Schattenbeherrscher, Und an der Schwelle noch, streng, rief er zurück sein Geschenk. Nicht stillt Aphrodite dem schönen Knaben die Wunde, Die in den zierlichen Leib grausam der Eber geritzt. Nicht errettet den göttlichen Held die unsterbliche Mutter, Wann er, am skäischen Tor fallend, sein Schicksal erfüllt. Aber sie steigt aus dem Meer mit allen Töchtern des Nereus, Und die Klage hebt an um den verherrlichten Sohn. Siehe! Da weinen die Götter, es weinen die Göttinnen alle, Daß das Schöne vergeht, daß das Vollkommene stirbt. Auch ein Klaglied zu sein im Mund der Geliebten, ist herrlich, Denn das Gemeine geht klanglos zum Orkus hinab.
Our translationEven beauty must die! What overcomes humans and gods cannot move the bronze breast of the Zeus beneath the earth. Only once did love soften the ruler of shadows, and sternly, even at the threshold, he recalled his gift. Aphrodite cannot close the beautiful boy’s wound, cruelly torn in his delicate body by the boar. The immortal mother cannot save the godlike hero when, falling at the Scaean gate, he fulfils his fate. But she rises from the sea with all the daughters of Nereus, and the lament begins for her glorious son. Look: the gods weep, and all the goddesses weep, because beauty passes away, because perfection dies. Even to be a lament in the mouths of loved ones is splendid, for the common thing descends to Orcus without a sound.

Eurydice is recalled to the underworld; Adonis dies of his wound; Achilles’ mother cannot save him. Brahms began this lament with a long oboe solo in D major, after which the choral voices enter in imitation. At the end he returned to Schiller’s penultimate line, which says that even to survive as a song of mourning is splendid. German text.

Friedrich Hölderlin

Hyperions Schicksalslied — first stanza

1799; set by Brahms in 1868–⁠71
GermanIhr wandelt droben im Licht Auf weichem Boden, selige Genien! Glänzende Götterlüfte Rühren euch leicht, Wie die Finger der Künstlerin Heilige Saiten.
Our translationYou walk above in the light on gentle ground, blessed spirits. Shining breezes of the gods touch you lightly, as a woman musician’s fingers touch sacred strings.

Brahms found the poem in his friend Albert Dietrich’s house in 1868 and began sketching during a visit to Wilhelmshaven. In the poem the gods live in light, and the mortals who appear later fall blindly into suffering. His setting moves from E♭ major into C minor. After the singers have finished, the orchestra plays the music of the opening again, in C major. He had thought of repeating the opening words instead; Hermann Levi talked him out of it.

Passed toAntonín DvořákArnold SchoenbergEdward ElgarMax Reger

Dvořák received encouragement from Brahms and an introduction to Simrock. Schoenberg, in Brahms the Progressive, explained how in Brahms a phrase can keep changing while its smallest intervals remain recognisable. Reger took up Brahms’s use of variation and counterpoint in piano and organ scores of greater density.
