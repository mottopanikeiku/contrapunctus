Life

In the autumn of 1853, a twenty-year-old pianist from Hamburg arrived at the Schumanns’ home in Düsseldorf with an introduction from Joseph Joachim. Johannes Brahms played his own music. Robert Schumann heard a composer of the future and announced him that October in an article called Neue Bahnen — New Paths. Publishers became interested. Brahms now had to live up to a public prophecy before most of the public had heard a note. The young man’s piano sonatas were enormous; the symphonies Schumann expected would take much longer.

Brahms’s father Johann Jakob earned his living playing instruments including the horn and double bass. His mother, Christiane Nissen, was a seamstress. Born in Hamburg in 1833, he studied piano with Otto Cossel and then piano and composition with Eduard Marxsen, who grounded him in Bach, Mozart and Beethoven. He played, arranged and accompanied for money. A tour with the Hungarian violinist Ede Reményi in 1853 brought him to Joachim, and Joachim sent him on to Düsseldorf.

1854–61The people who read the scores

Schumann attempted suicide in February 1854 and entered the asylum at Endenich. Brahms went to help Clara with the children and the household’s practical affairs while she continued earning money at the piano. Robert died in 1856. Brahms’s letters to Clara speak with an intensity he seldom allowed into public conversation; their friendship lasted more than forty years. She read his drafts, performed his music and told him what she thought of it. Her approval was precious, and never automatic.

The First Piano Concerto began as music for two pianos, passed through an attempt at a symphony and emerged in D minor with a long, turbulent orchestral opening. When the piano finally enters, it is quiet. Brahms played the première in Hanover in January 1859, with Joachim conducting. At Leipzig five days later he was hissed. The concerto asks its soloist to spend much of a huge first movement inside the orchestra’s struggle; the passages that look most difficult do not always advertise the difficulty.

He also worked with singers at the Detmold court and with a women’s choir in Hamburg. The Serenades and the First String Sextet gave him space to try out orchestral and chamber combinations. In 1861 he took an aria from Handel’s B♭ keyboard suite and wrote twenty-five variations and a fugue. The variations can turn the little theme into a march, a delicate ornamented line or a weighty chordal statement. A pianist’s hands have to change their manner of speaking almost every page.

Clara’s objectionThe F minor Piano Quintet began as a string quintet, then became a sonata for two pianos. Clara admired the music but thought the pianos lost too many of its ideas. Brahms tried again, combining piano and string quartet.

Rewriting was part of friendship. Joachim tried Brahms’s string parts; the surgeon and amateur musician Theodor Billroth read his chamber music; Clara played through new work. The F minor Quintet reached its final combination in 1864 after advice from Clara, Joachim and Hermann Levi. Brahms destroyed many discarded scores. He let the two-piano version of the quintet survive, so we can still hear one of the alternatives he considered.

ViennaA home, a choir and a Requiem

He first visited Vienna in 1862 and made it his principal home after hopes of a leading appointment in Hamburg came to nothing. He directed the Singakademie in 1863–64 and the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde’s concerts from 1872 to 1875. Publishing and performing eventually paid well enough for him to give up regular conducting. He collected manuscripts and early editions, studied Renaissance music and edited older works. His library was a working musician’s supply cupboard.

Critics cast him as Wagner’s opposite. Brahms had helped Joachim draft a declaration against the New German School in 1860; it reached print before they had gathered the intended signatures and was ridiculed. He subsequently kept out of such campaigns. He knew and admired Wagner’s music, though he never wrote an opera. The arguments in the newspapers were tidier than the contents of his bookshelves.

His mother died in 1865, while Ein deutsches Requiem was taking shape. Brahms chose his text from Luther’s Bible, beginning with a blessing for those who mourn and ending with one for the dead. Before the Bremen performance, the conductor Carl Reinthaler objected that the text omitted Christ’s redemptive death. On 9 October 1867 Brahms replied: “I would gladly leave out ‘German’ too and simply put ‘human’.” He kept his selection of words.

On Good Friday 1868 Brahms conducted six movements in Bremen Cathedral, with Clara among the audience. The success established him far beyond the circle that already knew his chamber music. He added a movement for soprano and chorus, whose words promise comfort like a mother’s, and the complete seven-movement work was heard in Leipzig in 1869. The singers move from the heavy funeral tread of Denn alles Fleisch to the easy, almost folk-like melody of Wie lieblich sind deine Wohnungen.

1876–85Four symphonies

The First Symphony finally reached Karlsruhe in November 1876. It begins with timpani beating beneath lines that push in opposite chromatic directions. In the finale, a horn call breaks through the C minor darkness; Brahms had sent that tune to Clara as a birthday greeting from the Alps in 1868. A brass chorale follows, then the great C major melody that recalls Beethoven’s Ninth. He began the Second Symphony the next summer at Pörtschach, beside the Wörthersee.

A publisher’s warningOn 22 November 1877 Brahms told Fritz Simrock that the Second Symphony was unbearably melancholy: “The score must appear with a mourning border.” Simrock was about to receive a symphony in D major with an exuberant brass ending.

The joke has a dark corner. Early in the Second’s first movement, trombones and tuba intrude into the gentle landscape, and even the lullaby-like second theme arrives with minor-key harmony beneath it. Brahms makes the tune sound comfortable while the accompaniment worries. In the Violin Concerto of 1878, written with Joachim’s advice, he gives the slow movement’s first melody to an oboe. The Second Piano Concerto, completed in 1881, has four movements and another borrowed soloist: a cello sings the opening of the slow movement while the pianist listens.

F–A–FThe motto is traditionally read as frei aber froh, “free but happy”, answering Joachim’s frei aber einsam, “free but lonely”. At the start of the Third Symphony Brahms lowers the middle note to A♭, putting a minor third inside F major.

All four movements of the Third Symphony, completed in 1883, end quietly. The finale lets its anger subside until the first movement’s opening returns. The Fourth, written in 1884–85, is less forgiving. Its first tune is made from a chain of thirds, some turned upside down into rising sixths. For the finale Brahms took an eight-bar progression from the closing chorus of Bach’s cantata Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich, BWV 150, altered it and built a sequence of variations. The last pages drive the music into E minor with nothing held back.

His independence now allowed him to help others. Through the Austrian state stipend scheme he encountered Dvořák’s scores, supported the younger man and recommended him to Simrock. Brahms knew the value of a publisher who would put music into people’s hands. His own Hungarian Dances, many based on tunes already circulating, sold far beyond the audiences for his symphonies.

1891–97A clarinettist changes his mind

Brahms had considered retiring from composition in 1890. Hearing Richard Mühlfeld of the Meiningen orchestra brought him back: a Clarinet Trio and Quintet in 1891, then two sonatas in 1894. In the quintet’s slow movement the clarinet’s elaborate outburst rises over muted strings; the finale eventually returns to the music with which the whole work began. The piano collections Opp. 116–119 appeared in the same late flowering, packing restless rhythms and middle-voice melodies into short pieces.

Clara died in May 1896. Brahms had completed his Four Serious Songs that spring, setting biblical words about death and love, and his final organ preludes returned to Lutheran chorales. In March 1897 he attended a Vienna performance of the Fourth Symphony. He died on 3 April. The books and scores in his library went to musicians who would go on reading them.

The Sound

𝄋A tune made of thirds

The Fourth Symphony opens B–G, then E–C: falling thirds, separated by upward leaps. Through the rest of the melody, the same interval keeps supplying the notes. Brahms can lengthen them, change the rhythm or move them into another part without losing the connection. Schoenberg called this process developing variation. A theme is already developing while we are first hearing it.

𝅘𝅥Where is the downbeat?

Brahms can make three beats lean against two, or carry a melody across a bar line while the accompaniment keeps counting underneath. The opening horn phrase of the Second Symphony obscures where a bar begins. A hemiola makes a related trick: two bars of three crotchets become three groups of two. The pleasure lies in feeling both at once.

𝄢Listening to the bass

In the Fourth Symphony’s finale, an eight-bar progression keeps returning while the orchestra changes what happens around it. Sometimes the tune is obvious; sometimes a flute solo draws attention away from the underpinning. The Handel Variations for piano also keep the bass in view. Even when the surface grows elaborate, much of the original remains beneath it.

♭Major with a shadow

The Third Symphony announces F major with F–A♭–F: its middle note comes from the minor scale. That disagreement keeps returning. In the Second Symphony, the lullaby-like second theme sounds warm and familiar, yet begins in F♯ minor. Brahms gives a listener something reassuring to sing while the accompanying chords make the reassurance difficult to trust completely.

𝄞Somebody else’s solo

A horn opens the Second Piano Concerto, and the piano answers. In its slow movement a cello gets the first long melody. The Violin Concerto lets an oboe sing before the solo violin enters its Adagio. Brahms repeatedly asks a virtuoso to listen, join in and accompany; the expensive guest on the platform has to become a chamber musician.

𝄆Finding the way home

The Clarinet Quintet finishes with a theme and five variations. Near the end, the first movement’s music comes back, heard through everything that has happened since. The Third Symphony also returns to its beginning in its final pages, softly. Brahms can make a familiar phrase carry the ending without swelling it into a victory song or introducing another tune.

Sister Arts

After Feuerbach died in 1880, Brahms wrote Nänie in his memory and dedicated it to the painter’s stepmother Henriette. Schiller’s poem mourns figures from Greek myth, a world Feuerbach repeatedly painted. This woman on the shore belongs beside the music’s grieving mothers and lost children; the dedication brought the lament home to the painter’s own family.

Klinger kept a piano in his studio and numbered his print cycles like musical works. His Brahmsphantasie joins prints to five songs and the Schicksalslied; Brahms received the finished book in 1894. Its figures turn the music into dreams and encounters, including this meeting with Death. Wesleyan’s account reproduces the book and traces the artists’ friendship.

Friedrich Schiller

Nänie

1800; set by Brahms in 1880–81
GermanAuch das Schöne muß sterben! Das Menschen und Götter bezwinget, Nicht die eherne Brust rührt es des stygischen Zeus. Einmal nur erweichte die Liebe den Schattenbeherrscher, Und an der Schwelle noch, streng, rief er zurück sein Geschenk. Nicht stillt Aphrodite dem schönen Knaben die Wunde, Die in den zierlichen Leib grausam der Eber geritzt. Nicht errettet den göttlichen Held die unsterbliche Mutter, Wann er, am skäischen Tor fallend, sein Schicksal erfüllt. Aber sie steigt aus dem Meer mit allen Töchtern des Nereus, Und die Klage hebt an um den verherrlichten Sohn. Siehe! Da weinen die Götter, es weinen die Göttinnen alle, Daß das Schöne vergeht, daß das Vollkommene stirbt. Auch ein Klaglied zu sein im Mund der Geliebten, ist herrlich, Denn das Gemeine geht klanglos zum Orkus hinab.
Our translationEven beauty must die! What overcomes humans and gods cannot move the bronze breast of the Zeus beneath the earth. Only once did love soften the ruler of shadows, and sternly, even at the threshold, he recalled his gift. Aphrodite cannot close the beautiful boy’s wound, cruelly torn in his delicate body by the boar. The immortal mother cannot save the godlike hero when, falling at the Scaean gate, he fulfils his fate. But she rises from the sea with all the daughters of Nereus, and the lament begins for her glorious son. Look: the gods weep, and all the goddesses weep, because beauty passes away, because perfection dies. Even to be a lament in the mouths of loved ones is splendid, for the common thing descends to Orcus without a sound.

Eurydice is recalled to the underworld; Adonis dies of his wound; Achilles’ mother cannot save him. Brahms begins this lament with a long oboe solo in D major, then lets the choral voices enter in imitation. At the end he returns to Schiller’s penultimate line: even to survive as a song of mourning is splendid. German text.

Friedrich Hölderlin

Hyperions Schicksalslied — first stanza

1799; set by Brahms in 1868–71
GermanIhr wandelt droben im Licht Auf weichem Boden, selige Genien! Glänzende Götterlüfte Rühren euch leicht, Wie die Finger der Künstlerin Heilige Saiten.
Our translationYou walk above in the light on gentle ground, blessed spirits. Shining breezes of the gods touch you lightly, as a woman musician’s fingers touch sacred strings.

Brahms found the poem in his friend Albert Dietrich’s house in 1868 and began sketching during a visit to Wilhelmshaven. Its gods live in light; the mortals who appear later fall blindly into suffering. His setting moves from E♭ major into C minor, then lets the orchestra recall the opening in C major after the singers have finished. He had thought of repeating the opening words instead; Hermann Levi talked him out of it.

Passed toAntonín DvořákArnold SchoenbergEdward ElgarMax Reger

Dvořák received something immediately useful from Brahms: encouragement and an introduction to Simrock. Schoenberg found a compositional lesson, explaining in Brahms the Progressive how a phrase can keep changing while its smallest intervals remain recognisable. Reger carried Brahms’s love of variation and counterpoint into still denser piano and organ scores.
