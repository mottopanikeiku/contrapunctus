Johannes Brahms

Born 7 May 1833, Hamburg
Died 3 April 1897, Vienna

A composer who made a small interval generate a large form, and let conflicting rhythms keep a familiar melody unsettled.

Life

Brahms was born in Hamburg, the son of a professional musician, Johann Jakob Brahms, who played several instruments, and Christiane Nissen, a seamstress. His father supplied an introduction to practical music-making; Otto Friedrich Willibald Cossel and Eduard Marxsen gave him systematic piano and composition lessons. The young Brahms played for income and learned the trade from inside, accompanying, arranging and performing. Marxsen’s teaching connected keyboard technique with the study of Bach, Mozart and Beethoven. Those composers supplied working materials: Brahms’s later scores are full of old procedures subjected to new rhythmic pressure.

In 1853 he toured with the Hungarian violinist Ede Reményi and met Joseph Joachim, who became a lifelong collaborator. Joachim sent him to Robert and Clara Schumann in Düsseldorf. Robert’s article “Neue Bahnen”, published that October, announced the twenty-year-old as a major new figure before his music was widely available. The endorsement opened publishers’ doors and imposed an awkward expectation of scale. His early piano sonatas, already orchestral in weight and contrast, could be treated as promises of symphonies he had not yet written. Brahms responded by publishing selectively and destroying much of the work he did not wish to preserve.

The Schumann householdFriendship under pressure

Schumann attempted suicide in February 1854 and entered the asylum at Endenich, where he died in 1856. Brahms helped Clara with practical affairs and her children while she continued her performing career. His letters record intense attachment; their friendship endured through disagreements about music and conduct for more than forty years. Clara was a professional reader of his scores, a pianist who tested their demands, and a critic whose approval mattered. The relationship belongs to the making of the music as well as to the story of its composer.

The First Piano Concerto grew out of experiments with a sonata for two pianos and a projected symphony. Its D minor opening gives the orchestra a long, turbulent argument before the soloist enters quietly. Brahms played the premiere in Hanover in January 1859; the Leipzig performance shortly afterwards met a hostile reception. The problem for listeners was partly generic: the piano participates in the orchestral conflict, in a first movement closer in scale and seriousness to a symphony than to a brilliant concert piece.

Developing variationSchoenberg’s later term describes a process already present in Brahms: each new phrase changes an interval or rhythm from the preceding one, so development begins inside the theme itself. The distinction between “theme” and “development section” becomes less absolute.

Work with choirs in Hamburg and at the Detmold court provided a contrasting education. Brahms learnt how a melodic line sits in a voice, how an inner part can obstruct a cadence and how limited forces make a texture intelligible. The Serenades, the first string sextet and the Handel Variations belong to this period of experiment. In the last work a compact Baroque theme supports twenty-five variations and a fugue; the bass and harmonic structure matter as much as the tune. The music can sound improvisatory while its proportions remain tightly controlled.

ViennaA modern composer with a historical library

Brahms first visited Vienna in 1862 and made it his principal home. A hoped-for leading appointment in Hamburg went elsewhere. In Vienna he directed the Singakademie for one season, later conducting the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde’s concerts from 1872 to 1875, but he avoided a permanent institutional career once publishing and performance income made independence possible. He collected manuscripts and early editions, studied Renaissance and Baroque music, and edited older repertory. This historical curiosity reached well beyond Beethoven. The choral writing can draw on imitative motet textures, while a piano miniature may compress an entire movement into the handling of one bass pattern.

Public controversy made him the supposed opposite of Wagner and Liszt. In 1860 he had joined Joachim and others in a declaration against claims made on behalf of the New German School; its premature appearance in print exposed the signatories to ridicule. The binary label remained convenient for critics. Brahms knew Wagner’s scores and could admire music outside his own methods. Poetry, memory and dramatic pacing animate his instrumental forms, with those associations entering the structure on his own terms.

Ein deutsches Requiem established his wider reputation. His mother died in 1865, during the work’s gestation. Brahms assembled German biblical passages for its text. The opening addresses those who mourn; the ending blesses the dead. A six-movement performance in Bremen on Good Friday 1868 became a decisive success, and a newly added soprano movement completed the familiar seven-part design. The full version was performed in Leipzig in 1869. Its architecture balances consolation against fugues in which the collective voice gains contrapuntal force.

Four symphoniesDifferent answers to the same inheritance

The First Symphony reached its premiere in Karlsruhe in 1876 after a long gestation. The slow introduction presses opposing chromatic lines against repeated timpani strokes; the finale moves from C minor disturbance towards C major. Its broad concluding theme invited comparison with Beethoven’s Ninth, but the route to it is Brahms’s own: a horn call and a brass chorale establish different kinds of memory before the principal Allegro begins. The Second followed in 1877, much of it written at Pörtschach. Its D major surface contains low brass, dark turns of harmony and rhythmic disturbances that complicate the easy label “pastoral”.

The Violin Concerto was written for Joachim in 1878, with his advice on the solo part. Its double-stopping and sustained phrases require both physical weight and chamber-musical attention to the orchestra. The Second Piano Concerto, completed in 1881, adds a scherzo to make four movements and gives the slow movement’s opening melody to a solo cello. The pianist is again one participant in a larger ensemble. Brahms composed against the habits of public virtuosity: entrances may be quiet, accompaniments difficult and the most memorable melody assigned to somebody else.

The F–A–F mottoThe letters are traditionally read as frei aber froh, “free but happy”, answering Joachim’s F–A–E, frei aber einsam. The Third Symphony’s opening actually inflects the middle note to A♭: the major-key declaration contains a minor-coloured contradiction.

The Third Symphony, completed in 1883, ends all four movements quietly. Its F–A♭–F opening and recurring tension between F major and F minor make the final return to major a withdrawal rather than a triumphal march. The Fourth, written in 1884–85, begins with a chain of thirds distributed across falling thirds and rising sixths. Its finale takes an eight-bar progression related to the closing chorus of Bach’s cantata Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich, BWV 150, and subjects it to continuous variation. A Baroque ground-bass procedure becomes a symphonic ending without the reassurance of a major-key conversion.

Friends and performers were essential to this independence. The surgeon Theodor Billroth read chamber scores; Joachim tested string writing; Clara heard and criticised new work. Brahms also helped Dvořák, whose scores he encountered through the Austrian state stipend system, and recommended him to the publisher Simrock. Their music shares resources in variation, rhythm and chamber texture while drawing on different melodic idioms. Brahms’s own Hungarian Dances circulated widely, drawing many of their melodies from existing popular repertory.

After retirementThe clarinet and the short piece

In 1890 he contemplated retirement from composition. Hearing Richard Mühlfeld, the clarinettist of the Meiningen orchestra, reversed the decision: the Clarinet Trio and Quintet followed in 1891, the two sonatas in 1894. The quintet’s finale varies a theme before recalling the first movement, bringing the whole work back into view. The piano pieces of Opp. 116–119 use similarly compressed means. An inner voice, an unexpected accent or a bass note held across a change of harmony can transform a repeated phrase. Quietness here exposes each compositional decision.

Clara died in May 1896. Brahms’s Four Serious Songs, completed that spring, set biblical reflections on mortality and love. His final chorale preludes for organ return to Lutheran hymn melodies and contrapuntal discipline. He died in Vienna in April 1897, after a last public appearance at a performance of his Fourth Symphony. In 1933 Schoenberg called him “Brahms the Progressive”, basing his argument on phrase construction. The apparent conservative had supplied a method by which a short motif could continually change without losing the thread of the argument.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Developing variation

Development begins within a theme. Its second phrase may alter the first phrase’s rhythm; its continuation may enlarge one interval or move the accent. In the Fourth Symphony, a chain of thirds is enough to generate both the opening melody and much of the ensuing argument. Schoenberg recognised this economy beneath the nineteenth-century surface.

Cross-rhythm

Two beats can be heard against three, or two bars of triple metre regrouped as three larger beats — a hemiola. Brahms also puts melodic accents away from the notated downbeat and allows accompaniment patterns to cross the bar line. The players must preserve the underlying pulse while making the competing grouping audible; simply slowing the displaced phrase removes the friction.

Bass and inner voices

A descending bass, a viola figure or the middle notes of a piano chord may retain the motif while the soprano line changes. The Piano Quintet and the late intermezzos require performers to distinguish several degrees of prominence within the texture.

Harmony and mode

Major and minor frequently share a tonic and contend inside the same phrase. The Third Symphony’s F–A♭–F gesture introduces that tension immediately. Modal turns, pedal notes and plagal relations connect the concert works with chorale and folk-song practices, while chromatic passing voices disturb apparently stable triads from within.

Orchestral register

Violas, cellos, horns and low woodwind give the middle register weight. Doublings can conceal the edge of a single instrument; exposed solos — the horn at the beginning of the Second Piano Concerto, the oboe in the Violin Concerto’s slow movement — emerge from that blended texture. The scoring asks for balance and articulation.

Forms remembered

Sonata, variation, fugue and passacaglia remain audible frameworks. Brahms alters their internal proportions and joins their functions: the Fourth’s finale combines recurring eight-bar units with a larger sequence of contrasted sections. In the Clarinet Quintet, the last variation leads back to the work’s opening, making cyclic recollection part of a formally complete ending.

Sister arts

Objects and texts, with the nature of each musical connection made explicit.

Shared context

Brahms composed Nänie, Op. 82, after the death of his friend Feuerbach in 1880 and dedicated it to the painter’s stepmother, Henriette. Its Schiller text invokes Greek myth and the death of beauty, a subject appropriate to Feuerbach’s repeated engagement with classical figures. The isolated Iphigenia provides a visual counterpart to that classicising language.

Documented encounter

Klinger’s Brahmsphantasie places prints alongside Brahms’s songs and the Schicksalslied, treating music and image as interacting parts of a book. Brahms received the completed work in 1894. This is a contemporary artist’s direct response to his music. The Met’s impression preserves the varied print techniques through which Klinger distinguishes line, darkness and atmospheric depth.

Documented useFriedrich Schiller

Nänie

1800; set by Brahms in 1880–81
GermanAuch das Schöne muß sterben! Das Menschen und Götter bezwinget, Nicht die eherne Brust rührt es des stygischen Zeus. Einmal nur erweichte die Liebe den Schattenbeherrscher, Und an der Schwelle noch, streng, rief er zurück sein Geschenk. Nicht stillt Aphrodite dem schönen Knaben die Wunde, Die in den zierlichen Leib grausam der Eber geritzt. Nicht errettet den göttlichen Held die unsterbliche Mutter, Wann er, am skäischen Tor fallend, sein Schicksal erfüllt. Aber sie steigt aus dem Meer mit allen Töchtern des Nereus, Und die Klage hebt an um den verherrlichten Sohn. Siehe! Da weinen die Götter, es weinen die Göttinnen alle, Daß das Schöne vergeht, daß das Vollkommene stirbt. Auch ein Klaglied zu sein im Mund der Geliebten, ist herrlich, Denn das Gemeine geht klanglos zum Orkus hinab.
English · Contrapunctus translationEven beauty must die! What overcomes humans and gods cannot move the bronze breast of the Zeus beneath the earth. Only once did love soften the ruler of shadows, and sternly, even at the threshold, he recalled his gift. Aphrodite cannot close the beautiful boy’s wound, cruelly torn in his delicate body by the boar. The immortal mother cannot save the godlike hero when, falling at the Scaean gate, he fulfils his fate. But she rises from the sea with all the daughters of Nereus, and the lament begins for her glorious son. Look: the gods weep, and all the goddesses weep, because beauty passes away, because perfection dies. Even to be a lament in the mouths of loved ones is splendid, for the common thing descends to Orcus without a sound.

Schiller’s elegiac couplets allude to Eurydice’s loss, Adonis’s wound and Achilles’s death. Brahms’s D major setting answers the opening declaration with flowing oboe writing and imitative choral entries. Near the end he returns to the penultimate line about survival in a lament: the music itself enacts that restricted form of remembrance. German text.

Documented useFriedrich Hölderlin

Hyperions Schicksalslied — first stanza

1799; set by Brahms in 1868–71
GermanIhr wandelt droben im Licht Auf weichem Boden, selige Genien! Glänzende Götterlüfte Rühren euch leicht, Wie die Finger der Künstlerin Heilige Saiten.
English · Contrapunctus translationYou walk above in the light on gentle ground, blessed spirits. Shining breezes of the gods touch you lightly, as a woman musician’s fingers touch sacred strings.

The opening stanza describes beings exempt from human suffering; the poem later drops into the blind, repeated falling of mortal lives. Brahms’s Schicksalslied, Op. 54, opposes a slow E♭ major opening to an agitated C minor section. Its wordless C major postlude returns to the opening music after the poem has ended, offering an instrumental response that Hölderlin’s final words do not supply.

Later connectionsAntonín DvořákArnold SchoenbergEdward ElgarMax Reger

Schoenberg’s “Brahms the Progressive” identifies a compositional inheritance beneath the stylistic opposition between Romanticism and modernism: musical ideas need not be repeated literally to remain connected. Dvořák learnt from Brahms’s instrumental forms and received practical help from him, but kept his own rhythmic and melodic vocabulary. Reger extended the contrapuntal density and the integration of chromatic harmony with older forms.
