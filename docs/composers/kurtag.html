György Kurtág

Born 19 February 1926, Lugoj, Romania

A composer of messages, memorials and interrupted sentences, who can make two notes carry the weight of a scene.

Life

Kurtág was born in 1926 in Lugoj, a town in the Banat which had passed from Hungary to Romania after the First World War. Hungarian, Romanian and German belonged to his early environment; later he would compose in all three languages and in several more. His piano teacher in Timișoara, Magda Kardos, taught him to coach younger pupils as well as to play. The close examination of somebody else’s phrase became a lifelong occupation: chamber-music teaching stood at the centre of his musical life alongside composition.

At the Budapest Academy’s entrance examinations in 1946 he met György Ligeti. They shared teachers and an admiration for Bartók, but eventually took different routes out of the restrictions of post-war Hungary. Kurtág studied composition with Sándor Veress and Ferenc Farkas, piano with Pál Kadosa and chamber music with Leó Weiner. He married the pianist Márta Kinsker in 1947. Her judgement, teaching and playing remained part of his working life until her death in 2019.

Paris, 1957–58Beginning again

Kurtág’s year in Paris followed the Hungarian uprising of 1956. He attended classes with Messiaen and Milhaud, studied Webern’s scores, and encountered a musical culture far less restricted than Budapest’s. The decisive assistance came from Marianne Stein, a psychologist who worked with artists. During a crisis in which composition had become almost impossible, her work with him helped reduce the task to elementary actions and relationships.

Opus 1Kurtág had composed before Paris. Calling the String Quartet of 1959 his Op. 1 marked a new beginning at thirty-three.

On his return he stopped in Cologne, where Ligeti introduced him to Stockhausen and the electronic-music studio. Back in Budapest, the String Quartet, Op. 1, organised six short movements from sharply contrasted intervals and attacks. A wind quintet, piano pieces and music for violin and cimbalom followed. The cimbalom — a struck Hungarian dulcimer — offered both precise attack and a decay that could be left exposed. Its sound would recur in vocal ensembles.

The Sayings of Péter Bornemisza, Op. 7, occupied him from 1963 to 1968. This large work for soprano and piano sets prose by a sixteenth-century Hungarian preacher, organised around confession, sin, death and spring. The piano part demands an independent virtuosity of attack, register and sharply broken continuity. The vocal writing has to make the consonants and stresses of Hungarian intelligible through difficult intervals. Brevity at the level of the gesture can support a work lasting most of a concert half.

GamesThe whole keyboard first

In 1973 the piano teacher Marianne Teöke prompted the project that became Játékok, or Games. It begins from a child’s physical curiosity about the keyboard. Glissandos, clusters struck with a palm or forearm, and leaps across the instrument can precede fluent note-reading. Graphic signs indicate the kind of action; conventional notes and detailed expressive markings refine it. The physical size of a gesture need not match the loudness or duration of the resulting sound.

The collection grew beyond its pedagogical starting point into a record of friendships, birthdays and deaths. Some pieces are homages to other composers; others preserve a gesture associated with a particular person. György and Márta performed selections beside his four-hand transcriptions of Bach. Their shared keyboard made ensemble balance visible: two players listening to the same decay, taking over a line, leaving a space for the other’s entrance. The small upright piano used in many of their performances also discouraged treating every cluster as a concert-grand spectacle.

A fragment is completeA short Kurtág piece may end before a phrase reaches a conventional cadence. Each “fragment” has a precisely specified form encompassing its title, last articulation and following silence.

Words under pressureTroussova and Kafka

International recognition widened after the Paris premiere of Messages of the Late Miss R. V. Troussova, Op. 17, in 1981. Its twenty-one Russian poems by Rimma Dalos make a sequence of compressed emotional situations for soprano and chamber ensemble. A single instrument can expose, interrupt or contradict the vocal line. The difference between a plucked attack, a sustained wind note and the singer’s consonant helps determine what the sentence means.

Kafka-Fragmente, Op. 24, composed in 1985–87, removes the ensemble altogether. Soprano and violin carry forty fragments from Kafka’s diaries, notebooks and letters. Some last only seconds; others develop into substantial scenes. The violin can shadow speech, oppose it, or become a second speaker. Kurtág sets texts about walking, paths, confinement and uncertain arrival without making them into a continuous plot. Changes of tempo, register and bodily effort provide the continuity that a conventional narrative would otherwise supply.

Memorial composition gave him another way to link separate pieces. The fifteen movements of Officium breve, Op. 28, for string quartet, recall the Hungarian composer Endre Szervánszky and draw on both Szervánszky and Webern. A borrowed phrase can emerge from a sparse texture, be cut short, or acquire a new force through the silence around it. The title’s short office is a sequence of acts of remembrance.

Larger stagesFrom Stele to Beckett

A residency with the Berlin Philharmonic in the 1990s produced Stele, Op. 33, premiered under Claudio Abbado in 1994. The orchestra enlarges the scale of Kurtág’s attacks and suspended sonorities. The three movements place forceful public utterance beside music that repeatedly seems to have difficulty continuing. In …concertante…, Op. 42, completed in 2003, violin and viola soloists carry that close-grained exchange into another large orchestral setting.

Kurtág had encountered Samuel Beckett’s theatre during the Paris period, but his first opera arrived only in 2018, at La Scala in Milan. Fin de partie, based on Beckett’s French Endgame, was staged by Pierre Audi and conducted by Markus Stenz. Four singers inhabit an enclosed situation in which speech, silence and the effort of repetition matter more than external action. The composer of miniature messages had made a full-length opera in which the individual syllable retained its weight. He celebrated his hundredth birthday in February 2026.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Gesture

A leap, a palm cluster, a two-note slur or a suddenly arrested phrase can be a complete musical unit. Játékok distinguishes the physical action from the exact pitches it produces. Its graphic notation requires control of the speed of the motion, the attack and the listening after the sound.

Intervals

Semitones, open intervals and abrupt changes of register receive the attention that a longer tonal movement might give to themes. A close interval can return several octaves away and remain recognisable without sounding equivalent. In the chamber works, which instrument owns each note alters the balance of the interval.

Speech

Hungarian, Russian, German and French demand different consonants, stresses and phrase lengths. Kurtág lets those differences shape his vocal music. In Kafka-Fragmente, the violin supplies another mode of utterance, from sustained tone to a broken or sharply articulated response.

Silence

Rests separate gestures, expose decay and determine whether a reply arrives promptly or seems delayed. A very short movement may require more concentration after its final attack than during it. Silence is measured and performed.

Memory & form

Homages, dedications and quotations supply relationships between otherwise separate miniatures. Hommage à R. Sch. takes the clarinet, viola and piano of Schumann’s Märchenerzählungen; Officium breve recalls both Szervánszky and Webern. Framing and interruption make the borrowed material sound afresh.

Sister arts

Objects and texts, with the nature of each musical connection made explicit.

Shared context

Kafka’s diaries, letters and notebooks supply the forty texts of Kafka-Fragmente. This late portrait belongs to the period before his death in 1924. Kurtág’s work repeatedly changes the scale at which the writer is heard: an aphorism lasting seconds may stand beside a much longer scene for the same soprano and violin.

Documented useFranz Kafka

Die Guten gehn im gleichen Schritt

Early twentieth century; set by Kurtág 1985–87
GermanDie Guten gehn im gleichen Schritt. Ohne von ihnen zu wissen, tanzen die andern um sie die Tänze der Zeit.
English · Contrapunctus translationThe good walk in the same step. Without knowing of them, the others dance the dances of the time around them.

The opening text of Kafka-Fragmente. Its contrast is already musical: common step against surrounding dances. Kurtág gives the soprano and violin distinct rhythmic behaviour. Line breaks make Kafka’s prose legible here.

Documented useFriedrich Hölderlin

An Zimmern

Before 1812
GermanDie Linien des Lebens sind verschieden, Wie Wege sind, und wie der Berge Grenzen. Was hier wir sind, kann dort ein Gott ergänzen Mit Harmonien und ewigem Lohn und Frieden.
English · Contrapunctus translationThe lines of life are different, as paths are, and the boundaries of mountains. What we are here, a god may complete there with harmonies and everlasting reward and peace.

Ernst Zimmer, the carpenter whose family cared for Hölderlin in Tübingen, transmitted these four lines in 1812. Kurtág sets them as the fourth of his Hölderlin-Gesänge, Op. 35a, for unaccompanied baritone. The word “harmonies” is entrusted to one melodic line.

Lineage

Study, performance and later responses are different relationships—not a single teacher–pupil chain. Read the note for the connections.

Kurtág
Later connectionsChamber-music teachingContemporary miniature and memorial cycles

Bartók connects keyboard experiment with pedagogy; Webern offers a precedent for concentrated intervals and exposed attacks. Kurtág and Ligeti exchanged ideas throughout their lives while pursuing distinct styles: Ligeti often makes continuity from masses of activity, Kurtág from sharply individual gestures. Bach transcriptions remained part of Kurtág’s own concert programmes.
