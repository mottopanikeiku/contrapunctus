— Vaughan Williams to Sylvia Townsend Warner, about a fortnight before his death in August 1958, as she recorded it in a letter

Life

On 9 February 1875 Arthur Vaughan Williams, vicar of All Saints, Down Ampney, died after a brief illness. His third child, Ralph, born in the vicarage on 12 October 1872, was two. His widow Margaret, a Wedgwood, took the children to her family’s house at Leith Hill Place in Surrey. Aunt Sophy gave the boy piano lessons, and at six he wrote a piece called The Robin’s Nest. He went to Charterhouse in 1887 and to the Royal College of Music in 1890, where Hubert Parry taught him composition; in 1892 he went up to Trinity College, Cambridge, to read history as well as music.

The greatest influenceHe met Gustav Holst at the Royal College in 1895. Vaughan Williams later called him “the greatest influence on my music”. The friendship lasted until Holst’s death in 1934.

On 9 October 1897, three days before his twenty-fifth birthday, he married Adeline Fisher at All Saints, Hove. She was a cellist and pianist, and Virginia Woolf was her first cousin. After a brief spell of study with Max Bruch in Berlin he returned to his post as organist at St Barnabas, South Lambeth. He had held the post since 1895 and disliked it, and he gave it up in 1899.

1903–08A song in Essex, a lesson in Paris

In December 1903, at Ingrave in Essex, he took down Bushes and Briars from a village singer. It was the first of more than 800 songs and carols he collected over the next ten years in East Anglia, Sussex and Herefordshire, and its modal turns remained in his writing for the rest of his life. From 1904 to 1906 he was music editor of The English Hymnal. The work brought him into daily contact with Tudor psalm tunes, and he contributed four tunes of his own, among them Sine Nomine (“For all the saints”) and Down Ampney (“Come down, O Love Divine”), named for his birthplace.

Points of colourIn the winter of 1907–08 he spent about three months in Paris with Maurice Ravel, who began by asking for a minuet in the style of Mozart. Vaughan Williams said he came away able “to orchestrate in points of colour rather than in lines”, and with “a bad attack of French fever”, which produced the song cycle On Wenlock Edge, to poems by Housman.

1910Two premieres in five weeks

On the evening of 6 September 1910 some two thousand people crowded into Gloucester Cathedral for the Three Choirs Festival, most of them to hear Elgar conduct The Dream of Gerontius. First came a new piece for strings, conducted by its thirty-seven-year-old composer: the Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis. The cathedral’s organist, Herbert Brewer, called it “a queer, mad work by an odd fellow from Chelsea”. In the audience was the seventeen-year-old Herbert Howells, who recalled it seventy-one years later as “so disturbing and moving that I even asked RVW for his autograph – and got it!” He and Ivor Gurney spent the night walking the streets of Gloucester. On 12 October, Vaughan Williams’s thirty-eighth birthday, A Sea Symphony, on texts by Walt Whitman, was first performed at the Leeds Festival.

Whose tune?The theme is the third of nine tunes Thomas Tallis wrote for Archbishop Parker’s Psalter of 1567, where it is set to Parker’s verse version of Psalm 2. Vaughan Williams came across it while editing The English Hymnal, where it is set to Addison’s hymn “When rising from the bed of death”.

1914–22Wagon orderly

On New Year’s Eve 1914, aged forty-two, he enlisted as a private in the Royal Army Medical Corps. In late June 1916, newly in France with his field ambulance, he wrote to Holst: “I am ‘Wagon orderly’ and go up the line every night to bring back wounded & sick on a motor ambulance.” His unit moved to Salonika that autumn. In 1917 he trained for a commission in the Royal Garrison Artillery, and he was demobilised on 15 February 1919.

First performancesComposed in 1914, The Lark Ascending was delayed by the war. Marie Hall and the pianist Geoffrey Mendham gave the first performance at Shirehampton Public Hall, Bristol, on 15 December 1920; the orchestral version was first played at Queen’s Hall the following June. The score is prefaced with lines Vaughan Williams chose from George Meredith’s poem.

The Pastoral Symphony, played at Queen’s Hall by the Royal Philharmonic Society under Adrian Boult on 26 January 1922, grew out of those nights. “It is really war time music,” he told Ursula Wood in a letter of October 1938, “… it’s not really Lambkins frisking at all as most people take for granted.” In the letter he named the place as a steep hill at Écoivres, seen from the ambulance wagon, with “a wonderful Corot-like landscape in the sunset”.

1919–35Dorking, Bach and a harder voice

In 1919 he began teaching composition at the Royal College and returned to the Leith Hill Musical Festival, which his sister Margaret and Lady Evangeline Farrer had founded in 1905; he conducted it until 1953. From 1921 to 1928 he was musical director of the Bach Choir. In 1929 he and Adeline moved from Cheyne Walk to The White Gates at Dorking, a house that suited her, as her arthritis was getting worse.

The music of those years includes Job, a masque for dancing drawn from Blake’s engravings of the Book of Job and staged in London in July 1931, and the Fourth Symphony, first played by the BBC Symphony Orchestra under Boult at Queen’s Hall on 10 April 1935. It opens with a dissonant semitone clash. He always denied that it answered events in Europe. Ursula’s explanation was that “without any philosophical, prophetic or political germ, No 4 took its life from a paragraph in The Times.”

1938–58Ursula, the Fifth and the Ninth

On 31 March 1938 the young poet Ursula Wood met him to talk over a ballet scenario based on the ballad Clerk Saunders, and he took her to lunch. On 5 October that year sixteen solo singers gave the first Serenade to Music, his setting of Shakespeare’s moonlit scene from The Merchant of Venice, at Henry Wood’s jubilee concert in the Royal Albert Hall.

On 24 June 1943 he conducted the first performance of the Fifth Symphony at a Promenade Concert, asking the London Philharmonic “not to take too much notice of me—just make music”. The score is inscribed “Dedicated without permission to Jean Sibelius”. Sibelius received a copy through the British Council and replied in June 1946: “I have seldom heard anything that is more English.”

Adeline died on 10 May 1951, a fortnight after The Pilgrim’s Progress, the Bunyan opera he had been turning over since 1905, opened at Covent Garden. He married Ursula on 7 February 1953 at St Pancras Vestry Chapel, and they moved to 10 Hanover Terrace, beside Regent’s Park. Malcolm Sargent conducted the Royal Philharmonic in the first performance of the Ninth Symphony on 2 April 1958. Vaughan Williams died on 26 August that year. At the service in Westminster Abbey on 19 September, Boult conducted Five Variants of Dives and Lazarus, and the congregation sang Come down, O Love Divine to the tune he had named for his birthplace while the casket was carried to the north aisle.

The Sound

♮Church modes

Tallis’s psalm tune is in the Phrygian mode, the white notes of the piano from E to E, with a flat second and a rising phrase to which Vaughan Williams returns repeatedly in the Fantasia. The oboe tune that opens the Intermezzo of the English Folk Song Suite, My Bonny Boy, is in F Dorian. Vaughan Williams took these modal scales both from Tudor psalm tunes and from Essex singers.

𝄢Strings in groups

The Tallis Fantasia splits the strings three ways: a main orchestra, a second orchestra of nine players (two first violins, two seconds, two violas, two cellos and one double bass) and a solo quartet. The small group often answers the large one.

𝄞Bird and violin

In The Lark Ascending the soloist enters with a cadenza marked pianissimo and sur la touche, bowed over the fingerboard, and its quick flurries stand for the skylark’s song. The 6/8 melody that follows has the shape of a folk song. The piece borrows no actual folk tune.

♩Quiet symphonies

The Pastoral Symphony has hardly three moments of fortissimo from first bar to last. Its large orchestra thickens the texture, and each melodic line carries its own harmony. The Fifth Symphony, in D, uses the smallest orchestra of all nine and opens over a low C in the strings, with a call on two horns.

♯Hard edges

The Fourth Symphony begins with a semitone clash, and a four-note figure runs through most of it. The finale is headed con epilogo fugato, and the clash from the opening returns near the end. The same composer wrote the Fifth.

𝄋Tunes for general use

The hymn tunes Sine Nomine and Down Ampney come from The English Hymnal of 1906. He was joint music editor of Songs of Praise in 1925 and of The Oxford Book of Carols in 1928, and he conducted amateur choirs at Dorking for nearly fifty years.

Sister Arts

Geoffrey Keynes, a surgeon and Blake scholar, saw dramatic groupings and gestures in the plates of the Illustrations of the Book of Job and proposed a ballet. Gwen Raverat, Vaughan Williams’s cousin, helped design it. The masque that resulted includes a Pavane and a Galliard of the Sons of the Morning. In plate 14 the morning stars stand in a row above the seated Creator, with the text “When the morning Stars sang together” beneath.

Writing to Ursula Wood in 1938, Vaughan Williams recalled the ambulance route at Écoivres as “a wonderful Corot-like landscape in the sunset”. This oval evening scene, with cattle by a lake and a hill town against a pink sky, was catalogued in an 1897 sale as an effect of the setting sun.

The Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis was first played in Gloucester Cathedral on 6 September 1910, before an audience of about two thousand. Vaughan Williams divided his strings into three groups, which play in alternation across the building.

George Meredith

The Lark Ascending — the lines in Vaughan Williams’s score

1881
EnglishHe rises and begins to round, He drops the silver chain of sound, Of many links without a break, In chirrup, whistle, slur and shake [ … ] For singing till his heaven fills, ‘Tis love of earth that he instils, And ever winging up and up, Our valley is his golden cup, And he the wine which overflows To lift us with him as he goes [ … ] Till lost on his aërial rings In light, and then the fancy sings.

Vaughan Williams chose these three passages from Meredith’s poem to preface the score. The solo violin plays trills and runs that stand for the skylark’s “silver chain of sound”.

Lineage

Drew fromHubert ParryThomas TallisGustav HolstMaurice RavelJean Sibelius
Vaughan Williams
Passed toHerbert HowellsGordon Jacob

Parry taught him at the Royal College and introduced him to an English choral tradition. Tallis’s psalm tune became the basis of the Fantasia. Holst was his closest friend and critic. Ravel gave him three months of lessons in 1907–08. The Fifth Symphony is dedicated to Sibelius, who replied in 1946. Howells was in the audience at Gloucester in 1910 and later received the dedication of Hodie. Gordon Jacob, his pupil, made the orchestration of the Folk Song Suite that is most often heard today.

Further reading

  • Hugh Cobbe (ed.), Letters of Ralph Vaughan Williams 1895–1958 (Oxford University Press, 2008; paperback 2010).letters

    Some 750 letters, selected from about 3,300 then known and arranged chronologically, covering Vaughan Williams’s musical, personal and political concerns, including his music, his war service and his friends. The Vaughan Williams Foundation’s online database now holds over 5,000.

  • Vaughan Williams Foundation, The Letters of Ralph Vaughan Williams (searchable database of over 5,000 items).letters database

    Each transcript gives the date, the recipient, the location of the original and the page in Cobbe’s edition, so that a quotation can be checked against the whole correspondence.

  • Ursula Vaughan Williams, R.V.W.: A Biography of Ralph Vaughan Williams (Oxford University Press, 1964).biography

    His widow’s biography records the composer’s life as the household remembered it, from the origins of the Pastoral Symphony at Écoivres to the paragraph in The Times behind the Fourth. The Foundation calls it a key study and a mine of information.

  • Michael Kennedy, The Works of Ralph Vaughan Williams (Oxford University Press, second edition 1980).critical survey

    An authoritative account of his musical life, with a select list of works in chronological order, covering the order in which the pieces appeared and their first performances.

  • Michael Kennedy, A Catalogue of the Works of Ralph Vaughan Williams (Oxford University Press, second edition 1996).catalogue

    The standard catalogue of his music and writings, giving the dates, scoring and first performances of each piece, published or unpublished.

  • Alain Frogley and Aidan J. Thomson (eds), The Cambridge Companion to Vaughan Williams (Cambridge University Press, 2013).essays

    Essays by leading specialists, including one on his large choral works and the British festival tradition.
