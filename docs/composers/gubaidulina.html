Sofia Gubaidulina

Born 24 October 1931, Chistopol, Tatar ASSR, Soviet Union
Died 13 March 2025, Appen, Germany

She made the crossing of registers, the pressure of a bow and the breath of an accordion carry the argument of a sacred drama.

Life

Gubaidulina was born in Chistopol in 1931, to a Volga Tatar father and a mother of Russian and Polish descent. The family spoke Russian and moved to Kazan while she was a child. Her education there joined piano playing to composition; she graduated from the Kazan Conservatory in 1954 and continued in Moscow with Nikolai Peiko, then Vissarion Shebalin. Her mature music retained little of the conservatory’s required public optimism. A bass register, a strained string or an accordion’s expiring breath could establish a physical predicament before a listener knew the work’s title.

Moscow offered encounters with performers as important as lessons with composers. Gubaidulina wrote for the percussionist Mark Pekarsky, the bassoonist Valery Popov, the cellist Vladimir Tonkha and the bayan player Friedrich Lips. The resulting works investigate particular mechanisms: how a reed speaks under changing air pressure, where a string produces a harmonic, how a bow passes from stable pitch into noise. Each mechanism can determine a piece’s form.

Soviet employmentTwo kinds of commission

After completing her studies she joined the Composers’ Union and supported herself largely through film music. The cinema provided work for roughly thirty years, including music for animation, while her concert scores could pursue religious subjects and unapproved techniques. Official employment coexisted with censorship. In 1979 Tikhon Khrennikov publicly attacked seven composers whose music had appeared at Western festivals without permission; Gubaidulina was among them. Performances abroad could make an already precarious professional position worse.

Instrumental play runs through her piano music and into the later sacred works. The Chaconne of 1962 works with repeated harmonic weight, registral leaps and increasingly forceful keyboard writing. The fourteen Musical Toys of 1969 reduce ideas to forms a child can handle: a clock, a mechanical toy, a little song. The bassoon concerto of 1975 sets a soloist against an ensemble of low instruments. Register already creates a dramatic condition.

AstrejaIn 1975 Gubaidulina, Viktor Suslin and Vyacheslav Artyomov formed an improvising group, using folk instruments alongside unfamiliar sound-producing objects. These sessions were a laboratory for ensemble sound.

The Astreja improvisations let her work directly with sound, before translating it into staff notation. Numerical proportions mattered, but the material being proportioned could come from bodily experiment: a scrape, a resonance, a change of pressure. In finished scores she specifies these actions closely. The player’s freedom in discovering a sound and the composer’s control of its place in time belong to different stages of the work.

The bayanAn instrument that breathes

The bayan is the Russian chromatic button accordion; a concert instrument with free bass allows independent low notes in the accompaniment. Lips showed Gubaidulina what its reeds and bellows could do. In De profundis (1978), for solo bayan, low clusters, high sustained tones and passages of moving air turn the instrument into something between a keyboard, a wind instrument and a human chest. The psalm title gives the upward movement a theological reference.

In Seven Words (1982), cello and bayan confront a string ensemble in seven meditations on Christ’s sayings from the cross. Glissandos, compressed intervals and changes between ordinary stopped notes and harmonics make tension audible as resistance. The cello’s bowed line and the bayan’s sustained breath remain identifiable even when the surrounding strings become dense. Dissonance, distortion and interrupted continuity give Gubaidulina’s Christian subjects their musical substance.

OffertoriumA theme given away

Gidon Kremer’s advocacy brought her international recognition through Offertorium, written in 1980 and first performed in Vienna in 1981. Its opening borrows the royal theme from Bach’s Musical Offering, distributed among orchestral colours in a manner recalling Webern’s arrangement of Bach’s six-part ricercar. Gubaidulina then removes notes from the ends of the theme. A recognisable musical object is gradually deprived of its identity; its later reconstruction is the formal counterpart of the title’s offering.

The concerto’s solo violin connects dissolution to reconstruction, with cadenzas, isolated lines and a final chorale-like stretch of sustained melody. Kremer carried the piece into Western concert programmes; his recording with Charles Dutoit and the Boston Symphony Orchestra followed in 1988. The work’s religious argument and its compositional process are unusually close: subtraction and restoration are events a listener can follow by ear.

Counting timeIn the Fibonacci sequence each number is the sum of the previous two: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Ratios between successive terms approach the golden ratio. Gubaidulina used such relationships to organise durations.

Sound and silenceA conductor without an orchestra

The twelve-movement symphony Stimmen… verstummen… (1986) makes opposing states into a large structure. Stable D major sonorities alternate with more disturbed, mobile music; their allotted lengths change as the work proceeds. In the ninth movement the conductor continues alone while the orchestra is silent. Precisely organised gesture retains duration after instrumental sound has disappeared. Here the conductor’s body, normally treated as a means of coordinating somebody else’s music, becomes the visible bearer of the composition.

Gubaidulina settled in Appen, outside Hamburg, in 1992. International commissions now allowed the scale of her music to increase without displacing the relationships with individual players. Yuri Bashmet introduced her Viola Concerto in 1997. The Johannes-Passion (2000), followed by Johannes-Ostern (2001), joins the Passion narrative to a much larger sacred design. The texts are in Russian; the Gospel of John and the Book of Revelation bring historical narrative and apocalyptic vision into the same work.

For Anne-Sophie Mutter she wrote In tempus praesens (2007), a second violin concerto whose orchestra omits violins. The solo instrument therefore occupies a register and colour without an orchestral section of its own kind to reinforce it. Later works included a Triple Concerto for violin, cello and bayan, premiered in Boston in 2017, and The Wrath of God, first performed in 2020. She died at home in Appen on 13 March 2025, aged ninety-three; the orchestral Prologue, commissioned by the Boston Symphony and Leipzig Gewandhaus orchestras, was still in progress.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Instrumental pressure

Bow pressure, harmonics, reed noise and bellows movement are compositional materials. The bayan in De profundis can produce a stable chord, a dense cluster or the sound of air without a normal pitched attack. A transition between these states changes the listener’s sense of physical effort as clearly as a change of harmony.

Register

High and low can behave like opposing participants. In In croce, originally for cello and organ, the instruments exchange registral territory; the crossing makes the title audible in the form. In In tempus praesens the absence of orchestral violins separates the soloist’s sound from the collective string body.

Proportion

Fibonacci relationships organise spans and returns. They are a way of giving unequal durations a family resemblance: 3, 5 and 8 differ, yet each larger number grows from its predecessors. In Stimmen… verstummen…, numerical planning extends into the silent conductor’s cadenza. Count and gesture survive when the instruments stop.

Harmony

Triads and chorales coexist with clusters, microtonal inflections and noisy attacks. A D major sonority in Stimmen… verstummen… serves as an identifiable pole within this varied language. Consonance can be placed at a distance, threatened, interrupted or withheld; its position within the form matters as much as its interval content.

Sacred form

Religious ideas become actions: crossing, offering, descent, ascent and the suspension of sound. Offertorium sacrifices a borrowed theme by removing notes, then rebuilds it. These operations on musical material allow theology and compositional technique to share a structure.

Editorial comparison

The cross, the bowed figures and the narrow vertical field organise the icon before individual facial expressions are read. Gubaidulina’s Seven Words treats the same subject through seven instrumental movements; In croce makes a crossing of registers into a formal event. The link is a shared Christian tradition.

Documented usePsalm 130, verses 1–3; Latin Vulgate

De profundis

Ancient psalm; late-antique Latin version
LatinDe profundis clamavi ad te, Domine; Domine, exaudi vocem meam. Fiant aures tuae intendentes in vocem deprecationis meae. Si iniquitates observaveris, Domine, Domine, quis sustinebit?
English · Contrapunctus translationFrom the depths I have cried to you, Lord; Lord, hear my voice. Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my pleading. If you take account of wrongs, Lord, Lord, who will stand?

Numbered 129 in the Vulgate and 130 in the Hebrew tradition, this psalm supplies the title of Gubaidulina’s 1978 bayan piece. Its first movement is verbal: a voice calls upwards from below. Her instrumental register and the audible labour of the bellows translate that relation into sound.

Later connectionsContemporary bayan repertoireFriedrich LipsElsbeth Moser

Bach supplies both contrapuntal precedent and, in Offertorium, an actual theme; Webern provides a model for distributing a line between instrumental colours. Gubaidulina’s work with bayan players changed the concert repertory of their instrument. Her religious forms remain distinct from Pärt’s tintinnabuli: noise, microtonal inflection and physical strain are structural resources.
