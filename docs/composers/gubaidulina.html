Life

At the family piano in Kazan, Vera Gubaidulina played the keys while her sister Sofia experimented with the strings inside. Their parents had bought a poor instrument; the children found more than one way to use it. Sofia had been born in Chistopol on 24 October 1931, to a Volga Tatar surveyor and a mother of Russian and Polish descent. The family spoke Russian and moved to Kazan during her childhood. In a 2013 interview with Stuart Jeffries, she explained her early ambition: “I was convinced there was too little music in the world. We needed more.”

She studied piano and composition at the Kazan Conservatory, graduating in 1954, then went to Moscow to work with Nikolai Peiko and Vissarion Shebalin. She finished her studies in 1961. While still a student, she played her symphony at the piano for Shostakovich. His advice, as she recalled it to Jeffries, was: “My wish for you is that you should continue on your own incorrect path.” In a conservatory where an incorrect path could be a serious charge, the older composer had given her permission to persist.

MoscowCinema and the Composers’ Union

Membership of the Composers’ Union made a freelance livelihood possible. Film scores paid most of her bills for the next thirty years, and cinema gave her something a desk could not: orchestral music heard soon after it was written. She credited this practical schooling with teaching her to handle an orchestra. Concert life was harder. In 1979 the Union’s secretary, Tikhon Khrennikov, denounced seven composers whose music had been performed at a festival in Cologne. Gubaidulina’s name was on the list, alongside those of her friends Viktor Suslin and Vyacheslav Artyomov.

The piano pieces show how much she could do with modest means. Her Chaconne (1962), commissioned by the pianist Marina Mdivani, begins with B minor chords and works its opening harmony through increasingly busy eight-bar stretches. In the fourteen Musical Toys (1969), the titles include Mechanical Accordion, The Woodpecker and The Echo. The bassoon concerto of 1975, written for Valery Popov, brought another character into her music: the persecuted little man of Gogol’s The Overcoat, facing a crowd of low instruments.

That same year she formed Astreja with Suslin and Artyomov, following the example of the percussionist Mark Pekarsky’s ensemble. They improvised on folk instruments from Russia, the Caucasus and Central Asia, and on objects that made interesting noises. Piano, violin and cello were excluded: familiar instruments would tempt their hands back into learned phrases. Most sessions were private. Gubaidulina described the group to Nolan Gasser in 2012 as a place to discover sounds before committing them to paper; an audience was an occasional guest.

1978–82Learning the bellows

The bayanA Russian button accordion. The free-bass instrument lets the left hand play individual notes independently, opening possibilities well beyond a fixed accompaniment of basses and chords.

Friedrich Lips remembered meeting Gubaidulina at a Composers’ Union hearing in the winter of 1974–75, after he had played a sonata by Vladislav Zolotaryov. She wanted to write for his bayan and asked him to teach her its workings. She visited his classroom and his home, questioning details that experienced players took for granted. In 1978 she arrived with De profundis and played it to him on a grand piano. Lips suggested adding a pitch-bending glissando. The finished piece makes a psalm out of low clusters, sustained notes and the air escaping through the instrument’s bellows.

Personal faith“My faith is my life, and it is my secret.” Gubaidulina, in written answers to Nolan Gasser, March 2012; translated from her Russian.

In Seven Words (1982), Lips and the cellist Vladimir Tonkha helped her find more ways for instruments to enact the Crucifixion. A held cello note is crossed by a glissando on the neighbouring string; the bayan bends one sound against another. The string orchestra answers like a choir. Across the first six movements the strain accumulates, until the cellist plays on the bridge. In the seventh the bow passes beyond it. Gubaidulina thanked the two players for discoveries she could build into the score, including the cello’s trembling double harmonics.

OffertoriumThe theme loses its edges

Gidon Kremer gave her music its decisive opening abroad. He introduced Offertorium in Vienna on 30 May 1981, with Leif Segerstam conducting the ORF Symphony Orchestra. The concerto begins with the royal theme from Bach’s Musical Offering, passed between instruments as in Webern’s orchestration of the six-part ricercar. Its final note is missing. At each subsequent statement Gubaidulina removes notes from the two ends, until the tune has been almost consumed. After the solo violin’s trials, the theme grows again from its centre. At the end the violin sings it backwards.

Kremer carried the concerto through Western concert halls, and in 1988 recorded it with Charles Dutoit and the Boston Symphony Orchestra during the American–Soviet cultural exchange in Boston. The scale of its success rested on a precise, almost severe idea: a piece of music could be offered up, note by note. The original theme was familiar enough to be missed when it disappeared. Its return required a violinist to make a newly assembled melody sing.

1986The orchestra stops

Counting timeEach Fibonacci number adds the previous two: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. Gubaidulina used these relationships to measure both sounding passages and silences.

Stimmen… verstummen…, her twelve-movement symphony, begins with the three notes of D major. Strings change their manner of playing them; winds enter; the organ holds the chord underneath. More restless movements interrupt this stillness. The work was written for the conductor Gennady Rozhdestvensky, and its ninth movement gives him an extraordinary solo: as the percussion dies away, he continues conducting in silence. His hands mark out carefully proportioned spans. The orchestra has stopped, but his part is still running.

AppenA room near the woods

In 1992 she settled in Appen, outside Hamburg, where she could walk in the woods and work away from city noise. Commissions grew larger, while particular players remained at their centre. For Yuri Bashmet she wrote the Viola Concerto (1996), first performed in Chicago in 1997. Its opening grows from a single note into a semitone; an extra string quartet, tuned a quarter-tone below the orchestra, makes the familiar instruments sound disquietingly out of place. In Sonnengesang (1997), written for Mstislav Rostropovich, she surrounded the cello with voices and percussion, setting St Francis of Assisi’s praise of creation.

The Johannes-Passion (2000), commissioned by the Bachakademie Stuttgart for the 250th anniversary of Bach’s death, joined two Russian biblical texts: the Gospel of John and Revelation. Events on earth answer visions of heaven. She had intended to include the Resurrection, but the Passion grew so large that she wrote a separate companion, Johannes-Ostern (2001). Valery Gergiev conducted the complete pair in Hamburg’s St Michael’s Church on 16 March 2002.

For Anne-Sophie Mutter’s In tempus praesens (2007), she removed all the orchestral violins: the soloist faces fifteen violas, twelve cellos and nine double basses, with heavy brass and percussion behind them. In Boston in 2017, Baiba Skride, Harriet Krijgh and Elsbeth Moser introduced the Triple Concerto for violin, cello and bayan under Andris Nelsons. The Wrath of God received its first performance in 2020. Gubaidulina died in Appen on 13 March 2025, aged ninety-three. She was still working on Prologue, an orchestral commission shared by the Boston Symphony and Leipzig Gewandhaus orchestras.

The Sound

𝄢Breath

The bayan can let air escape without sounding a note. In De profundis, that breath belongs beside the low clusters and sustained pitches: the instrument’s ordinary mechanism becomes part of the music. Friedrich Lips helped Gubaidulina explore its bellows and registers, and suggested the pitch-bending glissando. A note can sag under pressure, while the performer’s arms keep the sound alive.

𝄞Crossings

A cross can be made with two sounding lines. In Seven Words, a cello holds one note while a neighbouring string slides across it; the bayan produces a related crossing through pressure on an adjacent button. In In croce, cello and organ exchange high and low positions over the whole piece. The title describes something the players actually do.

𝅗𝅥Proportion

She measured the time around a phrase as carefully as its notes. Fibonacci numbers supply lengths for passages and rests in Stimmen… verstummen…, including the conductor’s silent cadenza. In her 2012 answers to Nolan Gasser, she explained that proportion helped her contain the music she imagined. She could spend a long time reconciling a phrase with the span allotted to it.

♮Triads

A plain major chord can occupy an entire movement. The opening of Stimmen… verstummen… holds D–F♯–A while the strings and winds change its sound; D♭ major clouds it at the end. The following movement brings sliding strings and wide leaps. The calm chord has acquired a precise place in the drama, and its next appearance carries the memory of that disturbance.

𝄋The solitary player

Her concertos often make the soloist’s predicament audible in the seating plan. In tempus praesens has no orchestral violins, leaving Mutter’s instrument alone above a string section that begins with violas. The Viola Concerto introduces a string quartet tuned a quarter-tone low. Even when the instruments seem to belong to the same family, their tuning can keep them apart.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1962
    ChaconnePiano
    Written at Marina Mdivani’s request. B minor chords establish the opening; repeated eight-bar spans become busier, with moving octaves and semiquavers pulling the harmony across the keyboard.
  2. 1969
    Musical ToysPiano
    Fourteen small pieces for children, beginning with Mechanical Accordion and ending with Forest Musicians. A woodpecker, an echo and a drummer offer young pianists characters to play as well as notes.
  3. 1975
    Concerto for Bassoon and Low InstrumentsSolo bassoon and low instrumental ensemble
    Valery Popov’s bassoon becomes the vulnerable individual in a crowd. Gogol’s The Overcoat lies behind this drama, with the low instruments surrounding a voice that has little room to escape.
  4. 1978
    De profundisSolo bayan
    A psalm for bellows and reeds, written with Friedrich Lips. He suggested the pitch-bending glissando; clusters and audible air make the cry from the depths a bodily effort.
  5. 1980; revised 1982, 1986
    OffertoriumViolin and orchestra
    Kremer’s concerto begins by giving away Bach’s royal theme: notes disappear from both ends at successive returns. The melody is rebuilt from the middle and finally sung backwards by the violin.
  6. 1982
    Seven WordsCello, bayan and strings
    Christ’s last sayings become seven instrumental movements. Tonkha and Lips helped devise the crossings and strained harmonics; in the last movement the cello bow passes beyond the bridge.
  7. 1986
    Stimmen… verstummen…Large orchestra
    D major alternates with sliding, unsettled music across twelve movements. The ninth belongs to the conductor, whose hands continue to shape time after the instruments fall silent.
  8. 1996
    Viola ConcertoViola and orchestra
    Written for Yuri Bashmet. A note opens into a semitone, and a separate string quartet, tuned a quarter-tone below the orchestra, supplies an uncanny second set of strings.
  9. 2000
    Johannes-PassionVocal soloists, two mixed choirs, organ and orchestra
    The Gospel of John meets Revelation in a Russian-language Passion. Its planned Resurrection expanded into a separate work, Johannes-Ostern; Gergiev first conducted the complete pair in Hamburg in 2002.
  10. 2007
    In tempus praesensViolin and orchestra without orchestral violins
    Mutter’s violin has no companions of its own kind in the orchestra. Fifteen violas, twelve cellos and nine double basses face it across a concerto whose central crisis Gubaidulina conceived as a ritual sacrifice.

Sister Arts

Dionisius painted this icon for the iconostasis of the Trinity Cathedral at Pavlo-Obnorsky Monastery in 1500. Mary stands with the women on one side of the cross; John and the centurion Longinus stand on the other. The same Crucifixion is the subject of Gubaidulina’s Seven Words. Where the painter gathers witnesses around Christ, she sets the two solo players against strings that recall choral singing.

Psalm 130, verses 1–3; Latin Vulgate

De profundis

Ancient psalm; late-antique Latin version
LatinDe profundis clamavi ad te, Domine; Domine, exaudi vocem meam. Fiant aures tuae intendentes in vocem deprecationis meae. Si iniquitates observaveris, Domine, Domine, quis sustinebit?
Our translationFrom the depths I have cried to you, Lord; Lord, hear my voice. Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my pleading. If you take account of wrongs, Lord, Lord, who will stand?

The psalm begins with someone asking to be heard: first a cry from below, then a plea for attentive ears. Its Latin opening supplies the title of Gubaidulina’s De profundis (1978), written for Friedrich Lips. His bayan has no words to sing, but its bellows can make a breath audible. The poem is Psalm 129 in the Vulgate, 130 in the Hebrew numbering.

Recordings to own

  • OffertoriumGidon Kremer, Boston Symphony Orchestra, Charles DutoitDeutsche Grammophon · 1988The most persuasive single introduction to her orchestral music: Kremer’s intensity has room around it, and Dutoit keeps the changing instruments distinct. The closing reconstruction of the Bach theme feels earned after the concerto’s violent middle.
  • Seven Words · In croce · SilenzioElsbeth Moser, Maria Kliegel, Camerata Transsylvanica, György SelmecziNaxosA useful portrait of the bayan at three distances: beside a cello, within a trio and against chamber strings. The intimate cello-and-bayan In croce is the attraction, though Kliegel’s soft-edged tone takes some bite out of its confrontations.
  • In tempus praesensAnne-Sophie Mutter, London Symphony Orchestra, Valery GergievDeutsche Grammophon · 2008Mutter and Gergiev give the encounter a fierce theatrical urgency, with the violin holding its ground against the orchestra’s low brass and percussion. The Gubaidulina is the compelling half of an album coupled with two Bach concertos.

Lineage

Gubaidulina
Passed toContemporary bayan repertoireFriedrich LipsElsbeth Moser

Asked about her models by Nolan Gasser in 2012, Gubaidulina named Bach, Webern and Shostakovich: Bach for joining invention to intuition, Webern for clear construction, Shostakovich for tragedy. Offertorium makes the first two connections audible in a single borrowed theme. Friedrich Lips and Elsbeth Moser belong on the other side of this history: their hands helped bring her discoveries into the bayan’s concert repertoire.
