35.1Life

Rimsky-Korsakov was born in Tikhvin into a family with a tradition of naval service. He entered the Naval College in Saint Petersburg in 1856, while continuing piano lessons and attending opera. His teacher Fyodor Kanille introduced him to Balakirev in 1861. The young officer began a symphony before he had the theoretical equipment expected of a conservatory composition student. A naval voyage on the clipper Almaz took him abroad in 1862–65, interrupting regular contact with the circle while work on the symphony continued. Balakirev conducted the First Symphony in Saint Petersburg in 1865.

His early colleagues included Mussorgsky, Borodin and Cui. With Balakirev they became the group known as the Five, united by friendship and cultural argument despite their differing methods. Rimsky-Korsakov’s orchestral picture Sadko, first written in 1867, already connects a supernatural subject with an unusual pitch organisation. The sea musician’s underwater world encouraged scales and chord relationships outside ordinary major and minor. His experience of ships and oceans gave the subject a biographical setting.

The professor learnsSaint Petersburg, 1871

Autobiography as evidenceIn My Musical Life, Rimsky-Korsakov described the gaps in his knowledge when he became a professor. The confession records a subsequent change of standards as well as an earlier lack of training.

In 1871 he accepted a professorship at the Saint Petersburg Conservatory. Teaching forced him into systematic study of harmony, counterpoint and fugue; the supposed representative of an anti-academic movement became one of Russia’s central academic musicians. He worked through exercises while teaching students, eventually revising earlier pieces in the light of what he had learned. In 1872 he married Nadezhda Purgold, a pianist and composer whose musicianship supported his arrangements, rehearsals and publications. Appointed inspector of naval bands in 1873, he also gained practical knowledge of wind instruments.

That knowledge was inseparable from listening to combinations at particular dynamic levels. A flute’s weak low register needs different treatment from the same notes at the top of its compass; a brass chord can conceal a string line even when the written rhythms match. His later Principles of Orchestration, completed for publication after his death by Maximilian Steinberg, treats these questions as matters of balance and function. The repertory illustrated in the book is chiefly his own, and its recommendations describe his orchestral practice.

The theatreCalendars, creatures and rituals

The Snow Maiden, composed in 1880–81 after Ostrovsky’s play, organised a fairy-tale society around seasonal ritual. Human love, winter and the return of the sun receive different musical materials; the heroine’s fate is also a change in the calendar. The opera matters more to Rimsky-Korsakov’s development than a list restricted to concert favourites suggests. He continued to move between historical subjects, folk ceremony and supernatural plots. The orchestra could identify a class of being, maintain a recurring environment or connect a succession of numbers without eliminating songs, dances and public choruses.

The orchestral works of 1887–88 made that technique portable. Capriccio espagnol passes material through exposed instrumental solos and brilliant combinations. Scheherazade uses a returning solo violin as a framing presence while transforming themes across four movements. Rimsky-Korsakov resisted an excessively literal assignment of every recurrence to a named character or incident. The Russian Easter Festival Overture places Orthodox chant melodies in an instrumental design whose opening recitative-like passages prepare a much faster celebration. All three works depend on the redistribution of recognisable material.

Editing the deadRimsky-Korsakov prepared performing versions of Mussorgsky’s works and, with Glazunov, completed Borodin’s Prince Igor. Preservation, completion and rewriting are different activities, although his editions sometimes combine all three.

His treatment of Mussorgsky remains contested because its benefits came with changes. He regularised what he regarded as faulty harmony or awkward instrumentation, helping establish Boris Godunov internationally but replacing parts of its musical argument with his own preferences. The familiar Night on Bald Mountain draws on versions later than Mussorgsky’s 1867 score and supplies a new performing structure. Comparison with the surviving originals reveals the extent of his intervention.

The private operaPainting enters the stage

Savva Mamontov’s Moscow Private Opera created a setting in which his later fairy tales met new painting and stage design. The soprano Nadezhda Zabela, wife of Mikhail Vrubel, became an important interpreter; she created the Swan Princess in The Tale of Tsar Saltan in 1900. Vrubel’s costumes and paintings grew from this collaboration. Sadko, now an opera, and Tsar Saltan could make the supernatural audible through altered scale collections while singers and dancers remained in distinctly articulated theatrical numbers.

1905–08The fairy tale becomes dangerous

During the 1905 revolution Rimsky-Korsakov defended protesting Conservatory students and opposed police interference. His dismissal caused a public dispute and solidarity resignations; he was reinstated later that year. This was the same teacher whose private pupils included Stravinsky, learning orchestration and submitting compositions for criticism. His last opera, The Golden Cockerel, completed in 1907, turns an incompetent ruler and his military apparatus into a fairy-tale satire. The aftermath of the Russo-Japanese War sharpened its political implications.

Censorship prevented an untroubled production. Rimsky-Korsakov died at Lyubensk in June 1908 before the opera reached the stage in Moscow in 1909. The Legend of the Invisible City of Kitezh, premiered in 1907, and The Golden Cockerel offer different late treatments of collective belief: one follows a threatened community towards a transformed city, the other exposes the machinery of obedience. Both make harmony and orchestration do dramatic work. Stravinsky’s early ballets inherited the distinction between ordinary and supernatural musical worlds, then changed the rhythmic organisation far beyond his teacher’s practice.

35.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞Orchestral balance

Instrumental register determines what can be heard. Rimsky-Korsakov distinguishes a low flute from a high one, a solo horn from a horn section, and a doubled melody from a genuinely blended chord. Capriccio espagnol repeatedly moves the listener’s attention from the ensemble to a single exposed player.

♮Symmetrical harmony

The octatonic scale alternates tones and semitones, giving eight notes to the octave. Because its interval pattern repeats, a transposition by a minor third preserves the same collection. That weakens the unique authority of a tonic and supplies a differentiated harmonic environment for supernatural characters.

♯Human & supernatural

Diatonic melody, folk-like phrases and recognisable dances coexist with whole-tone or octatonic passages. The contrast gives the drama distinct musical environments. A character can cross between them as its status changes, while orchestration makes the crossing immediately audible.

𝄋Thematic transformation

Scheherazade keeps several themes in circulation while changing their pace, register and instrumental carrier. The recurring material acts as a frame and a memory, allowing contrasting episodes to belong to one four-movement design.

𝅘𝅥Song & ritual

The operas retain songs, choruses, dances and ceremonial scenes. A public ritual can organise an act’s pacing: seasonal festivals in The Snow Maiden, the sea kingdom in Sadko, or the ruler’s public display in The Golden Cockerel. Genre is part of the social organisation on stage.

35.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

    An early underwater fantasy and an important laboratory for the symmetrical harmonic language later expanded in the opera.
  2. 1880–81
    The Snow MaidenOpera; prologue and four acts
    After Ostrovsky. Seasonal ritual, human desire and the heroine’s supernatural origin form one dramatic system; premiered in 1882.
  3. 1887
    Capriccio espagnolOp. 34Orchestra
    Five linked movements built around Spanish material; solo violin and winds repeatedly emerge from and return to the ensemble.
  4. 1888
    ScheherazadeOp. 35Orchestra with prominent solo violin
    Four movements after The Thousand and One Nights. Themes change their function and colour as they recur.
  5. 1888
    Russian Easter Festival OvertureOp. 36Orchestra
    Chant melodies from the Orthodox tradition pass from slow, recitative-like presentation to a fast orchestral celebration.
  6. 1894–96
    Sadko, operaOpera in seven scenes
    Novgorod merchants, visiting traders and an underwater kingdom; the three visiting guests sing musically differentiated accounts of distant places.
  7. 1898
    The Tsar’s BrideOpera in four acts
    A historical tragedy with a more directly lyrical vocal idiom than the fairy-tale operas; poisoning and mistaken identity destroy Marfa’s marriage prospects and sanity.
  8. 1899–1900
    The Tale of Tsar SaltanOpera after Pushkin
    The Swan Princess and the transformed Prince Gvidon belong to a magic world distinguished by harmony and orchestration. “The Flight of the Bumblebee” originated as an operatic interlude.
  9. 1903–04; revised 1905
    The Legend of the Invisible City of Kitezh and the Maiden FevroniyaOpera in four acts
    Folk legend, invasion and religious transformation; the orchestral interludes are structural links within a much larger vocal drama.
  10. 1906–07
    The Golden CockerelOpera in three acts with prologue and epilogue
    Pushkin’s tale becomes a satire of autocracy. Censorship delayed the premiere until 1909, after the composer’s death.

35.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Vrubel worked on the visual world of The Tale of Tsar Saltan; his wife, Nadezhda Zabela-Vrubel, created the Swan Princess in Mamontov’s production. The painting grew from that theatrical collaboration. Its feathered costume, jewelled headdress and uncertain border between body and plumage have a direct counterpart in an opera where transformation is an event in harmony as well as plot.

Bilibin’s illustrated edition appeared while Rimsky-Korsakov was completing his opera on the same Pushkin tale. This image belongs to the book. Bilibin subsequently designed for the opera’s first productions; his bounded colour areas and ornamental borders offered a visual language for the apparently archaic world in which the political satire could operate.

Alexander Pushkin

The Golden Cockerel — final couplet

1834; published 1835
RussianСказка ложь, да в ней намек! Добрым молодцам урок.
Our translationThe tale is a lie, but there is a hint in it! A lesson for fine young fellows.

Pushkin ends by refusing to name the lesson. Rimsky-Korsakov and his librettist Vladimir Belsky retained the distance between an improbable fairy story and its recognisable human arrangements: the ruler’s promises, his dependence on military advice, and the cost of treating others as possessions. The opera’s framing Astrologer makes the status of the spectacle itself unstable.

35.8Lineage

Passed toIgor StravinskySergei ProkofievAlexander GlazunovAnatoly LyadovOttorino Respighi

Stravinsky studied privately with Rimsky-Korsakov; Prokofiev encountered him at the Conservatory. The inheritance includes register-conscious orchestration and the use of contrasting pitch collections. Stravinsky’s The Firebird makes the connection particularly audible, while its successors turn inherited harmonic resources into new kinds of rhythmic construction.
