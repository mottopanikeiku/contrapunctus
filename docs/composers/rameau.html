Life

When Hippolyte et Aricie opened at the Paris Opéra on 1 October 1733, Rameau had just turned fifty. The singers and players had already defeated one of its boldest pages: a trio for the Fates whose lurching harmonies proved too difficult in rehearsal and had to be cut. The rest was quite enough to divide the house. Admirers heard a new theatrical voice; devotees of Lully heard an attack on their inheritance. An organist from Dijon had arrived with a first opera and a great deal of music to get off his chest.

1683–1722The organist’s long apprenticeship

His father played the organ in Dijon. Jean-Philippe followed the trade through a succession of French churches, with appointments in Clermont-Ferrand, Dijon and Lyon and an early attempt to establish himself in Paris. There he published his first harpsichord book in 1706. It begins with an unmeasured prelude: notes whose timing the player must work out, followed by a faster section that suddenly acquires a regular beat. Allemandes, courante and sarabandes follow. He was twenty-three, writing within the French keyboard tradition before Couperin had published his first book.

Back at Clermont-Ferrand, Rameau worked on the book that would make his name. The Traité de l’harmonie appeared in 1722, its preface declaring: “Music is a science which must have definite rules.” He wanted to explain the chords an organist’s fingers knew, and why one led naturally to another. Put C, E and G in a different order and the bass changes, yet the chord remains recognisably itself. From observations like this he built his account of the fundamental bass, a sequence of roots beneath the notes actually being played.

The bass below the bassIn C–E–G, C is both the lowest note and the root. Move E to the bottom and C remains the root. Rameau’s basse fondamentale follows those roots; it is an explanation of the harmony, and may never sound as a separate part.

He settled permanently in Paris in the early 1720s, published the Nouveau système in 1726 and kept revising his explanations as he studied the behaviour of vibrating strings. But he wanted a theatre. On 25 October 1727 he wrote to the librettist Houdar de La Motte asking for an opera text. His reputation for learning was getting in the way. “I try to conceal art through art itself,” he told La Motte, directing him to the harpsichord pieces and cantatas as proof of what he could do. No libretto came.

The keyboardBirds, hammers and a hen

The harpsichord books of 1724 and about 1728 are full of things that move. Birds call across the keyboard in Le Rappel des oiseaux. In Les Cyclopes, the hands alternate like drumsticks, then the left passes over the right to strike high and low notes: Rameau explains both manoeuvres in his preface. La Poule begins with repeated notes, a hen clucking, and works itself into an astonishing commotion. The tune of the A minor Gavotte survives six doubles, or variations, while the fingers acquire ever more work.

L’Enharmonique has a more treacherous surprise. A note is reinterpreted, and the next chord carries the music somewhere the ear had not expected. Rameau wrote a preface explaining the turn; then left the player to make it sound inevitable. In the Pièces de clavecin en concerts of 1741 he gave the harpsichord a fully written-out part, with a violin or flute and a viol or second violin alongside. The keyboard player could put away the usual task of filling in chords from a figured bass.

1733Hell comes home

The financier Alexandre Le Riche de La Pouplinière supplied the orchestra and society Rameau needed. His household brought the composer into contact with Voltaire and the abbé Simon-Joseph Pellegrin, who finally provided a libretto. Hippolyte et Aricie takes its lovers and its dangerous stepmother from Racine’s Phèdre, but opens the doors to gods, sea monsters and a whole act in the underworld. Pellegrin also saves the young lovers. Phèdre’s catastrophe continues around them.

In the underworld, the Fates tell Thésée that the hell he has escaped awaits him in his own house. The trio Rameau wrote for that prophecy, cut in 1733 and restored in 1757, makes a chord’s destination uncertain just when his future is being announced. Above ground, horns lead the hunt; flutes become blasts of wind when the sea monster appears. Rameau can be crueller still with a cheerful dance. Thésée, convinced that his son has betrayed him, must sit through a celebration of his safe return. The sailors dance their rigaudons while he waits to call down punishment.

1735–44Love in distant places

Les Indes galantes brought separate love stories to the stage in 1735, with Turkish, Peruvian and Persian settings. The North American act arrived in 1736. Its famous dance had begun at the harpsichord: Rameau wrote Les Sauvages after seeing two Indigenous visitors from Louisiana dance at the Comédie-Italienne in 1725. In the opera, Zima rejects both her French and Spanish suitors and chooses Adario. The rejected Europeans end by joining the celebration.

Two CastorsThe 1737 Castor et Pollux begins its main action beside Castor’s tomb. In the 1754 revision, a new first act shows him alive and brings about his death. Rameau also removed the prologue and rewrote large stretches of the remaining acts.

In Castor et Pollux (1737), two brothers love the same woman, and the immortal one must decide whether to take the mortal one’s place among the dead. Télaïre’s lament, Tristes apprêts, has a bassoon line whose dark sound stays close to her grief. Pollux’s sacrifice leads eventually to the brothers becoming stars. Dardanus followed in 1739, with such a troublesome libretto that Rameau returned in 1744 to replace much of the drama. He could be stubborn about a principle and ruthless with an opera.

1745–64A wedding, a quarrel, a north wind

Haute-contrePlatée is sung by a high tenor, the voice French opera also gave to its young male lovers. The same demanding range serves a heroine who becomes the gods’ practical joke.

Platée was performed at Versailles in 1745 during the Dauphin’s wedding celebrations. Jupiter pretends he will marry an ungainly marsh nymph to cure Juno of jealousy. Platée believes him; everyone else is in on it. Frogs croak around her, dances become absurd, and La Folie carries off a virtuoso singing display with manic assurance. The score is hilarious. The humiliation is real. That same year Rameau became a royal composer.

By the Querelle des Bouffons of 1752, the former disturber of French opera had become its chief defender. A visiting Italian company’s performances of Pergolesi’s La serva padrona set Paris arguing again. Rousseau championed Italian melody and attacked French musical speech; Rameau answered from his conviction that melody’s force rested on harmony. He kept composing through the dispute: the comic Les Paladins reached the stage in 1760.

Rehearsals for Les Boréades began in 1763 and stopped before a public performance. Its queen, Alphise, wants to marry the man she loves; the descendants of the north wind have other plans, and a storm tears through the festivities. Rameau died in Paris on 12 September 1764, just short of eighty-one. The opera waited until 1982 for its first full staging, at Aix-en-Provence under John Eliot Gardiner. The old man’s winds were finally let loose.

The Sound

♮Harmony

The Fates’ trio in Hippolyte et Aricie unsettles the ear by making a chord lead somewhere unexpected. L’Enharmonique performs a related trick at the harpsichord: a familiar pitch takes on a new spelling and points towards another key. Rameau uses the same keyboard notes as everyone else, then changes the destination at the moment of departure.

𝅘𝅥French speech

Phèdre’s confrontation with Hippolyte moves at the speed of an accusation. Short phrases, leaps and changes of harmony let her words wound before an aria has time to form. Rameau inherited French recitative from Lully, then asked more of the singer’s range and ornaments. A bass change can make an otherwise ordinary syllable suddenly dangerous.

𝄋Dance

In Act III of Hippolyte et Aricie, sailors celebrate Thésée’s return with rigaudons while he is burning with jealousy. The dancing delays his revenge, which makes its cheerfulness painful. Rameau’s gavottes, menuets and chaconnes have their own pleasure as instrumental pieces; in the theatre, the person standing beside the dancers can change everything they mean.

𝄞The orchestra

Horns announce the hunt in Hippolyte et Aricie; flutes become wind in its sea storm. The bassoon beside Télaïre in Tristes apprêts has an equally memorable voice at a much quieter pitch of grief. Rameau chooses an instrument for the work it can do in a scene, from an outdoor call to the sound of a private lament.

𝄢The hands

In Les Cyclopes, alternating hands imitate a drummer’s sticks, and the left hand crosses the right to reach the treble before returning to the bass. Rameau explains the technique in his 1724 preface. His A minor Gavotte keeps the same harmonic route through six doubles while quick notes and repeated attacks progressively take over the keyboard.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1706
    Premier Livre de pièces de clavecinHarpsichord
    An unmeasured prelude opens Rameau’s first published suite. Its freely timed beginning becomes a quick, regular dance before the allemandes begin.
  2. 1724
    Pièces de clavecinHarpsichord
    The two suites include the calling birds of Le Rappel des oiseaux and the hammering hands of Les Cyclopes. Rameau explains his new keyboard techniques in the preface.
  3. c. 1728
    Nouvelles Suites de pièces de clavecinHarpsichord
    La Poule makes a whole drama from a clucking repeated note. The collection also includes Les Sauvages, L’Enharmonique and the A minor Gavotte with six doubles.

  4. 1733
    Hippolyte et AricieTragédie en musique, soloists, chorus and orchestra
    His first opera, after Racine through Pellegrin. The Fates’ most adventurous trio had to be cut when the performers could not master it.
  5. 1735–36
    Les Indes galantesOpéra-ballet, soloists, chorus, dancers and orchestra
    Separate love stories set in Turkey, Peru, Persia and North America. The American act, added in 1736, reuses the harpsichord dance Les Sauvages.
  6. 1737; revised 1754
    Castor et PolluxTragédie en musique
    Pollux offers his immortality to save his brother. The first version opens at Castor’s tomb; the revision lets us meet him before he dies.
  7. 1739; revised 1744
    DardanusTragédie en musique
    A score Rameau refused to abandon when its libretto drew criticism. The 1744 revision rebuilt much of the action around the lovers.
  8. 1741
    Pièces de clavecin en concertsHarpsichord with violin or flute and bass viol or second violin
    Five chamber concerts with the harpsichord part written out in full. The keyboard has become a principal player, freed from merely supplying chords.
  9. 1745
    PlatéeComic stage work with high-tenor title role, chorus and orchestra
    A marsh nymph is promised Jupiter’s hand as a practical joke. Written for royal wedding festivities, with La Folie’s virtuoso scene at the centre of the mayhem.
  10. 1763
    Les BoréadesTragédie en musique in five acts
    The north wind interrupts a queen’s attempt to choose her own husband. Rehearsed in 1763, it received its first full staging at Aix-en-Provence in 1982.

Sister Arts

Rameau put his argument on sale in Paris: the Nouveau système of 1726 followed his Traité by four years. Print had made his name as a theorist. A year later he was writing to La Motte for an opera libretto, pointing to his harpsichord pieces and cantatas to persuade the poet that he could also write for the stage.

Jean Racine

Phèdre — Act I, scene 3

1677
FrenchJe le vis, je rougis, je pâlis à sa vue ; Un trouble s’éleva dans mon âme éperdue ; Mes yeux ne voyaient plus, je ne pouvais parler ; Je sentis tout mon corps et transir et brûler.
Our translationI saw him; I blushed, I turned pale at the sight; a disturbance rose in my bewildered soul; my eyes no longer saw, I could not speak; I felt my whole body both freeze and burn.

Phèdre remembers first seeing Hippolyte: she blushes, turns pale, loses her voice, freezes and burns. Pellegrin took Racine’s tragedy to Rameau in Hippolyte et Aricie, explaining in his 1733 preface why he had changed it. Gods could appear in an opera; the lovers could be saved. Phèdre still had to face what she had done.

Lineage

Drew fromJean-Baptiste LullyAndré CampraFrançois CouperinFrench dance and declamatory theatre
Rameau
Passed toChristoph Willibald GluckHector BerliozClaude DebussyMaurice Ravel

Lully supplied the five-act theatre, its French declamation and its dancers; Rameau filled it with more demanding harmonies and instrumental parts. Berlioz admired Télaïre’s Tristes apprêts. Debussy attended the 1903 revival of Castor et Pollux and later placed Hommage à Rameau at the centre of his first book of Images.
