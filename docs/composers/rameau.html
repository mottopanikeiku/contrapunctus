Jean-PhilippeRameau

Born 25 September 1683, DijonDied 12 September 1764, Paris

ILife

Rameau was born in Dijon, where his father worked as an organist. The documentation of his first four decades is much thinner than that of his Parisian operatic career. He held church appointments in several French cities, published a first book of harpsichord pieces in Paris in 1706, and worked again at Clermont-Ferrand before settling permanently in the capital in the early 1720s. This was a musician trained through keyboards, church duties and the analysis of musical practice, not an opera-house apprentice. By the time he wrote his first surviving opera he had already published the theoretical book for which his name was known.

Two kinds of bassRameau’s basse fondamentale is the succession of chord roots. It is not necessarily the lowest sounding part: C–E–G and E–G–C can have different bass notes while belonging to the same fundamental harmony.

1722Harmony as a system

The Traité de l’harmonie réduite à ses principes naturels appeared in 1722. Rameau proposed that chords and their connections could be understood through a fundamental bass, rather than treating every arrangement of intervals above the sounding bass as an unrelated object. Inversion became central: a chord could retain its identity while one of its upper notes moved into the bass. His explanations developed across later books, and his claims to a foundation in nature changed as he engaged with acoustics. The theory should not be mistaken for a complete description of his compositional practice; it is an attempt to explain why harmonic progressions cohere.

The harpsichord books of 1724 and about 1728 already make harmony an event that a listener can register without reading a treatise. L’Enharmonique turns the interpretation of pitches into a change of tonal direction. Les Cyclopes brings the hands into rapid alternation; La Poule builds a character from a repeated-note figure. Rameau retained French dances and ornament signs, but gave keyboard technique a more exposed role than in much of Couperin. Register, repetition and the weight of chords can become the piece’s subject. His later Pièces de clavecin en concerts place a fully written keyboard part at the centre of a chamber ensemble.

1733A new composer at the Opéra

The financier Alexandre Le Riche de La Pouplinière became a major patron, providing access to performers, a household orchestra and literary society. Rameau’s first opera, Hippolyte et Aricie, reached the Académie royale de musique in October 1733, shortly after his fiftieth birthday. Simon-Joseph Pellegrin’s libretto reworked the world of Racine’s Phèdre, but expanded the supernatural machinery and allowed the young lovers to survive. The dispute that followed was not about whether the work belonged to French opera: it retained recitative, chorus, dance and five-act architecture. The issue was how much harmonic and instrumental complexity those established forms could carry.

The underworld scene makes that complexity dramatic. The Fates’ trio confronted performers with difficult enharmonic writing; Rameau’s score makes uncertainty about a character’s destiny audible as uncertainty about a chord’s direction. Elsewhere, storms, hunts and ceremonies require the orchestra to do more than double singers. French recitative remains responsive to the language’s stresses, but can change harmonic position rapidly beneath a short phrase. The resulting theatre does not separate musical construction from spectacle: a dance can interrupt, complete or redirect a scene rather than merely occupy the interval between important arias.

Les Indes galantes followed in 1735. Its separate entrées place love stories in settings imagined as Turkish, Peruvian, Persian and North American; the final entrée was added in 1736. These are French theatrical constructions of distant societies, not accounts of those societies’ music. Rameau reused his keyboard piece Les Sauvages in the American entrée, bringing an earlier response to a Parisian performance into a new dramatic context. Castor et Pollux, first performed in 1737 and extensively revised in 1754, instead uses the rivalry and devotion of brothers to organise a continuous tragedy. The two versions redistribute the story’s emotional and musical weight.

Court and controversyComedy beside tragedy

Rameau wrote Platée for the festivities surrounding the Dauphin’s marriage at Versailles in 1745. A high tenor plays a marsh nymph deceived into believing Jupiter will marry her; the gods stage the deception to cure Juno’s jealousy. Repeated syllables, animal noises and exaggerated dance patterns turn operatic eloquence into a comic instrument, while the character La Folie displays virtuosity within the satire. The cruelty of the plot is part of the work, not cancelled by the comic genre. Rameau’s court appointment in the same year confirmed his institutional position, but did not end controversy over his musical principles.

The Querelle des BouffonsThe Paris performances of Italian comic opera in 1752 sharpened arguments over French and Italian music. Rousseau’s attack on French musical language and Rameau’s defence of harmony were connected disputes, not a simple contest between two composers’ styles.

During the 1750s he revised earlier operas while continuing theoretical arguments with writers including Rousseau. His position was not that melody had no value, but that its musical effects depended on harmonic relations. That distinction matters when listening: the orchestral bass and inner parts can change the force of a vocal phrase without changing its outline. The revised Castor et Pollux and Dardanus also show that he did not treat a first version as inviolable. Dramatic order, recitative and whole movements could be rewritten in response to the theatre’s needs.

Les Paladins appeared in 1760. Les Boréades, rehearsed in 1763 but not publicly staged in Rameau’s lifetime, remained outside the repertory for generations. Its plot joins dynastic coercion, a forbidden marriage and the force of the north wind; its dances and orchestral scenes require sustained coordination rather than decorative interludes. Rameau died in Paris in 1764, almost eighty-one. The modern recovery of these scores has depended on singers able to articulate French declamation, dancers able to make the metres visible, and orchestras willing to treat articulation, unequal rhythms and ornament as compositional information.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Harmonic direction

A chord’s root matters even when it is not in the bass. Rameau uses inversions to let the sounding bass move smoothly while the fundamental progression remains clear. Chromatic alterations and enharmonic reinterpretations can then change the expected destination. These turns are particularly forceful in supernatural or emotionally unstable scenes.

𝅘𝅥French declamation

Recitative changes note values and sometimes metre to follow verbal stress. Cadences articulate the sentence; the continuo and orchestra can colour a word without requiring the singer to expand it into an aria. Listen for the difference between a syllable given a long note for rhetorical weight and one extended by ornamental singing.

𝄋Dance

Gavottes, menuets, rigaudons, musettes and chaconnes are structural parts of the operas. Each supplies a different relation between upbeat, accent and phrase length. Rameau can preserve the recognisable step pattern while changing register, orchestration or harmonic tension across repeated strains.

𝄞Orchestration

Flutes, oboes and bassoons contribute independent lines and distinctive attacks rather than a continuous coating over the strings. Storms and hunts use repetition, register and instrumental mass; more intimate scenes can isolate a wind colour. The orchestral number often performs dramatic work that an Italian opera might entrust to a solo aria.

𝄢Keyboard mechanics

Hand crossings, rapid alternation and repeated notes can be the audible premise of a piece. The Pièces de clavecin en concerts reverse the usual continuo hierarchy: the harpsichord part is fully composed, while the accompanying instruments contribute to a keyboard-centred texture. On a piano, extra sustain must not obscure the separation of attacks.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1706
    Premier Livre de pièces de clavecinHarpsichord
    A suite beginning with a prelude that retains an unmeasured opening. The French dance tradition supplies the collection’s framework.
  2. 1724
    Pièces de clavecinHarpsichord
    Includes Le Rappel des oiseaux, Les Tendres Plaintes, Les Cyclopes and the E minor suite. Rameau also explains aspects of keyboard technique.
  3. c. 1728
    Nouvelles Suites de pièces de clavecinHarpsichord
    Includes La Poule, Les Sauvages, L’Enharmonique and the Gavotte with six doubles: a repeated harmonic design becomes a succession of technical problems.
  4. 1733
    Hippolyte et AricieTragédie en musique, soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Rameau’s first opera, with a libretto by Pellegrin after Racine and classical sources. The underworld scene makes harmonic instability part of the drama.
  5. 1735–36
    Les Indes galantesOpéra-ballet, soloists, chorus, dancers and orchestra
    A prologue and separate entrées in imagined non-European settings. The North American entrée, Les Sauvages, was added in 1736.
  6. 1737; revised 1754
    Castor et PolluxTragédie en musique
    Brotherly devotion and romantic rivalry lead from mourning to the underworld and apotheosis. The two principal versions differ substantially in dramatic construction.
  7. 1739; revised 1744
    DardanusTragédie en musique
    An opera repeatedly reworked after objections to its libretto. Its instrumental and vocal scenes should be heard within the chosen version, not assembled as a supposedly definitive text.
  8. 1741
    Pièces de clavecin en concertsHarpsichord with violin or flute and bass viol or second violin
    Five concerts in which the written-out keyboard part leads rather than merely realising continuo. Several movements carry the names of people in Rameau’s circle.
  9. 1745
    PlatéeComic stage work with high-tenor title role, chorus and orchestra
    A divine deception staged for a marsh nymph. La Folie’s scene makes virtuoso singing itself part of the comedy.
  10. 1763
    Les BoréadesTragédie en musique in five acts
    Rehearsed but not publicly staged during Rameau’s lifetime. Dynastic restrictions and a storm generated by Boreas give the orchestra a central dramatic role.

Jean Racine

Phèdre — Act I, scene 3

1677
FrenchJe le vis, je rougis, je pâlis à sa vue ; Un trouble s’éleva dans mon âme éperdue ; Mes yeux ne voyaient plus, je ne pouvais parler ; Je sentis tout mon corps et transir et brûler.
Our translationI saw him; I blushed, I turned pale at the sight; a disturbance rose in my bewildered soul; my eyes no longer saw, I could not speak; I felt my whole body both freeze and burn.

Pellegrin’s Hippolyte et Aricie draws on Racine’s tragedy, but these lines are not sung in Rameau’s opera. Racine makes Phèdre recount desire through failures of bodily control; the opera redistributes the story among recitative, supernatural scenes, dances and a surviving pair of lovers. Pellegrin discusses his debt and his alterations in the 1733 libretto’s preface. The difference between the two works is an adaptation for another medium, not a musical setting of the play unchanged.

VIILineage

Drew fromJean-Baptiste LullyAndré CampraFrançois CouperinFrench dance and declamatory theatre
Rameau
Passed toChristoph Willibald GluckHector BerliozClaude DebussyMaurice Ravel

Rameau retained the institutions and genres of Lully’s French opera while expanding the harmonic and orchestral work performed inside them. Debussy explicitly honoured him in Hommage à Rameau, the central piece of the first book of Images. The connection is a considered tribute to an older French musical tradition, not a claim that Debussy accepted Rameau’s harmonic theory.
