ILife

György Ligeti was born into a Hungarian-speaking Jewish family in Transylvania, a region transferred from Hungary to Romania after the First World War. His family moved to Cluj, known in Hungarian as Kolozsvár, when he was a child. He studied composition there with Ferenc Farkas, but in 1944 the Hungarian authorities sent him to a forced-labour unit. His father and younger brother were murdered in the Holocaust; his mother survived Auschwitz. Ligeti survived the labour service and resumed his studies in Budapest in 1945. The catastrophes belong to his biography, but his later scores do not offer a single programme through which to translate them into music.

At the Liszt Academy he studied with Sándor Veress and Farkas, graduating in 1949 and subsequently teaching harmony and counterpoint. Bartók was the chief musical reference. Under Hungary’s Stalinist government, however, even parts of Bartók’s output were politically suspect; Western broadcasts provided irregular access to newer music. Ligeti earned a living with teaching and folk-song arrangements while writing more experimental pieces privately. Musica ricercata, composed in 1951–53, begins almost entirely on A, introducing D only at the end of its first movement. Each succeeding piece permits one additional pitch class, until the eleventh uses all twelve.

Not quite one noteThe first piece of Musica ricercata is often called a one-note composition. It has two pitch classes, A and the concluding D. Octave placement, attack, rhythm and acceleration supply contrasts before the second pitch class arrives.

1956–61From Budapest to the electronic studio

After Soviet forces suppressed the Hungarian uprising in 1956, Ligeti and his wife Vera escaped to Austria. He soon reached Cologne, where the West German Radio electronic studio offered contact with Karlheinz Stockhausen and Gottfried Michael Koenig. Tape work let him study sounds too short or too close together to be heard as separate events. Artikulation, completed in 1958, assembles electronically produced noises and tones into something resembling speech, without words. The studio did not turn him permanently into an electronic composer: it helped him imagine what an orchestra could do with density, register and minute differences of timing.

Apparitions and Atmosphères made that orchestral language public. Hans Rosbaud conducted the premiere of Atmosphères at Donaueschingen in 1961. Instead of a tune accompanied by chords, the score offers closely spaced pitches whose internal movement alters the collective sound. Each string player can have a separate part, while individual attacks are concealed. A mass can become brighter because low notes drop away, or narrower because outer voices move inward. These are composed changes of harmony and orchestration, not a performer’s improvisation over an undifferentiated cluster.

MicropolyphonyLigeti’s term describes many independent contrapuntal lines packed so closely that the ear hears their aggregate. The technique depends on detailed notation. It is different from simply asking everyone to hold neighbouring notes.

Voices and mechanismsPrayer without a congregation

The Requiem of 1963–65 sets only part of the Latin service. Its Kyrie makes a densely divided choir into a fugue whose individual subjects are difficult to extract; the following ‘De die judicii sequentia’ breaks that continuity with more violent contrasts. Lux aeterna, written in 1966 for sixteen unaccompanied voices, extends the quiet side of this choral writing. Entrances are staggered, syllables overlap, and changes of vowel alter the blend. Stanley Kubrick used both works, with Atmosphères and material from Aventures, in 2001: A Space Odyssey. Ligeti had not written the music for the film and was not initially consulted about its use.

Not all his 1960s music conceals attack. The Poème symphonique of 1962 sets one hundred mechanical metronomes running at different speeds until they stop. With fewer clocks left, rhythmic patterns emerge from the initial congestion. Continuum for harpsichord, written in 1968, produces another paradox: notes are struck so rapidly that patterns seem to move at a speed different from the player’s basic motion. The Second String Quartet and Chamber Concerto move between sustained fields, jerking gestures and mechanisms that lose synchronisation. Ligeti’s humour is often a property of timing: a machine behaves almost, but not quite, as expected.

The theatreDeath visits Breughelland

Appointed professor of composition in Hamburg in 1973, Ligeti devoted much of the following years to Le Grand Macabre. Its libretto, made with Michael Meschke after Michel de Ghelderode’s play, places a threatened apocalypse in the disreputable country of Breughelland. The Stockholm premiere took place in 1978. Car horns replace an operatic overture’s ceremonial instruments; tonal fragments, distorted marches and virtuoso vocal writing coexist. Nekrotzar announces universal death, but the ending leaves his authority in doubt. Ligeti revised the opera for its 1997 Salzburg production, replacing spoken dialogue with sung passages and making other changes to the score.

The Horn Trio of 1982, subtitled ‘Hommage à Brahms’, surprised listeners who expected another continuation of the 1960s. Its violin, horn and piano match Brahms’s ensemble, and its movements include a march and a lament. Yet natural horn harmonics disturb the piano’s tempered pitches, and recurrent figures are displaced rather than allowed to settle. This was not a restoration of nineteenth-century harmonic practice. Ligeti was bringing melody, recognisable gestures and pulse back into a music still governed by mismatched systems.

Borrowing a procedureLigeti studied Central African polyphony through recordings and Simha Arom’s research. He did not simply orchestrate those recordings: interlocking parts and simultaneous metres became problems to solve with a pianist’s two hands or a European ensemble.

The late workshopHands, clocks and tuning

The piano études, begun in 1985, combine several kinds of technical research. Conlon Nancarrow’s player-piano studies suggested independent speeds; African ensemble music suggested interlocking rhythmic layers; the keyboard itself suggested separating white and black keys. In ‘Désordre’, the hands’ accented patterns fall out of alignment even though the small notes keep moving. In ‘Arc-en-ciel’, shifting accents and extended chords admit the influence of jazz. Pierre-Laurent Aimard became a principal interpreter and worked closely with Ligeti on this repertory. Across three books, eighteen études turn physical playing problems into audible forms.

The late concertos make disagreement between instruments equally productive. The Piano Concerto combines rapid rhythmic layers with unusual colours including slide whistle and ocarina. The Violin Concerto places its soloist beside an ensemble containing retuned strings and ocarinas, so that equal temperament is only one available tuning. The Hamburg Concerto extends the natural-horn investigation. Ligeti retired from his Hamburg post in 1989, but continued revising and composing into the early 2000s. Illness increasingly restricted his work before his death in Vienna in 2006; his final period left young composers models of complex rhythm that do not depend on abandoning the listener’s sense of pulse.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄢Micropolyphony

Canon remains present even when no single line can be followed. In Lux aeterna and the Requiem Kyrie, voices enter at different times and move through overlapping pitches. The listener hears density, register and harmonic colour changing; the performers read precise contrapuntal parts. Texture is the result of counterpoint, not its replacement.

♮Harmonic fields

A cluster is a collection of adjacent pitches. Ligeti differentiates clusters by width, register, spacing and instrumentation, and by the pitches they gradually admit or exclude. Lontano lets recognisable intervals and harmonic regions emerge from densely moving lines, without turning them into an ordinary chord progression.

𝅘𝅥Interlocking rhythm

Several accent cycles can share a fast underlying pulse without sharing a bar-length. In the études, the hands may seem to play at different speeds because their accents recur at different distances. The listener follows more than one clock; the pianist must maintain the small-note mechanism beneath both.

𝄪Unequal tuning

Natural horn harmonics, retuned string instruments and ocarinas disturb the piano’s twelve equally divided semitones. The late concertos do not replace one tuning system with another: they make systems coexist. A note that sounds misplaced against the orchestra may be acoustically correct within the horn’s harmonic series.

𝄞The voice without words

Aventures and Nouvelles Aventures notate invented phonetic material, breaths and changes of vocal delivery instead of a semantic text. Laughter, fear and ceremony can be suggested by articulation alone. Le Grand Macabre restores an intelligible libretto but keeps this interest in the voice as an acrobatic, unstable instrument.

𝄋Limits as form

Musica ricercata makes a new pitch class a structural event by withholding it. Continuum asks how discrete attacks become a continuous sensation. The études use restrictions of fingering, register or accent to produce development; a piece’s rule is often audible before its notation is understood.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1951–53
    Musica ricercataPiano
    Eleven pieces, progressing from two pitch classes to twelve. The last is a contrapuntal homage to Frescobaldi; six movements were recast as the Six Bagatelles for wind quintet.
  2. 1953–54
    String Quartet No. 1 “Métamorphoses nocturnes”String quartet
    A continuous sequence of transformations, still close to Bartók in its intervallic cells, dance rhythms and contrasting bowing techniques. First performed after Ligeti had left Hungary.
  3. 1961
    AtmosphèresLarge orchestra
    A work of changing density and register in which separate instrumental parts merge into collective sonorities. The opening chromatic mass contains no conventionally foregrounded melody.
  4. 1963–65
    RequiemSoprano, mezzo-soprano, mixed chorus and orchestra
    Four movements: Introitus, Kyrie, De die judicii sequentia and Lacrimosa. Closely packed choral imitation gives way to extreme registral and dynamic contrasts.
  5. 1966
    Lux aeternaSixteen unaccompanied mixed voices
    Quiet canonic entrances create a continuously changing choral blend. Breath, vowel and the timing of consonants are as consequential as the pitches.
  6. 1967
    LontanoOrchestra
    Canonic writing produces changes of harmonic perspective. Recognisable intervals seem to emerge at different distances without becoming a tune with accompaniment.
  7. 1974–77; revised 1996–97
    Le Grand MacabreOpera in two acts; libretto by Ligeti and Michael Meschke after Michel de Ghelderode
    An apocalypse in Breughelland, announced by a figure whose power may be fraudulent. Car horns, an extensive percussion section and extreme vocal registers give the comedy its material substance.
  8. 1982
    Trio for Violin, Horn and PianoViolin, horn and piano
    Subtitled ‘Hommage à Brahms’. Four movements bring march, dance and lament into contact with displaced accents and the horn’s unequally tuned natural harmonics.
  9. 1985–2001
    Études pour pianoPiano; eighteen études in three books
    Independent accent cycles, blocked keys, repeated notes and competing registers turn keyboard technique into formal design. ‘Désordre’, ‘Fanfares’ and ‘L’escalier du diable’ are useful contrasting entrances.
  10. 1989–93
    Violin ConcertoSolo violin and chamber orchestra
    Five movements in the final version. Retuned orchestral strings, natural harmonics and ocarinas complicate the solo violin’s relation to the ensemble; the second movement recalls material from the early piano music.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Le Grand Macabre takes place in Breughelland, the name inherited from Michel de Ghelderode’s play. Bruegel’s crowded panorama makes the relationship specific: soldiers, lovers, clergy and aristocrats occupy the same field while death overrides their distinctions. This is a visual counterpart to the opera’s setting, not a claim that Ligeti transcribed this painting. His ending also departs from its apparent finality: Nekrotzar’s promised annihilation may have failed.

Latin Requiem liturgy

Lux aeterna

Traditional Latin text; set by Ligeti in 1966
LatinLux aeterna luceat eis, Domine, cum sanctis tuis in aeternum, quia pius es. Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine, et lux perpetua luceat eis.
Our translationMay eternal light shine upon them, Lord, with your saints for ever, for you are merciful. Grant them eternal rest, Lord, and may perpetual light shine upon them.

The Communion text of the Mass for the Dead is the source of Ligeti’s sixteen-part motet. Its promise of light differs from the judgement imagery dominating his Requiem. He repeats and overlaps its words, so the printed sentence and the heard sequence are not identical: individual syllables become part of a shifting choral harmony before a whole phrase can be understood.

VIILineage

Drew fromBéla BartókClaude DebussyAnton WebernConlon NancarrowCentral African polyphony
Ligeti
Passed toKaija SaariahoThomas AdèsUnsuk Chin

Bartók supplied intervallic economy and rhythmic models; Webern supplied an example of concentration that Ligeti could extend or resist. His orchestral fields became a reference for composers including Saariaho, while the late études offered Adès and others a different route into complex pulse. Unsuk Chin studied with Ligeti in Hamburg; her concertos continue the investigation of instrumental virtuosity without reproducing his harmonic language.
