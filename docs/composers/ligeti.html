Born 28 May 1923, Diciosânmartin, Transylvania, Romania (now Târnăveni)Died 12 June 2006, Vienna

Life

In December 1956, György Ligeti and Vera, his former wife, hid beneath mailbags on a train out of Budapest. Soviet troops had crushed the uprising; the last stretch into Austria had to be crossed through mud. Recalling the escape to Christopher Ford in The Guardian on 7 May 1974, Ligeti said: “We went across on foot. Four foot.” He made a crawling gesture. He was thirty-three. He and Vera remarried the following year.

1923–56Music for the drawer

He had been born in Diciosânmartin, Transylvania, into a Hungarian-speaking Jewish family. They moved to Cluj, where he studied composition with Ferenc Farkas from 1941. Music was already a second choice imposed on him: he had wanted to study mathematics and physics, but the university’s quota for Jewish applicants kept him out. War interrupted the conservatory course. Ligeti was sent into forced labour; his father and younger brother were murdered in concentration camps. His mother survived Auschwitz.

In 1945 he went to Budapest and resumed composition studies with Farkas and Sándor Veress. At the Liszt Academy, where he joined the teaching staff in 1950, he learnt and taught the exact craft of putting one voice against another. Bartók supplied a more recent example, though the Stalinist authorities banned much of his music along with Webern and Schoenberg. Ligeti made folk-song arrangements that could be published and wrote other music that could scarcely leave his desk. His First String Quartet waited until 1958 for its première, in Vienna.

Two notesA pitch class includes every octave of the same note. The first piece of Musica ricercata has two: A, which occupies almost the whole piece, and D, reserved for the end.

At a cousin’s piano he worked on Musica ricercata (1951–53), eleven pieces made from a deliberately restricted supply of notes. In the first, A is hammered, repeated in octaves and driven faster and faster until D finally arrives. Each subsequent piece allows another pitch class; the last uses all twelve in a homage to Frescobaldi. A waltz, a lament and a memorial to Bartók fit inside this little laboratory. The cycle was banned.

1957–61Scissors, tape and strings

Cologne offered him sounds he had struggled to hear on Western broadcasts in Hungary. At the WDR electronic studio he worked alongside Gottfried Michael Koenig and Karlheinz Stockhausen, cutting and joining tape with scissors and glue. Artikulation (1958) makes electronic sounds behave like an invented language: something seems to speak, though there are no words. He would later carry that idea into Aventures and Nouvelles Aventures, for three singers and seven instrumentalists.

The orchestra could do things the tape studio had helped him hear. Apparitions brought him to international attention in 1960; Atmosphères followed at Donaueschingen in October 1961, conducted by Hans Rosbaud. It opens with a quiet cluster spanning almost five octaves. Later, twenty-eight violins descend while twenty violas and cellos climb, squeezing the space between them down to a minor third. Forty-eight lines are moving, yet the ear catches the slow tightening of one immense chord.

MicropolyphonyLigeti’s name for counterpoint packed too closely for its individual lines to stand out. In the Requiem Kyrie, five groups of singers each divide into a four-part canon: twenty lines in all.

Explaining this music to Ford in 1974, Ligeti picked at his jacket sleeve: “Here are different threads, green, blue, yellow – but it gives the one surface.” The cloth was his demonstration of micropolyphony. Beneath the apparently motionless sound lay the sort of carefully interwoven parts he had studied in Budapest. In Lontano (1967), those parts share more familiar intervals and double one another at the octave. The harmony comes into view through the orchestral mist.

1962–68Choirs, clocks and a cinema

The Requiem (1963–65) places its twenty-part Kyrie beside a violently broken-up judgement scene. Ligeti had the apocalyptic pictures of Memling, Bruegel and Bosch in mind; the soprano and mezzo-soprano confront a choir large enough to withstand the orchestra. He set only selected parts of the service. In 1966 he returned separately to its Communion text for Lux aeterna. Sixteen unaccompanied voices enter almost imperceptibly; at the first Domine, the basses appear high in falsetto. The final notes fade, and the conductor continues beating seven silent bars.

He also wrote music for one hundred metronomes. In the Poème symphonique of 1962, they are wound up, set to different speeds and allowed to run down. The initial clatter thins into patterns; eventually a single machine is left ticking. Continuum (1968) works the other way round. A harpsichordist plays so quickly that separate plucks merge into a buzzing, almost stationary sound. Both pieces make the ear struggle pleasantly with what the hands or mechanisms are actually doing.

Then Kubrick put Atmosphères, the Requiem and Lux aeterna into 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968). Ligeti learnt about the film from a friend and attended its Vienna opening. Nobody had consulted him. He went back with a stopwatch and counted more than half an hour of his music in the soundtrack. The eventual payment was $3,000. An American publisher subsequently asked him to describe his collaboration with Kubrick for a book about the film.

1973–82Death misses his appointment

From 1973 Ligeti taught composition in Hamburg, while working on Le Grand Macabre. He and Michael Meschke adapted Michel de Ghelderode’s play about a threatened apocalypse in Breughelland. Car horns open the opera. Nekrotzar announces the world’s end, encounters a court of drunks and schemers, and drinks too much himself; the population survives his appointed catastrophe. Elgar Howarth conducted the Stockholm première in 1978. Ligeti returned to the score for a revised version first staged in Salzburg in 1997.

After the opera came several lean years, then the Horn Trio of 1982, subtitled Hommage à Brahms. Its violin, horn and piano use Brahms’s combination, but the three players keep slipping out of step. A fast dance divides eight beats in competing ways; the march also misaligns its parts. In the final Lamento, descending chromatic lines tighten around a recurring five-bar pattern, and the low piano becomes a huge imaginary drum. The horn answers its dying resonance.

1985–2006What two hands can do

He was collecting new rhythmic possibilities. In 1980 he encountered Conlon Nancarrow’s player-piano music and bought two recordings in Paris, fascinated by the simultaneous speeds. In 1982 his former pupil Roberto Sierra introduced him to Simha Arom’s recordings of Central African ensembles. Ligeti could play these records again and again. Later, discussing African lamellophones, he explained how a player’s two hands could suggest separate instruments: a problem he wanted to bring to the piano keyboard.

The eighteen piano études, written between 1985 and 2001, made such problems into pieces. In Désordre, the right hand takes white keys and the left black ones; their accents begin together and drift apart. Ligeti himself could play most of the studies only slowly. “Everything is playable. I know my trade!” he told Bruno Serrou in a late-1996 interview for Piano. Pierre-Laurent Aimard became a leading interpreter of these works, bringing their apparently incompatible demands under two human hands.

Natural harmonicsA horn’s uncorrected upper notes fall between some of the piano’s equally spaced semitones. Ligeti writes these differences into the music; the players are meant to disagree.

The late concertos extended the experiment beyond the keyboard. In the Violin Concerto, an orchestral violin and viola are retuned, horns play uncorrected natural notes, and four woodwind players take up ocarinas. The Hamburg Concerto surrounds its horn soloist with four natural horns. At eighty, speaking to Tom Service in The Guardian on 17 October 2003, Ligeti was still explaining why he had added its seventh movement: “With me, the plan and the piece develop at the same rate.” Illness curtailed his composing. He died in Vienna on 12 June 2006.

The Sound

𝄢Hidden canons

In the Requiem Kyrie, five choral groups each divide into four parts, following one another through a closely woven canon. The twenty lines become too entangled to pick out singly. Their notes remain exact: instruments reinforce the groups’ entries to help the singers find their pitches. This is micropolyphony, in which moving voices can make a choir seem to stand still.

♮Orchestral colour

Atmosphères begins with neighbouring semitones spread across almost five octaves. Within a later cluster, changes of loudness bring first the white-note pitches, then the black-note pitches forward. Harmony changes as particular instruments emerge. One sudden cut carries the music from piercing piccolos to double basses six octaves below; at the end, percussionists brush the strings inside a piano.

𝅘𝅥Displaced accents

The hands in Désordre share a quick underlying pulse, with white keys in the right hand and black keys in the left. Shortening a phrase by a quaver puts their accented notes out of step. The fingers keep moving evenly while the tune seems to stumble, hurry and pull away from itself.

𝄪Unequal tuning

The Violin Concerto puts normally tuned strings beside one retuned violin and one retuned viola. In two movements, the horns use uncorrected natural harmonics, whose upper notes disagree with equal temperament. Four woodwind players also play ocarinas. These instruments make an interval waver or roughen where an ordinary orchestra would smooth it out; the solo violin has several competing versions of being in tune.

𝄞Invented speech

Aventures and Nouvelles Aventures give a soprano, alto and baritone phonetic texts without dictionary meanings. An exchange can sound intimate or furious before any intelligible word appears. Seven instrumentalists join this little theatre. In Le Grand Macabre, the Chief of the Secret Political Police inherits its frantic vocal manner: a coloratura soprano entrusted with urgent, half-nonsensical messages.

𝄋Keyboard experiments

The first Musica ricercata piece extracts drama from repeated As: octaves, stronger attacks and increasing speed make the eventual D an event. Continuum finds another extreme at the harpsichord. Each hand works a separate manual so rapidly that successive plucks fuse. The instrument can scarcely sustain a note, yet Ligeti makes its mechanism produce a nearly continuous hum.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1951–53
    Musica ricercataPiano
    Eleven pieces that increase their allowance of pitch classes from two to twelve. The last honours Frescobaldi; six of the others became the Six Bagatelles for wind quintet.
  2. 1953–54
    String Quartet No. 1 “Métamorphoses nocturnes”String quartet
    A quartet from the Hungarian years, with Bartók still close at hand. The Ramor Quartet first performed it in Vienna in May 1958, after Ligeti had escaped to the West.
  3. 1961
    AtmosphèresLarge orchestra
    A quiet cluster spanning almost five octaves opens a score in which forty-eight string parts can move at once. Hans Rosbaud conducted the Donaueschingen première; the dedication remembers the composer Mátyás Seiber.
  4. 1963–65
    RequiemSoprano, mezzo-soprano, mixed chorus and orchestra
    The Kyrie divides the choir into twenty contrapuntal parts; the judgement sequence breaks into wild dramatic exchanges. In the closing Lacrimosa, the two soloists remain with a reduced orchestra.
  5. 1966
    Lux aeternaSixteen unaccompanied mixed voices
    The first bass entry is in falsetto, on Domine, above the softly overlapping choir. Seven bars of conducted silence follow the last audible notes.
  6. 1967
    LontanoOrchestra
    Unison canons and octave doublings soften the orchestral edges; recognisable intervals appear inside the closely written parts. Ernest Bour conducted its first performance at Donaueschingen.
  7. 1974–77; revised 1996
    Le Grand MacabreOpera in four scenes; libretto by Ligeti and Michael Meschke after Michel de Ghelderode
    Car horns announce an apocalypse which gets entangled in drinking, sex and court politics. The first production was in Stockholm in 1978; the revised version reached Salzburg in 1997.
  8. 1982
    Trio for Violin, Horn and PianoViolin, horn and piano
    A homage to Brahms whose dance and march repeatedly put the players out of step. The final Lamento builds descending chromatic lines around a five-bar recurring pattern.
  9. 1985–2001
    Études pour pianoPiano; eighteen études in three books
    In Désordre, white-key and black-key patterns slip apart; in Touches bloquées, silently held keys leave holes in a running line. Ligeti admitted that he could play most of the études only slowly.
  10. 1989–93
    Violin ConcertoSolo violin and chamber orchestra
    Five movements with retuned strings, natural horn notes and a quartet of ocarinas. The second movement’s singing line reaches back to the seventh piece of Musica ricercata.

Sister Arts

At the lower right, a man plays the lute while a woman sings; a skeleton joins their music. Around them, Bruegel’s army of death overruns every rank of society. Ghelderode drew on these paintings for the Breughelland of Le Grand Macabre. Ligeti’s Nekrotzar brings the same threat into a world of lovers and drinkers, then becomes too drunk to carry it through.

Latin Requiem liturgy

Lux aeterna

Traditional Latin text; set by Ligeti in 1966
LatinLux aeterna luceat eis, Domine, cum sanctis tuis in aeternum, quia pius es. Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine, et lux perpetua luceat eis.
Our translationLet eternal light shine on them, Lord, with your saints for ever, for you are merciful. Give them eternal rest, Lord, and let perpetual light shine on them.

These words belong to the Communion of the Mass for the Dead, a section Ligeti had left out of his Requiem. He set them separately in 1966 for Clytus Gottwald’s Schola Cantorum Stuttgart. The sixteen parts overlap their syllables until Domine comes forward in the bass voices: first in falsetto, later low down.

  • Musica ricercata · Piano ÉtudesPierre-Laurent AimardSony ClassicalThe best starting point for the piano music: Aimard combines firm rhythmic control with a lightness that lets the quieter studies float. This volume contains Musica ricercata and the first fifteen études, through White on White.
  • Cello, Violin and Piano ConcertosJean-Guihen Queyras, Saschko Gawriloff, Pierre-Laurent Aimard; Ensemble intercontemporain, Pierre BoulezDeutsche GrammophonA forceful concerto collection, especially for Aimard’s hard-driven piano playing. Gawriloff, for whom Ligeti wrote the Violin Concerto, makes the coupling particularly valuable; the three works span the choral-like stillness of the 1960s and the later rhythmic machinery.

Lineage

Ligeti
Passed toKaija SaariahoThomas AdèsUnsuk Chin

Bartók stands close behind the Hungarian works; Debussy remained a model for Ligeti’s piano writing. Nancarrow’s records and Simha Arom’s Central African recordings joined that education in the 1980s. In Hamburg, Unsuk Chin studied with him from 1985 to 1988. He encouraged her to look beyond the prevailing avant-garde.
