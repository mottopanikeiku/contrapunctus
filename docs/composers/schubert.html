Composers / III. Romanticism

FranzSchubert

Born 31 January 1797, Himmelpfortgrund, ViennaDied 19 November 1828, Vienna

A Viennese songwriter who made the piano an equal narrator, and carried the harmonic dislocations of the Lied into symphonies and chamber music.

Trained
Vienna Stadtkonvikt, 1808–13; composition with Antonio Salieri, 1812–16
Worked
Assistant in his father’s school; music tutor to the Esterházy family at Zseliz in 1818 and 1824; thereafter chiefly an independent composer
Circle
Johann Michael Vogl · Josef von Spaun · Franz von Schober · Johann Mayrhofer · Moritz von Schwind
Output
More than 600 songs; symphonies, chamber music, piano sonatas and duets, masses and music for the stage

ILife

Schubert grew up in a schoolmaster’s household in Himmelpfortgrund, then a suburb of Vienna. His father taught him violin, his brother Ignaz taught him piano, and the parish musician Michael Holzer supplied further instruction. Family quartet sessions put Franz on viola, between the melody and the bass: he knew chamber music from an inner part. In 1808 he entered the imperial chapel choir and the Stadtkonvikt boarding school. Its orchestra introduced him to Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven as a player, while Antonio Salieri began giving him composition lessons in 1812.

After leaving the school in 1813, he trained as a teacher and became an assistant in his father’s classroom. Composition continued around the timetable. Gretchen am Spinnrade, dated 19 October 1814, gives Goethe’s girl a right-hand spinning wheel, a left-hand treadle and a vocal line that repeatedly tries to escape them. Schubert was seventeen. In 1815 he wrote roughly 140 songs, including Erlkönig, as well as symphonies, church music and stage works. The songs did not wait for an instrumental apprenticeship to finish: voice and piano already carried distinct dramatic responsibilities.

Songs and roomsA profession without a post

A SchubertiadeThe name describes gatherings centred on Schubert’s music, not a fixed concert format. Songs, piano duets, reading and dancing could share an evening. Schwind’s familiar group portrait was drawn in 1868: remembrance, not an eyewitness record made on the night.

Friends helped him loosen his dependence on schoolteaching. Franz von Schober offered lodging; Josef von Spaun found supporters; the poet Johann Mayrhofer supplied texts and, from 1818 to 1820, shared rooms with him. The painter Moritz von Schwind belonged to the same network. In 1817 Schubert met the opera singer Johann Michael Vogl, whose performances brought the songs beyond their original domestic audience. Friends financed the publication of Erlkönig as Op. 1 in 1821. This was not Schubert’s first song in print, but a more substantial commercial beginning, supported by subscriptions rather than an established publisher’s confidence.

The summers of 1818 and 1824 took him to Zseliz, now Želiezovce in Slovakia, to teach the daughters of Count Johann Karl Esterházy. Four-hand piano music belonged to this teaching and domestic world; the player beside him was a participant rather than an audience. Schubert also pursued the theatre, where a successful opera promised money and public recognition. Alfonso und Estrella and Fierrabras remained unstaged during his lifetime. The play Rosamunde, with his incidental music, closed after two performances in December 1823. His livelihood increasingly depended on selling songs and piano pieces.

1822–24Unfinished scores, continuing work

The B minor Symphony of 1822 stops after two fully orchestrated movements, with an incomplete scherzo surviving. Neither Schubert’s early death nor a declaration that two movements were sufficient explains its abandonment six years before he died. He left other symphonic and sonata projects unfinished while completing substantial works elsewhere. The Wanderer Fantasy, also from 1822, connects four sections through the rhythm and melody of a song. Its keyboard writing is conspicuously athletic: repeated chords, rapid octaves and a fugal finale contradict the idea of a composer concerned only with an easily singable tune.

In 1823 serious illness interrupted his working life. The surviving evidence strongly suggests syphilis, but precise diagnoses of individual later episodes remain uncertain. That year he composed Die schöne Müllerin, twenty settings from Wilhelm Müller’s poetic sequence about a young miller, his employer’s daughter and the huntsman who displaces him. The stream supplies both a recurring piano motion and, eventually, a speaking voice. Schubert omitted Müller’s framing prologue and epilogue, removing the poet’s explicit distance from his protagonist. A cycle that begins with a buoyant work-song ends with the brook’s lullaby over the miller’s death.

In a letter to Leopold Kupelwieser of 31 March 1824, Schubert described his damaged health and disappointed hopes, but also reported his plans for chamber music as preparation for a large symphony. The A minor and D minor string quartets belong to this effort. The latter takes the piano introduction of his 1817 song Der Tod und das Mädchen as the basis of its slow-movement variations. Those repeated chords become a communal statement for four strings. The borrowed song does not provide a story for every movement; its rhythm and harmonic shape give the variations something definite to resist and transform.

1825–27Long forms and a winter cycle

Which symphony number?The Unfinished has been numbered Seven or Eight, the Great C major Eight or Nine. D. 759 and D. 944 are unambiguous: the D numbers come from Otto Erich Deutsch’s chronological catalogue.

Travelling through Upper Austria with Vogl in 1825, Schubert worked towards the C major Symphony now called the Great, generally dated 1825–26. Its opening horn melody returns at the end of the first movement, enlarged by the full orchestra. Between those appearances, repeated rhythmic cells and extensive changes of key sustain a scale far beyond his early symphonies. The Vienna Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde received the score, but no securely documented public performance followed during his lifetime. Three trombones participate throughout the four movements, not merely as reinforcement reserved for a finale.

In 1827 Schubert set Winterreise in two groups of twelve songs. He first encountered Müller’s shorter sequence in the almanac Urania, then found the expanded collection and composed the remaining poems without rebuilding his first group into Müller’s final order. The resulting cycle follows an excluded lover past a frozen stream, an inn resembling a graveyard and a signpost pointing away from travelled roads. Walking figures continue when the singer has no practical destination. The major mode brings remembered shelter or imagined spring, not a dependable release from the minor.

Schubert carried a torch at Beethoven’s funeral in March 1827. On 26 March 1828, the first anniversary of Beethoven’s death, he gave a public concert devoted to his own music, with songs, chamber music and choral pieces. It earned money and an enthusiastic reception. During 1828 he completed the E♭ Mass, the F minor Fantasy for piano duet, the C major String Quintet and the last three piano sonatas. The quintet adds a second cello rather than Mozart’s second viola, separating a mobile lower singing voice from the instrument that maintains the bass.

1828 and afterMusic still awaiting its audience

No authorised swansongSchwanengesang is publisher Tobias Haslinger’s posthumous title. His 1829 collection joins seven Rellstab settings and six Heine settings to Die Taubenpost, on a poem by Seidl; it is not a cycle ordered and named by Schubert.

In October Schubert offered the new sonatas, Heine songs and quintet to the Leipzig publisher Heinrich Albert Probst. He was still seeking publication and planning further study: early in November he took a counterpoint lesson with Simon Sechter. He died on 19 November, aged thirty-one, at his brother Ferdinand’s home. The reported final illness was typhoid fever; its relationship to his earlier illness remains disputed. He was buried near Beethoven in Währing, and both composers’ remains were transferred to Vienna’s Central Cemetery in 1888.

Publication and performance continued to alter the picture after his death. Schumann helped bring the Great C major to Mendelssohn, who conducted it in Leipzig in 1839; Schumann’s published discussion praised its “heavenly length”. The String Quintet received its first documented public performance in 1850, the Unfinished Symphony in 1865. Schubert had been known above all through songs and smaller piano works. These delayed premieres revealed a composer who could extend a movement through returns, changed registers and remote keys without making the tune itself unrecognisable.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞Song as the core

The Lied is a composition for singer and pianist, not a melody with optional backing. Schubert can repeat one musical stanza, vary it as the poem changes, or write continuously through the narrative. Heidenröslein uses the first method; Erlkönig the last. In the two Müller cycles, piano introductions and postludes establish conditions that the singer may misunderstand. The miller hears encouragement in a stream whose music will eventually accompany his death.

Song also enters instrumental form directly: the Trout Quintet, Death and the Maiden Quartet and Wanderer Fantasy each take material from an earlier Lied.

𝅘𝅥The piano paints the scene

In Gretchen am Spinnrade, circling semiquavers suggest the wheel while the left hand supplies the treadle; the mechanism stops at the remembered kiss. Erlkönig keeps a triplet pulse running beneath four characters sung by one voice. In Auf dem Wasser zu singen, arpeggiated motion supports a poem about a boat and passing time. These are not decorative effects: each repeating figure determines how much freedom the vocal line possesses, and its interruption changes the drama.

♮Thirds and changing mode

Schubert often moves between keys whose tonics lie a third apart, rather than relying only on the tonic–dominant relationship. C major and A♭ major share C; two other notes can move by a semitone. A familiar melodic shape can therefore arrive in a changed harmonic setting without a long transition. The C major Quintet’s second subject begins in E♭ major, a minor third from the home tonic.

Major and minor can shade the same memory. In Der Lindenbaum, E major carries the remembered tree and its promised rest; E minor belongs to the walker’s exposure to the night. Major is not automatically reassurance.

𝄋“Heavenly length”

Schumann’s phrase concerned the Great C major Symphony, not an instruction to play Schubert slowly. Repetition becomes a means of hearing a phrase under altered conditions: a different bass, another register, a key a third away. In the late sonatas, entire thematic spans can recur rather than being immediately broken into small motives. The B♭ Sonata’s first-movement exposition repeat includes a disruptive first-time passage that disappears if the repeat is omitted. Length is partly a matter of what the listener is allowed to remember.

𝄾The wanderer

The outsider of Der Wanderer, the apprentice of Die schöne Müllerin and the rejected lover of Winterreise are different poetic characters, not interchangeable entries in Schubert’s diary. Repeated walking rhythms give them physical motion without guaranteeing arrival. In Der Leiermann, the last song of Winterreise, even walking yields to a stationary instrument: the hurdy-gurdy’s drone. The singer asks whether its player will accompany his songs. Schubert leaves the question without a narrative answer.

𝄢Register and inner voices

The Trout Quintet uses a double bass below a single cello, leaving the piano room for its bright upper register. The C major String Quintet instead adds a second cello; the pair can sing together or divide melody from bass. In its E major Adagio, second violin, viola and first cello hold the central melodic texture between the first violin’s short figures and the second cello’s pizzicato. Schubert’s sustained lines depend on the activity around and inside them, not only on the leading voice.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1814–15
    Gretchen am Spinnrade · ErlkönigD. 118 · D. 328Voice and piano; poems by Goethe
    Two early Goethe settings. In Gretchen (D minor, 1814), the spinning-wheel accompaniment stops at the kiss; in Erlkönig (G minor, 1815), one singer distinguishes narrator, father, child and supernatural tempter while the pianist maintains repeated triplets.
  2. 1819
    Piano Quintet in A major “Trout”D. 667Piano, violin, viola, cello and double bass
    Written for the circle of Sylvester Paumgartner in Steyr. Five movements, with a fourth-movement variation set on the song Die Forelle. The double bass separates the bass function from the cello; the song’s rippling accompaniment is withheld until the last variation.
  3. 1822
    Symphony in B minor “Unfinished”D. 759Orchestra
    Two completed movements, Allegro moderato and Andante con moto, followed in the manuscript by an incomplete scherzo. Low strings begin alone; oboe and clarinet then sing over a string tremolo. Johann Herbeck conducted the first performance in Vienna in December 1865.
  4. 1822
    Wanderer Fantasy in C majorD. 760Piano
    Four linked sections share the long–short–short rhythm of Der Wanderer, D. 489. The C♯ minor Adagio quotes the song; the final section turns its material into a fugue. Liszt later arranged the work for piano and orchestra.
  5. 1823
    Die schöne Müllerin (The Fair Maid of the Mill)D. 795Voice and piano; twenty poems by Wilhelm Müller
    A young miller follows a brook to employment, love and rejection. Strophic songs keep the language of work and folk song audible; altered accompaniments and shifts of mode undermine the protagonist’s confidence. The brook takes over the singing at the end.
  6. 1824
    String Quartet in D minor “Death and the Maiden”D. 810Two violins, viola and cello
    The G minor slow movement varies the chordal introduction of Der Tod und das Mädchen, not the maiden’s agitated vocal plea. A sharply accented scherzo and a tarantella finale keep the surrounding movements in motion; all four movements begin in minor keys.
  7. 1825–26
    Symphony in C major “Great”D. 944Orchestra, including three trombones
    A broad horn introduction leads into an Allegro driven by dotted rhythms and triplets. The A minor Andante con moto grows from an oboe melody into a sustained orchestral crisis. The manuscript bears the date March 1828, but the main composition is generally assigned to 1825–26.
  8. 1827
    Winterreise (Winter Travel)D. 911Voice and piano; twenty-four poems by Wilhelm Müller
    Composed in two instalments, in February and October. A steady walking pulse opens Gute Nacht; by Der Leiermann, the final song, accompaniment has contracted to the hurdy-gurdy’s bare fifth and small revolving figures. The cycle ends with a question addressed to its player.
  9. 1827
    Two sets of ImpromptusD. 899 · D. 935Piano; four pieces in each set
    The title does not mean improvised construction. D. 899 No. 3 sustains a G♭ major melody above continuous broken chords; D. 935 No. 3 is a B♭ major variation set on a theme also heard in Rosamunde and the A minor Quartet. Only the first two of D. 899 appeared in Schubert’s lifetime.
  10. 1828
    Schwanengesang (Swansong)D. 957 · D. 965aVoice and piano; poems by Ludwig Rellstab, Heinrich Heine and Johann Gabriel Seidl
    Haslinger’s posthumous collection, not a unified cycle sanctioned by Schubert. The Heine group includes Der Doppelgänger, whose spare repeated chordal pattern supports the recognition of a figure outside a former lover’s house. Die Taubenpost, D. 965a, was appended to the thirteen songs catalogued as D. 957.
  11. 1828
    String Quintet in C majorD. 956Two violins, viola and two cellos
    The E major Adagio sets a broad inner-voice melody against first-violin interjections and a pizzicato bass, then breaks into F minor. The scherzo’s C major gives way to a D♭ major trio. Semitone shifts disturb the large tonal blocks as decisively as the added cello changes the ensemble’s balance.
  12. 1828
    Piano Sonata in B♭ majorD. 960Piano
    The last completed sonata. A low G♭ trill interrupts the first movement’s opening paragraph; the second movement moves to C♯ minor. Its four movements require distinct kinds of pulse: broad Molto moderato, the slow movement’s persistent accompaniment, a lightly articulated scherzo and a finale repeatedly interrupted by a single G.
