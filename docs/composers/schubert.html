Life

In the Schubert family quartet, Franz played the viola. His brothers took the violins and his father the cello: the future songwriter learnt to make himself heard from an inner part. He was born on 31 January 1797 in Himmelpfortgrund, a Viennese suburb, and grew up in his father’s schoolhouse. His father taught him violin, his brother Ignaz piano, and the parish musician Michael Holzer helped with the rest. At eleven he entered the imperial chapel choir and the Stadtkonvikt boarding school, where the orchestra put Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven under his fingers.

Salieri began teaching him composition in 1812. The next year Schubert left the boarding school, trained as a teacher and returned to his father’s classroom as an assistant. On 19 October 1814, aged seventeen, he wrote Gretchen am Spinnrade. The piano’s right hand turns the wheel, the left hand works the treadle, and Goethe’s girl cannot stop thinking about Faust. At the remembered kiss the mechanism stops. The following year brought roughly 140 songs, among them Erlkönig, as well as church music, a symphony and works for the stage.

1816–21Friends with a spare room

Franz von Schober offered him somewhere to live, helping him escape schoolteaching. Josef von Spaun, a friend from the Stadtkonvikt, found people willing to help. The poet Johann Mayrhofer shared rooms with him from 1818 to 1820 and supplied words for songs; Moritz von Schwind drew the faces around him. In 1817 Schober introduced the opera singer Johann Michael Vogl. Here was a professional who could take Schubert’s songs into rooms the composer could not easily enter on his own.

A SchubertiadeSongs, piano duets, reading and dancing could share an evening. Schwind’s famous drawing of the assembled friends dates from 1868, forty years after Schubert’s death.

These friends did practical things. They supplied manuscript paper, lodging, introductions and, in 1821, the subscriptions that paid to publish Erlkönig as Op. 1. A few songs had already appeared in newspapers and almanacs; now there was a serious attempt to sell them. Vogl sang and Schubert played. A single voice could become the father, child, narrator and seductive apparition of Erlkönig, while the pianist hammered out the same galloping triplets beneath them all.

In the summers of 1818 and 1824 Schubert went to Zseliz, now Želiezovce in Slovakia, to teach Count Johann Karl Esterházy’s daughters. Four-hand piano music belonged naturally to this household: two players sharing a bench and negotiating the same keyboard. He also kept writing operas. Alfonso und Estrella, with a libretto by Schober, and Fierrabras went unstaged in his lifetime. The play Rosamunde lasted two performances in December 1823. Its incidental music outlived it, and one of its tunes returned in the A minor Quartet and a set of piano variations.

1822–24A symphony left on the desk

The B minor Symphony of 1822 begins with cellos and basses alone, low and quiet. Above shivering strings, oboe and clarinet introduce the first long melody; later, a new cello tune in G major is interrupted by a silence and a brutal orchestral outburst. Schubert finished two movements and began a scherzo, then put the score aside. He had six years left to live. In the same year he completed the Wanderer Fantasy, joining four sections through the rhythm of one of his songs and ending with a fugue that gives the pianist little mercy.

The illnessesThe recurring illness that struck in 1823 is generally identified as syphilis. His final fever in 1828 was diagnosed as typhoid; the precise relationship between the two remains unsettled.

Serious illness shadowed the composition of Die schöne Müllerin in 1823. A young miller follows a brook to work and falls in love with the miller’s daughter; a huntsman takes her away. Schubert chose twenty poems from Wilhelm Müller’s sequence, leaving out the poet’s framing prologue and epilogue. At first the stream runs cheerfully under the singer. By the last song it has taken his place, singing a lullaby over the drowned young man.

On 31 March 1824 he wrote to the painter Leopold Kupelwieser in Rome of a man “whose brightest hopes have come to nothing”. Further down the same letter he was making plans. He had written two quartets and an octet and wanted another quartet: “In this way I want to clear myself a path towards a great symphony.” The A minor and D minor quartets belonged to that campaign. In the latter, Death and the Maiden, four strings take up the grave, repeated chords from the introduction to his 1817 song.

1825–27The road and the frozen road

Counting symphoniesThe Unfinished is numbered Seven or Eight; the Great C major, Eight or Nine. Otto Erich Deutsch’s catalogue numbers avoid the confusion: D. 759 and D. 944.

In 1825 Schubert travelled through Upper Austria with Vogl, singing and playing for friends and hosts. The C major Symphony occupied him in 1825–26. Two horns begin it with a melody that will return, grown enormous, at the end of the first movement. Three trombones belong to the orchestra throughout, sometimes singing their own lines. He sent the score to the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde, which paid him for it. The public performance that would establish the Great C major came in Leipzig in 1839, eleven years after his death.

In 1827 he returned to Müller for Winterreise. He first found twelve poems in the almanac Urania and set them as a complete cycle. Later he discovered twelve more, interleaved among the original poems, and appended his new settings to the first group. The walker leaves his sweetheart’s house at night, passes the frozen river and mistakes a graveyard for an inn. The piano’s footsteps keep going. In Der Lindenbaum, remembered shelter glows in E major; the cold night returns in E minor.

When Schubert sang the new songs to his friends at Schober’s, Josef von Spaun remembered them sitting bewildered by the gloom. Schober liked only Der Lindenbaum. Schubert answered, “I like these songs more than all the others, and you will come to like them too.” At the end of the completed cycle, a barefoot hurdy-gurdy player stands beyond the village. The piano keeps turning over a bare fifth. The singer asks whether the old man will accompany his songs, and gets no reply.

1828Still looking for a publisher

Schubert carried a torch at Beethoven’s funeral in March 1827. On 26 March 1828, the anniversary of Beethoven’s death, he gave a public concert of his own music. It was a success and made money. During that year came the E♭ Mass, the F minor Fantasy for piano duet, the last three piano sonatas and the C major String Quintet. The quintet adds a second cello; in the first movement the two cellos sing the second theme together. In the Adagio, the middle three instruments sustain the tune while the outer two seem to speak around it.

A publisher’s farewellSchwanengesang was Tobias Haslinger’s title for his posthumous collection of 1829: seven Rellstab settings, six Heine settings and Seidl’s Die Taubenpost.

On 2 October he offered the sonatas, Heine songs and quintet to Heinrich Albert Probst in Leipzig. “I have played the sonatas in several places to much applause,” he wrote; the quintet was still awaiting a trial. Early in November he took a counterpoint lesson with Simon Sechter. He died at his brother Ferdinand’s home on 19 November, aged thirty-one, and was buried near Beethoven at Währing. Both graves were moved to the Central Cemetery in 1888.

The manuscripts kept arriving after him. Schumann helped bring the C major Symphony to Mendelssohn, who conducted it in 1839; reviewing it in 1840, Schumann delighted in its “heavenly length”. The String Quintet reached a public audience in 1850. Johann Herbeck conducted the Unfinished in Vienna in December 1865, forty-three years after Schubert had put down his pen.

The Sound

𝄞Two storytellers

In Erlkönig, one singer plays four people while the pianist maintains the gallop that none of them can stop. In Die schöne Müllerin, the brook’s accompaniment seems to encourage the young miller long before it sings him to sleep. Schubert gives the pianist knowledge the singer has yet to acquire; the last few bars after the voice stops can change everything.

𝅘𝅥A wheel, a horse, a drone

The accompaniment often tells us where we are before a word is sung. Gretchen am Spinnrade has its turning semiquavers; Erlkönig its repeated triplets. At Gretchen’s remembered kiss the wheel stops, and starting it again is an effort. At the other end of Schubert’s life, Der Leiermann reduces the piano to a bare fifth and a small figure that keeps turning.

♮Major and minor

In Der Lindenbaum, the tree and its remembered promise of shelter belong to E major. The night wind brings E minor. Schubert can change the meaning of the same tune by moving a single note of its chord. He also moves easily between keys a third apart: the C major Quintet’s second theme begins in E♭, with the two cellos singing together.

𝄋Taking time

The horns’ opening tune in the Great C major Symphony comes back near the end of the first movement, now claimed by the full orchestra. Schubert makes room for the return to matter. In the B♭ Sonata, taking the exposition repeat also means playing a disturbing first-time passage that is otherwise lost. The familiar beginning has a different aftertaste the second time.

𝄾Walking

Gute Nacht begins with steady piano chords: the man is already walking before he speaks. That motion returns through Winterreise, sometimes exhausted, sometimes driven onwards by anger. The major key often belongs to a remembered happiness or a dream of spring. By Der Leiermann, there is a stationary player beyond the village and a wheel turning where footsteps used to be.

𝄢Who carries the tune

The Trout Quintet puts a double bass beneath the cello, freeing the piano to sparkle higher up. The C major String Quintet adds another cello. In its Adagio, second violin, viola and first cello sing the long melody; first violin and plucked second cello surround it. When the opening returns after the F minor storm, those outer parts are newly restless.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1814–15
    Gretchen am Spinnrade · ErlkönigD. 118 · D. 328Voice and piano; poems by Goethe
    The spinning wheel in the D minor Gretchen stops at the remembered kiss. In the G minor Erlkönig, the pianist’s triplets carry a narrator, father, child and tempter, all sung by one person.
  2. 1819
    Piano Quintet in A major “Trout”D. 667Piano, violin, viola, cello and double bass
    Written for Sylvester Paumgartner’s circle in Steyr, with a double bass below the cello. The fourth movement varies Die Forelle, saving the song’s rippling piano figure until the end.
  3. 1822
    Symphony in B minor “Unfinished”D. 759Orchestra
    Two finished movements and the beginning of a scherzo survive. Johann Herbeck conducted the first public performance in December 1865, long after Schubert had become famous for his songs.
  4. 1822
    Wanderer Fantasy in C majorD. 760Piano
    The song Der Wanderer supplies the C♯ minor Adagio and the long–short–short rhythm running through four linked sections. Liszt later made a version for piano and orchestra.
  5. 1823
    Die schöne Müllerin (The Fair Maid of the Mill)D. 795Voice and piano; twenty poems by Wilhelm Müller
    A miller follows a brook into love and jealousy. Schubert leaves out Müller’s framing prologue and epilogue, ending with the brook’s lullaby over the young man’s death.
  6. 1824
    String Quartet in D minor “Death and the Maiden”D. 810Two violins, viola and cello
    The slow movement varies the repeated chords from Schubert’s earlier song of the same name. All four movements begin in minor keys; the finale is a tarantella.
  7. 1825–26
    Symphony in C major “Great”D. 944Orchestra, including three trombones
    The opening horn melody returns in the first movement’s coda, now enormous. Three trombones play throughout the symphony; the manuscript’s March 1828 date follows the main work of 1825–26.
  8. 1827
    WinterreiseD. 911Voice and piano; twenty-four poems by Wilhelm Müller
    Schubert set twelve poems in February, then added twelve more in the autumn. The walking chords of Gute Nacht eventually dwindle to a hurdy-gurdy’s bare fifth in Der Leiermann.

    Two sets of ImpromptusD. 899 · D. 935Piano; four pieces in each set

    Haslinger assembled and named the collection after Schubert’s death. In Heine’s Der Doppelgänger, a man recognises himself in the figure grieving outside his former sweetheart’s house.

Sister Arts

Schwind, who had belonged to the circle, draws Schubert at the piano with Vogl beside him. Friends fill the room and lean towards the music. He made the drawing in 1868, forty years after Schubert’s death: the spare rooms, singers and subscriptions that had sustained a career gathered into one remembered evening.

A man has set down his crutches in the snow and prays before a crucifix. Beyond the firs a church rises in the mist. Friedrich’s traveller has found somewhere to turn; in Winterreise the walker approaches a graveyard as an inn and discovers there is no room for him. The discarded crutches are the difference between the two scenes.

Wilhelm Müller

Der Leiermann — first and last stanzas

Published 1823; collected 1824; set by Schubert 1827
GermanDrüben hinter’m Dorfe Steht ein Leiermann, Und mit starren Fingern Dreht er was er kann. Wunderlicher Alter, Soll ich mit dir gehn? Willst zu meinen Liedern Deine Leier drehn?
Our translationOver there beyond the village stands a hurdy-gurdy player; with stiffened fingers he turns as hard as he can. Strange old fellow, am I to go with you? Will you turn your hurdy-gurdy to the songs I sing?

Müller leaves the old man barefoot on the ice, his plate empty while the village dogs growl. The first and last stanzas are shown here. Schubert’s piano turns the wheel of the hurdy-gurdy, a string instrument sounded by cranking a handle; the final questions receive no answer from the singer or the old man.

Lineage

Drew fromJoseph HaydnWolfgang Amadeus MozartLudwig van BeethovenAntonio SalieriJohann Wolfgang von GoetheWilhelm Müller
Schubert
Passed toRobert SchumannFranz LisztJohannes BrahmsAntonín DvořákBenjamin BrittenLuciano Berio

Salieri taught him to set words; the school orchestra taught him Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven from the inside. Schumann helped bring the Great C major to performance, Liszt carried the songs onto the concert platform in piano transcriptions, and Brahms edited the symphonies for the collected edition. Dvořák defended the instrumental music in his 1894 essay. Britten worked at the songs beside Peter Pears; Luciano Berio’s Rendering (1989–90) places his own music between the surviving stretches of a late Schubert symphony sketch.
