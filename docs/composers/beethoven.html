Born Baptised 17 December 1770, BonnDied 26 March 1827, Vienna

ILife

Beethoven was born in Bonn in 1770 and baptised on 17 December; 16 December is the probable, not documented, birthday. His father Johann sang in the electoral court chapel, and his grandfather Ludwig had been its Kapellmeister. The family name came from the Low Countries; the ‘van’ did not indicate nobility. Johann attempted to present his son as a child prodigy, but Beethoven’s more substantial training came from Christian Gottlob Neefe, who introduced him to Bach’s keyboard music and helped him obtain work as an organist. Playing viola in the court orchestra also gave him practical knowledge of instrumental and operatic repertory.

The Bonn years supplied more than a musical apprenticeship. The court of Elector Maximilian Franz, educated patrons and friends such as the Breuning family brought Beethoven into contact with Enlightenment literature and social ideas. After his mother’s death in 1787 and his father’s worsening incapacity, he assumed increasing responsibility for the household. In 1792 he left for Vienna to study with Haydn. The intended period of further training became permanent residence: the French occupation of Bonn in 1794 ended the old electoral establishment to which he might have returned.

ViennaA pianist with patrons

Haydn taught him composition; Johann Georg Albrechtsberger supplied contrapuntal instruction, and Antonio Salieri worked with him on Italian vocal writing. Beethoven established himself first as a pianist and improviser in aristocratic houses. Prince Karl Lichnowsky was an important early supporter, and the three piano trios published as Op. 1 in 1795 were dedicated to him. The arrangement was neither court service nor complete economic independence. Gifts, teaching, concert receipts, commissions and sales to publishers overlapped, sometimes with competing promises to different publishers about the same work.

The piano sonatas and chamber works of the 1790s enlarge a language already made flexible by Haydn and Mozart. Four-movement sonatas give the piano the proportions of a symphonic or quartet cycle. Slow introductions can return inside fast movements, as in the ‘Pathétique’ Sonata, and a development can concentrate on a small fragment instead of supplying another tune. Beethoven’s surviving sketchbooks show repeated attempts at transitions, themes and large-scale connections. A phrase that sounds forcefully direct in performance may have reached that shape only after several discarded alternatives.

The three periods‘Early’, ‘middle’ and ‘late’ are useful listening labels, not divisions Beethoven announced. The more disruptive works overlap with deliberately compact ones: the Eighth Symphony is not less sophisticated because it refuses the outward scale of the Seventh.

1802–09Hearing and public scale

Letters of 1801 describe hearing difficulties that had begun several years earlier. In October 1802, while staying at Heiligenstadt outside Vienna, Beethoven wrote a document addressed to his brothers explaining his isolation, despair and determination to continue composing. He did not send it. The ‘Heiligenstadt Testament’ is evidence of a crisis, but it is not a programme for every minor-key passage that followed. The Second Symphony, written around the same period, is in D major and contains substantial comedy. Biography does not assign a single emotional meaning to a key.

The Third Symphony, Eroica, composed mainly in 1803, changed the proportions of the genre. Its first movement develops the opening material across a much longer span than Beethoven’s earlier symphonies; the development introduces a new theme, and the coda continues the argument instead of merely confirming the ending. A funeral march replaces the usual lyrical slow movement. Three horns, rather than two, reshape the wind balance and become soloists in the scherzo’s trio. The finale subjects material already used in the ballet The Creatures of Prometheus and the piano variations Op. 35 to a further series of transformations.

The political story requires care. Beethoven associated the symphony with Bonaparte, and the surviving copyist’s score carries a violently erased reference to him. Ferdinand Ries later described Beethoven’s anger at Napoleon’s adoption of the imperial title. The physical document survives; the exact scene comes through recollection. The published score of 1806 names Prince Lobkowitz as dedicatee and describes the work as composed to celebrate the memory of a great man. David’s images of Napoleon and Beethoven’s revised title page belong to the same struggle over what a modern hero could represent.

His only opera, first staged as Fidelio in 1805, underwent major revisions in 1806 and 1814. Leonore disguises herself as a man to rescue her imprisoned husband Florestan; the plot joins domestic fidelity to the public exposure of political imprisonment. The work’s several overtures show Beethoven repeatedly reconsidering how much of the drama should precede the curtain. The 1814 version established its performing life. An extended duet or chorus could express liberation collectively in a way that the solitary pianist or instrumental ensemble could only imply.

The Fourth and Fifth Piano Concertos, the Violin Concerto, the ‘Razumovsky’ Quartets and the Fifth and Sixth symphonies belong to the same concentrated period of work. The Fifth carries a short rhythmic figure across movements and ends by adding piccolo, contrabassoon and trombones to the orchestra. The Sixth replaces that trajectory with five named scenes, including a storm; its last three movements are linked. Both were first performed at Beethoven’s long Vienna concert of 22 December 1808. Their proximity is a warning against reducing his orchestral language to continuous struggle.

A salary without a postIn 1809 Archduke Rudolph, Prince Kinsky and Prince Lobkowitz promised an annuity to keep Beethoven in Vienna. War, currency depreciation and changes in the patrons’ circumstances complicated payment. Patronage did not disappear when he stopped wearing an employer’s livery.

Private obligationsLetters, law and conversation

Archduke Rudolph was also a pupil and the dedicatee of major works, including the ‘Archduke’ Trio and the Missa solemnis. Beethoven’s friendships could coexist with disputes about payment, performance and personal loyalty. The unsent letter of July 1812 to the woman he called his ‘Immortal Beloved’ survives without an agreed identification of its recipient. After his brother Kaspar Karl died in 1815, Beethoven became embroiled in a prolonged custody dispute with his sister-in-law Johanna over their son Karl. His determination to control the boy’s education produced years of litigation and conflict; Karl attempted suicide in 1826.

As Beethoven’s hearing declined, public performance as a pianist became impracticable. From 1818 visitors increasingly wrote their side of conversations in notebooks, while he often answered aloud. These books preserve shopping, household negotiations and professional arrangements beside discussions of music. They are not full transcripts, since his spoken replies are usually missing. Composition continued through reading, writing, remembered sound and the physical habits of a keyboard player; deafness did not remove the need to calculate balance, register or the capabilities of actual performers.

Last decadeVariation, fugue and unfinished closure

The ‘Hammerklavier’ Sonata of 1817–18 gives the piano an enormous slow movement and a final fugue whose leaps and trills strain conventional keyboard textures. The last three sonatas pursue other proportions. Op. 111 has only two movements: a C minor opening and a C major Arietta with variations whose accelerating subdivisions eventually dissolve the ordinary sense of a beat. The Diabelli Variations extract separate properties from a publisher’s small waltz — repeated chords, a turn, a bass motion — rather than simply decorate its melody thirty-three times.

The Missa solemnis, written for Rudolph’s installation as archbishop but finished too late for the 1820 ceremony, and the Ninth Symphony bring large vocal forces into very different designs. The Ninth’s finale recalls and rejects earlier movements before the low strings introduce a new tune. Beethoven then adds his own appeal for different sounds and turns to Schiller’s An die Freude. At the Vienna premiere on 7 May 1824, Michael Umlauf provided the practical direction of a performance in which Beethoven also participated. The choral ending creates new formal problems; it is not merely a symphony with a hymn attached.

A commission from Prince Nikolai Galitzin prompted three of the late quartets. Op. 131 links seven movements; Op. 132 places a thanksgiving ‘in the Lydian mode’ between more mobile sections; Op. 130 originally ended with the Grosse Fuge, later published separately as Op. 133 when Beethoven supplied a replacement finale. These works set dance, recitative, hymn, variation and fugue beside one another without smoothing their differences away. Beethoven died in Vienna in March 1827. His final completed quartet, Op. 135, ends not with another large fugue but with a compact movement built around a question and its answering musical figure.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝅘𝅥Motivic development

A few notes can provide rhythm, accompaniment and large-scale connection. The Fifth’s short-short-short-long figure is not confined to the opening melody: it migrates between instruments and functions. Development can therefore continue while the recognisable tune has disappeared.

𝄾Rhythm

Accents can contradict the bar-line for long stretches. In the Eroica, repeated two-beat groupings disturb the written triple metre; in the Seventh, persistent rhythmic figures give each movement its physical identity. A rest can stop accumulated motion as decisively as a loud chord can redirect it.

♯Harmony

A remote key can become a structural destination rather than a passing colour. The ‘Waldstein’ Sonata moves from C major to E major in its exposition; the ‘Hammerklavier’ repeatedly exploits relations by thirds. In late works, a modal passage or prolonged trill can suspend the ordinary drive from dominant to tonic.

𝄋Form

Introductions return, codas develop, and movements connect without a break. The finale of the Ninth must justify its new voices from within the instrumental cycle. The seven linked movements of Op. 131 move through fugue, dance, recitative and variations without pretending they are all the same type of discourse.

𝆑Instrumental weight

Beethoven changes the balance through register and attack as much as through numbers. Three horns matter in the Eroica; the added trombones, piccolo and contrabassoon matter at the Fifth’s finale. A low piano tremolo or a quartet unison can acquire orchestral weight without adding an instrument.

𝄢Variation & counterpoint

A variation may isolate one component of a theme rather than preserve its outline. The Diabelli Variations repeatedly do so. Late fugues use inversion, augmentation and close entries, but their technical procedures remain audible as clashes of register, rhythmic density and physical effort.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1793–95
    Three Piano TriosOp. 1Piano, violin and cello
    Four-movement designs give the ensemble unusual breadth. The third trio’s C minor is a tonal and rhetorical alternative to the first two major-key works.
  2. 1798–99
    Piano Sonata No. 8 in C minor “Pathétique”Op. 13Piano
    The Grave introduction returns within the first movement. Slow declamation and the driving Allegro remain separate forces rather than a preface and its sequel.
  3. 1801
    Piano Sonata No. 14 in C♯ minorOp. 27 No. 2Piano
    Marked Sonata quasi una fantasia. The posthumous “Moonlight” nickname obscures a cycle that places its fastest and most disruptive movement last.
  4. 1802–04
    Symphony No. 3 in E♭ major “Eroica”Op. 55Orchestra with three horns
    An extended first movement, funeral march, scherzo and variation finale. The opening cello theme’s C♯ unsettles E♭ major within its first phrase.
  5. 1803–04
    Piano Sonata No. 21 in C major “Waldstein”Op. 53Piano
    The exposition turns towards E major. A short slow introduction links to the rondo, whose sustained pedal and wide registers depend on a changing conception of piano sound.
  6. 1804–06
    Piano Concerto No. 4 in G majorOp. 58Piano and orchestra
    The piano begins alone; the orchestra answers in B major. The slow movement opposes terse string unisons to the soloist’s quiet, chordal replies.
  7. 1806
    Three “Razumovsky” QuartetsOp. 59String quartet
    Large designs written for Count Andrey Razumovsky. Russian themes occur in Nos. 1 and 2; No. 3 ends with a fast, fugal finale.
  8. 1806
    Violin Concerto in D majorOp. 61Violin and orchestra
    Repeated timpani notes begin the first movement. The soloist’s high register and elaboration develop against an orchestral structure of unusual breadth.
  9. 1804–08
    Symphony No. 5 in C minorOp. 67Orchestra, enlarged in the finale
    The four-note opening becomes a resource across the cycle. A transition over timpani and strings links the scherzo to a C major finale with new instruments.
  10. 1807–08
    Symphony No. 6 in F major “Pastoral”Op. 68Orchestra
    Five movements with descriptive headings. Repetition, drones and slowly changing harmony create a different sense of duration from the Fifth.
  11. 1809
    Piano Concerto No. 5 in E♭ majorOp. 73Piano and orchestra
    Orchestral chords alternate with written-out solo flourishes at the start. The “Emperor” nickname was not Beethoven’s; the slow movement leads directly into the finale.
  12. 1811–12
    Symphony No. 7 in A majorOp. 92Orchestra
    Each movement establishes a persistent rhythmic character. The A minor Allegretto builds a processional pattern through successive layers and contrapuntal entries.
  13. 1805–14
    FidelioOp. 72Opera in two acts in its final version
    Leonore rescues Florestan from political imprisonment. Three principal versions and four overtures document Beethoven’s continuing revision of the theatrical problem.
  14. 1817–18
    Piano Sonata No. 29 in B♭ major “Hammerklavier”Op. 106Piano
    A large four-movement sonata with an F♯ minor Adagio and an extended final fugue. Leaps and trills become contrapuntal material.
  15. 1821–22
    Piano Sonata No. 32 in C minorOp. 111Piano
    Two movements only. The C major Arietta transforms a simple theme through changes of subdivision, register and sustained resonance.
  16. 1819–23
    Missa solemnis in D majorOp. 123Four soloists, chorus, orchestra and organ
    A liturgical text on a concert scale. A solo violin descends into the Benedictus; martial sounds interrupt the Agnus Dei’s prayer for peace.
  17. 1819–23
    Thirty-three Variations on a Waltz by DiabelliOp. 120Piano
    The theme is taken apart into repeated chords, turns, bass patterns and cadences. Fugue and a final minuet become transformations of its proportions, not just its decoration.
  18. 1822–24
    Symphony No. 9 in D minorOp. 125Four soloists, chorus and orchestra
    The finale reviews earlier movements, introduces the joy theme instrumentally and then turns to Schiller’s words. The opening’s bare fifth withholds a clear major or minor identity.
  19. 1824–26
    The Late QuartetsOpp. 127, 130–133, 135String quartet
    Five quartets and the separately published Grosse Fuge. The seven movements of Op. 131 and the modal thanksgiving of Op. 132 test different ways of organising an entire cycle.

Ludwig van Beethoven

Heiligenstadt Testament — art and survival

6 October 1802; postscript 10 October
German proseEs fehlte wenig, und ich endigte selbst mein Leben — nur sie die Kunst, sie hielt mich zurück, ach es dünkte mir unmöglich, die Welt eher zu verlassen, bis ich das alles hervorgebracht, wozu ich mich aufgelegt fühlte.
Our translationLittle was lacking, and I would have ended my life myself — only art held me back; ah, it seemed impossible to me to leave the world before I had brought forth everything that I felt capable of producing.

The unsent document addresses Beethoven’s brothers and explains conduct that others had interpreted as hostility. This excerpt concerns his decision to continue living and working, not a secret narrative assigned to one composition. The original is held by the Staats- und Universitätsbibliothek Hamburg. Spelling and punctuation are lightly regularised here. German text.

Friedrich Schiller

An die Freude — opening stanza

1785; revised 1803
GermanFreude, schöner Götterfunken, Tochter aus Elysium, Wir betreten feuertrunken, Himmlische, dein Heiligtum! Deine Zauber binden wieder, Was die Mode streng geteilt; Alle Menschen werden Brüder, Wo dein sanfter Flügel weilt.
Our translationJoy, beautiful spark of the gods, daughter from Elysium, drunk with fire we enter, heavenly one, your sanctuary! Your enchantments bind together again what custom has sternly divided; all human beings become brothers where your gentle wing rests.

Beethoven selected and reordered parts of Schiller’s poem for the Ninth, rather than setting it complete. ‘All human beings become brothers’ belongs to the revised wording; the earlier version spoke of beggars becoming brothers of princes. The bass soloist’s introductory O Freunde, nicht diese Töne! is Beethoven’s addition, not Schiller’s line. Textual versions.

VIILineage

Beethoven
Passed toFranz SchubertHector BerliozJohannes BrahmsRichard WagnerGustav MahlerBéla Bartók

Later composers inherited different Beethovens: Berlioz the expanded orchestra and dramatic cycle, Brahms the concentration of motivic work, Wagner the choral Ninth, Bartók the late quartet’s mixture of fugue, folk-like rhythm and discontinuity. Schubert’s response was already present within Beethoven’s lifetime, not a delayed nineteenth-century discovery.
