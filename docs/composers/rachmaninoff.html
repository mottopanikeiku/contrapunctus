Life

“My hearty thanks to the old man for not having forgotten about me!” Rachmaninoff wrote to the singer Mikhail Slonov on 26 December 1892. The old man was Tchaikovsky, who had praised his opera Aleko in a newspaper interview. Rachmaninoff was nineteen, newly graduated from the Moscow Conservatory with its Great Gold Medal. Tchaikovsky helped get the opera into the Bolshoi, and in May 1893 sat in the audience applauding. For a young composer, it was an excellent beginning.

Two calendarsRachmaninoff’s birthday was 20 March 1873 in Russia’s Julian calendar, 1 April in the Gregorian calendar. Full dates here are Gregorian.

His childhood had been less orderly. Born into a landowning family, he grew up at Oneg, near Novgorod, until his father’s financial failures forced a move to St Petersburg. He entered the Conservatory there in 1883. His parents separated; his sister Sofia died of diphtheria; schoolwork went badly. His grandmother took him to Orthodox services, where he heard the voices and bells that would stay with him. In 1885 his cousin Alexander Siloti recommended a move to Moscow, under the formidable care of the piano teacher Nikolai Zverev.

1885–93The boys at Zverev’s

Zverev housed Rachmaninoff and taught him without charge. He regulated practice, fed his pupils, took them to concerts and brought Moscow’s musicians into the house. Scriabin was another pupil. Rachmaninoff went on to study piano with Siloti, a pupil of Liszt, counterpoint with Sergei Taneyev and composition with Anton Arensky. In 1889 he asked for more privacy to compose. Zverev objected, and the boy moved in with his relatives, the Satins. Their estate at Ivanovka became his summer home from 1890.

He passed the piano examination in 1891 and completed his composition course the following year. Aleko was the examination piece, its libretto by Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko taken from Pushkin’s The Gypsies. The C♯ minor Prelude also dates from 1892: three descending notes, A–G♯–C♯, followed by quiet chords. Tchaikovsky liked it. When he died in November 1893, Rachmaninoff wrote the D minor Trio élégiaque, with a large variation movement and a funeral ending that recall Tchaikovsky’s own memorial trio.

1897–1901The doctor’s dedication

The First Symphony’s première in St Petersburg on 27 March 1897 undid that early confidence. Alexander Glazunov conducted; rehearsals went badly and the reviews were savage. Rachmaninoff put the score aside. Its sharp motto and violent accents would have to wait until 1945 for a second hearing. For about three years he composed little, though he taught and conducted. At Savva Mamontov’s private opera in 1897–98 he worked with Feodor Chaliapin, a bass whose attention to words and dramatic timing offered a pianist plenty to learn.

In 1900 Dr Nikolai Dahl treated him with hypnosis and encouragement. The Second Piano Concerto bears Dahl’s name on its dedication page. Its last two movements were played in December 1900; the complete work followed in Moscow on 9 November 1901, Rachmaninoff at the piano and Siloti conducting. The opening chords grow heavier until the orchestra takes up the first melody. Immediately the celebrated soloist becomes an accompanist, spreading arpeggios beneath the strings. The Cello Sonata and Second Suite for two pianos belong to the same return to work.

1902–08A farmer in the opera house

He married Natalia Satina, his cousin and a trained pianist, in 1902. Ivanovka gave him uninterrupted summers; he helped run the estate and put money into livestock and machinery. Winters meant rehearsals and concerts. At the Bolshoi from 1904 to 1906 he conducted painstakingly prepared performances, including the premières of his The Miserly Knight and Francesca da Rimini. Pushkin’s miser gets a long bass monologue over his gold. Dante’s lovers are caught in an orchestral whirlwind.

In 1906 the family moved to Dresden, returning to Ivanovka for summers. There he wrote the Second Symphony, which he premiered successfully in St Petersburg in 1908. The slow movement’s clarinet solo can seem to breathe for ever. Around it, strings develop another melody; the two lines build towards a climax far larger than either promised alone. Ideas from the opening return across the four movements, binding an hour of music together.

1907–10A boat and an ocean crossing

In Paris in 1907 he saw a black-and-white reproduction of Böcklin’s Isle of the Dead. The symphonic poem followed in Dresden in 1909; a painted version he later saw in Leipzig pleased him less than the reproduction. The music rows in 5/8, its uneven five-quaver bars carrying the boat towards the island. A broader central passage opens out before the rowing returns. The medieval Dies irae chant enters the score, a visitor who would return in the Paganini Rhapsody and Symphonic Dances.

The Third Concerto was written for his first American tour in 1909. It begins with a plain D minor tune in octaves, then asks the pianist to build a cathedral around it. Rachmaninoff gave the première in New York under Walter Damrosch in November and played it with Mahler and the New York Philharmonic in January 1910. Mahler’s meticulous rehearsing impressed him. He went home afterwards, leaving America with a concerto few pianists then dared to take on.

1913–15The sound of bells

An anonymous correspondent sent him Konstantin Balmont’s Russian version of Poe’s The Bells. In Rome in 1913 he began setting it for three soloists, chorus and orchestra, finishing at Ivanovka. Each movement has its own bells: sleigh, wedding, alarm, funeral. The third has no vocal soloist; in the fourth a cor anglais laments before the baritone enters. The final pages turn C♯ minor into D♭ major, the strings finding warmth after the last words about death.

The Vigil’s chantsTen of its fifteen movements use existing znamenny, Kievan or Greek chants. Five use Rachmaninoff’s own melodies. The texts come from Vespers, Matins and the First Hour, sung in Church Slavonic.

The All-Night Vigil followed in 1915, dedicated to the chant scholar Stepan Smolensky. Nikolai Danilin conducted the Moscow Synodal Choir at its première. Here the voices supply everything, passing a chant through the middle parts while other singers surround it. At the end of the fifth movement, the Song of Simeon, the basses descend to B♭1. Its quietness makes the depth more astonishing.

1917–31A living at the keyboard

In December 1917 Rachmaninoff left Russia with Natalia and their daughters Irina and Tatiana, taking a Scandinavian concert invitation as his way out. They reached the United States in November 1918. He never returned to Russia. Touring now paid for the household, leaving little time to compose. Only six new opus-numbered works followed his departure, alongside revisions and transcriptions. The Fourth Concerto and Three Russian Songs, completed in 1926, broke the long interruption.

The recorded composerHe recorded for Edison in 1919 and Victor from 1920. His discs include all four concertos and the Paganini Rhapsody, as well as his conducting of the Third Symphony, Isle of the Dead and Vocalise.

The records reveal a pianist with huge hands and a remarkably light touch when he wanted it. A tune stays clear inside the busiest figuration; a passage surges forward without every note growing heavy. He played Chopin, Schumann and Liszt beside his own music, and made transcriptions that found fresh voices within familiar works. His recitals also kept bringing back the C♯ minor Prelude which audiences insisted on hearing.

1933–43Old voices in the last dance

Villa Senar, on Lake Lucerne, restored a summer workplace from 1933. Its name joined Sergei, Natalia and their surname’s initial. He wrote the Paganini Rhapsody there in 1934 and the Third Symphony in 1935–36. The Rhapsody’s famous eighteenth variation turns Paganini’s tune upside down and opens it into D♭ major. Around that expansive melody, the piano and orchestra trade much sharper blows.

War ended the Swiss summers. On Long Island in 1940 he wrote Symphonic Dances for Eugene Ormandy and the Philadelphia Orchestra, who premiered it the following January. An alto saxophone sings in the first dance; near its end comes a major-key recollection of the abandoned First Symphony. The finale sets the Dies irae against resurrection music from the ninth movement of the Vigil, with “Alliluya” written over its return.

“I write down on paper the music I hear within me, as naturally as possible,” he told David Ewen in The Etude, December 1941. He moved to Beverly Hills in 1942 and became an American citizen in February 1943. His last recital, at Knoxville on 17 February, included Chopin’s B♭ minor Sonata. Cancer ended the tour. He died on 28 March, four days before his seventieth birthday, and was buried at Kensico Cemetery in New York state.

The Sound

𝅘𝅥The singing line

The Third Concerto begins with a narrow-ranging tune in octaves. Its small steps will support some of the most elaborate piano writing in the repertory. In the Second Symphony, a clarinet spins the Adagio’s melody across the bar lines. Vocalise gives a singer the same task without words: carry the phrase towards its high point, then let it fall away.

𝄢Bells at the piano

The Second Concerto begins with repeated chords growing steadily louder, each still sounding when the next arrives. In the C♯ minor Prelude, the low A–G♯–C♯ signal returns after the rushing middle section, now spread across four staves. Rachmaninoff makes the piano sound enormous by separating its registers. A deep bass note can keep ringing beneath a whole crowd of quicker notes.

♮Inside the chord

A Rachmaninoff pianist has to make melodies audible inside handfuls of notes. Common notes hold one chord to the next while another voice slides by a semitone. The Second Concerto’s slow movement reaches E major through a short orchestral bridge from C minor. His harmonies can travel a long way, with a moving inner voice quietly showing the route.

𝄋Returning chants

The Orthodox chants in the Vigil and the Latin Dies irae have different histories. Rachmaninoff brings both into his last Symphonic Dance: fragments of the Western chant meet music from his own setting of the resurrection. In the Paganini Rhapsody, the pianist introduces Dies irae in Variation 7, while Paganini’s tune continues around it. Two borrowed melodies become one argument.

𝄾Rhythm & wit

The boat in Isle of the Dead rows in unequal five-quaver bars. The late concertos and dances favour sharp attacks, quick exchanges and offbeat accents; their long tunes have brisk company. The Paganini Rhapsody saves its best joke for the end. After all the virtuoso noise, the piano slips away with a small, quiet fragment.

𝄞Orchestral faces

He often trusts an important melody to one wind player: clarinet in the Second Symphony, cor anglais in the funeral movement of The Bells, alto saxophone in the first Symphonic Dance. Strings can then enlarge what began as a solo. In the concertos, the piano repeatedly changes jobs, moving from foreground melody to the accompaniment beneath an orchestral voice.

Sister Arts

A white figure and coffin approach an island cut with tombs. Böcklin painted five versions between 1880 and 1886; this is the first, now in Basel. A monochrome reproduction seen in Paris in 1907 prompted Rachmaninoff’s music. When he later saw a painted version in Leipzig, he preferred the black-and-white image that had already set the boat moving in his imagination.

Rachmaninoff stands second from the left in the back row; Natalia Satina, whom he would marry, sits in the middle of the front row. He first came to her family’s estate in 1890. Ivanovka gave him room to write the First Concerto and much of the Third, and later became a farm into which he poured earnings from the concert platform.

Steinway commissioned a large portrait for Carnegie Hall in 1925. Somov painted this study in two weeks at the Rachmaninoffs’ home at Corbeville, near Orsay. Still dissatisfied, he only finished it a year later, adding the landscape behind the composer. Somov, a founder of Mir iskusstva, had also left Russia; the sitter’s exile had a counterpart on the other side of the easel.

Edgar Allan Poe

The Bells — opening section

1849
EnglishHear the sledges with the bells — Silver bells! What a world of merriment their melody foretells! How they tinkle, tinkle, tinkle, In the icy air of night! While the stars that oversprinkle All the Heavens, seem to twinkle With a crystalline delight; Keeping time, time, time, In a sort of Runic rhyme, To the tintinnabulation that so musically wells From the bells, bells, bells, bells, Bells, bells, bells — From the jingling and the tinkling of the bells.

Poe makes the words ring before a composer touches them: “tinkle” and “bells” pile up with almost reckless pleasure. Rachmaninoff used Balmont’s Russian adaptation, keeping the four stages from sleigh bells to funeral bells. The tenor begins the sleigh scene; the chorus answers him. Poe’s complete poem appeared in November 1849, after his death.

Alexander Pushkin

Не пой, красавица, при мне… (Do not sing, beautiful one, before me)

1828; set by Rachmaninoff 1892
RussianНе пой, красавица, при мне Ты песен Грузии печальной: Напоминают мне оне Другую жизнь и берег дальный. Увы! напоминают мне Твои жестокие напевы И степь, и ночь — и при луне Черты далекой, бедной девы.
Our translationDo not sing, beautiful one, before me those songs of sorrowful Georgia: they remind me of another life and a distant shore. Alas, your cruel melodies remind me of the steppe, the night — and, in the moonlight, the features of a distant, unhappy girl.

The first two of four stanzas. Rachmaninoff dedicated his A minor setting, Op. 4 No. 4, to Natalia Satina, his future wife. The piano introduces the haunting melody before the singer asks for silence, so the plea arrives too late: the song has already recalled the distant girl. It was published in 1893.

Recordings to own

  • Piano Concerto No. 2Sergei Rachmaninoff, Philadelphia Orchestra, Leopold StokowskiVictor / RCA · 1929The electrical remake moves with a directness that still surprises. Rachmaninoff keeps the opening movement taut; the old recording catches the piano better than the strings.
  • Piano Concerto No. 3Sergei Rachmaninoff, Philadelphia Orchestra, Eugene OrmandyRCA Victor · 1939–40Lean, quick and wonderfully clear in its phrasing. He chooses the lighter first-movement cadenza and makes cuts: this is the composer’s performing version, with all its brisk decisions.
  • Rhapsody on a Theme of PaganiniSergei Rachmaninoff, Philadelphia Orchestra, Leopold StokowskiRCA Victor · 1934Recorded on Christmas Eve, weeks after the première. The playing has the alertness of something freshly made, and the famous eighteenth variation stays within the pace of the whole work.
  • Symphony No. 2London Symphony Orchestra, André PrevinEMI / Warner Classics · 1973Sumptuous strings and Jack Brymer’s warm clarinet make this a generous first choice. Previn restores the passages commonly cut, while omitting the first movement’s exposition repeat.
  • The Bells · Symphonic DancesElizaveta Shumskaya, Mikhail Dovenman, Alexei Bolshakov, Yurlov Russian State Academic Choir, Moscow Philharmonic Orchestra, Kirill KondrashinMelodiyaMEL CD 1000840: raw-edged sound, fierce choral singing and a conductor who keeps everything moving. The alarm bells are genuinely alarming; the dances have a hard, exhilarating attack.
  • All-Night VigilKlara Korkan, Konstantin Ognevoi, USSR State Academic Russian Choir, Alexander SveshnikovMelodiya · 1965The pioneering complete recording has immense bass weight and a distinctly theatrical fervour. The tenors can waver and the pace can be stately; its scale is still formidable.
  • 24 PreludesSteven OsborneHyperion, CDA67700Osborne keeps melody and ornament distinct even in the thickest writing. The D major Prelude, Op. 23 No. 4, sings with unusual tenderness; the G minor march has a firm, unhurried tread.

Lineage

Drew fromPyotr Ilyich TchaikovskyFrédéric ChopinFranz LisztSergei TaneyevAnton ArenskyOrthodox chant and Russian bell-ringing
Rachmaninoff
Passed toVladimir HorowitzSviatoslav RichterVladimir AshkenazyThe twentieth-century piano-concerto repertory

Tchaikovsky encouraged the student and gave him a model for a memorial trio. Taneyev taught him how to keep voices independent inside a crowded score. The friendship with Medtner produced reciprocal concerto dedications; Horowitz championed the Third Concerto. Records make this inheritance unusually tangible: Rachmaninoff’s own quick, finely weighted playing stands beside the larger gestures of his successors.
