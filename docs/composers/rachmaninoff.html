SergeiRachmaninoff

Born 1 April 1873, Semyonovo, Novgorod province, RussiaDied 28 March 1943, Beverly Hills, California

A composer-pianist who built long melodies over tolling basses, and carried Russian chant into the concertos and dances of his American exile.

ILife

Rachmaninoff learnt the sound of Russian worship before he learnt orchestration. His grandmother took him to Orthodox services, where unaccompanied voices and bells occupied different parts of the ceremony: chant inside the church, metal sounding above and outside it. His music repeatedly brings the two together. A melody moves in narrow steps while bass notes toll beneath it; a chord is struck again before its previous resonance has died. The C♯ minor Prelude, the Second Piano Concerto and The Bells make this audible without requiring an actual church bell. The All-Night Vigil removes the instruments altogether, leaving a choir to produce both the sustained line and the accumulating weight of the harmony.

Two calendarsHis birthday was 20 March in the Julian calendar then used in Russia, 1 April in the Gregorian calendar. Full dates here use the Gregorian calendar; the difference was twelve days in the nineteenth century and thirteen in the twentieth.

Born into a landowning family in 1873, he spent his early childhood at Oneg, near Novgorod. His father’s financial failures brought the sale of the estates and a move to St Petersburg. Rachmaninoff entered its Conservatory in 1883, but his general schoolwork deteriorated; his parents separated, and his sister Sofia died of diphtheria. In 1885 his cousin Alexander Siloti recommended a transfer to Moscow and the supervision of Nikolai Zverev. The move exchanged a disorganised household for one in which practice, meals and attendance at concerts were regulated by a piano teacher.

1885–92The boarding pupils

Zverev housed a small group of boys and trained them without charging for their board or lessons. Rachmaninoff lived there while attending the Moscow Conservatory; Scriabin also studied with Zverev, though he was not one of the resident pupils. Piano discipline came with access to Moscow’s musicians. Tchaikovsky visited Zverev’s musical gatherings, and the boys performed for an audience that could include their future examiners. Rachmaninoff subsequently studied advanced piano with Siloti, a pupil of Liszt, counterpoint with Sergei Taneyev and composition with Anton Arensky. Taneyev’s insistence on the independent movement of voices would remain audible beneath the thickest piano textures.

Composition also broke up the boarding arrangement. In 1889 Rachmaninoff’s request for more private space to write provoked a quarrel with Zverev, who expected him to become a pianist. He moved in with his relatives, the Satins, and in 1890 began visiting their estate at Ivanovka, near Tambov. He passed his final piano examination in 1891 and graduated in composition the following year with the Great Gold Medal. His examination piece was Aleko, a one-act opera on Pushkin’s The Gypsies, with a libretto by Vladimir Nemirovich-Danchenko. Its bass protagonist murders the woman whose freedom he cannot accept; the student composer already had a use for the low male voice beyond vocal display.

1892–1901A prelude, a catastrophe, a concerto

The Prelude in C♯ minor appeared in 1892, when Rachmaninoff was nineteen. Its opening A–G♯–C♯ descent, the rapid triplets of its middle section and the return spread across four staves supplied a miniature version of his later large-scale designs: a compact signal, accumulated motion, then the signal enlarged. Its popularity eventually followed him into almost every recital hall. Tchaikovsky admired the piece and supported Aleko, attending its Bolshoi premiere in May 1893. When Tchaikovsky died that November, Rachmaninoff wrote the D minor Trio élégiaque, Op. 9. Its large variation movement and concluding funeral music deliberately recall Tchaikovsky’s own memorial Piano Trio.

What happened in 1897The failed performance and hostile reviews are documented. Reports of Glazunov’s alleged drunkenness come from later testimony.

The First Symphony, completed in 1895, received its premiere in St Petersburg on 28 March 1897, with Alexander Glazunov conducting. Rehearsals had distressed Rachmaninoff, and the performance drew destructive criticism, most notoriously from César Cui. The symphony’s abrupt motto, rhythmic violence and recurrent thematic cells gave it a character distinct from the popular Prelude. Rachmaninoff lost confidence in the work and in his ability to compose. He wrote little for roughly three years, although he continued to earn money by teaching and conducting. The symphony was never performed again during his lifetime; its orchestral parts eventually made possible the posthumous reconstruction of a score thought lost.

In 1900 he underwent treatment with Dr Nikolai Dahl, a physician and amateur musician who used hypnosis and repeated encouragement. The Second Piano Concerto emerged in stages: Rachmaninoff performed its second and third movements in December 1900, then finished the first. The complete concerto was premiered in Moscow on 9 November 1901, with Siloti conducting and the composer at the piano, and dedicated to Dahl. The opening puts the soloist to work as a bell tower, gradually strengthening a sequence of chords before the orchestra takes the first melody. The piano then becomes an accompaniment, its arpeggios carrying the orchestral line.

Ivanovka and MoscowLand, family and the theatre

Rachmaninoff married Natalia Satina, his first cousin and a trained pianist, in 1902. Ivanovka became their principal summer refuge; he eventually bought the estate and spent money on its farming operations as well as its buildings. The distinction between work and retreat was practical. Winters meant pupils, rehearsals and performances; the country offered uninterrupted stretches for composition. The Cello Sonata, the two-piano Suite No. 2 and the Second Concerto belong to the same recovery of 1900–01. In each, the piano can fill several registers while leaving a single sustained melody intelligible. In the Sonata, cello and piano repeatedly exchange the foreground.

Conducting was more than a way to pay for composing. At Savva Mamontov’s private opera in 1897–98 Rachmaninoff worked with the bass Feodor Chaliapin, whose treatment of words and dramatic pacing supplied a model of singing for a pianist. His Bolshoi appointment, from 1904 to 1906, involved detailed rehearsal with the singers and orchestra. He conducted the premieres of his own The Miserly Knight, after Pushkin, and Francesca da Rimini, after Dante, in 1906. The first concentrates a father’s possessiveness in an extended bass monologue; the second surrounds its lovers with the circling orchestral motion of the damned. Neither displaced Aleko as his most frequently staged opera.

1906–10Dresden, an island and New York

Seeking time away from Moscow’s obligations, the family moved to Dresden in 1906, returning to Ivanovka for summers. Rachmaninoff wrote the Second Symphony there, and conducted its successful premiere in St Petersburg in 1908. Its slow introduction supplies material that returns across the four movements. The Adagio’s clarinet solo is only one strand: its long sentences are interleaved with a string melody, then drawn into a much larger climax. The finale recalls earlier music before closing in E major. Where the First Symphony had made recurrence abrasive, the Second allows recognition itself to bind an hour of music together.

In Paris in 1907 he saw a black-and-white reproduction of Arnold Böcklin’s Isle of the Dead. The symphonic poem followed in Dresden in 1909. He later saw a painted version in Leipzig; the monochrome reproduction had already prompted the music. The boat’s approach becomes a recurring 5/8 figure: five quavers resist the evenly balanced rocking of a conventional barcarolle. A central expansion releases longer melodic phrases before the rowing returns. Within this movement Rachmaninoff uses the Dies irae, the medieval Latin chant associated with the Mass for the Dead. Its small descending cells make the subject identifiable even when the complete phrase is withheld.

The Third Piano Concerto was written for his first American tour in 1909. Its opening is deliberately spare: the pianist plays a narrow-range D minor melody in octaves over a quiet orchestral accompaniment. The first movement’s huge cadenza grows from material initially presented without virtuoso decoration; a pianist can choose between two versions, one chordal and one more lightly textured. Rachmaninoff premiered it in New York with Walter Damrosch in November 1909 and played it under Mahler with the New York Philharmonic in January 1910. He admired Mahler’s meticulous rehearsal, then returned to Russia after the tour.

1913–15Bells with words, bells without instruments

The Bells, begun in Rome and completed in 1913, sets Konstantin Balmont’s free Russian version of Edgar Allan Poe. Four kinds of bells become four movements: silver sleigh bells, golden wedding bells, the alarm bell and the funeral bell. The tenor sings in the first movement, the soprano in the second and the baritone in the fourth; the chorus carries the third without a solo singer. Instrumental attacks and vocal repetitions turn Poe’s onomatopoeia into symphonic rhythm. The final movement’s cor anglais introduces a lament before the baritone enters, and the orchestral conclusion changes the ending’s colour without removing the fact of death from the text.

Beyond “Vespers”The fifteen movements of Op. 37 draw on Vespers, Matins and the First Hour. Ten use existing chants, including znamenny, Kievan and Greek traditions; the remaining five are Rachmaninoff’s own chant-like inventions. All-Night Vigil is the accurate title.

The All-Night Vigil followed in 1915, during the First World War. Nikolai Danilin directed the Moscow Synodal Choir at its premiere. Dedicated to the chant scholar Stepan Smolensky, it sets Church Slavonic texts for unaccompanied voices, with alto and tenor solos. Rachmaninoff places chant inside changing textures: a tune can pass through the middle voices while upper and lower parts expand around it. In the fifth movement, the Song of Simeon, the basses descend to B♭1, two octaves and a tone below middle C. In the ninth, resurrection verses alternate with the returning blessing, creating the material he would recall in his final orchestral work.

1917–31The pianist pays for exile

In December 1917, after the revolutions and the loss of his Russian way of life, Rachmaninoff left with Natalia and their daughters Irina and Tatiana. A Scandinavian concert invitation provided the route out; the family reached the United States in November 1918. He never returned to Russia. At forty-five he made the piano his principal profession, enlarging his repertory and organising his year around American and European tours. The cost is measurable in the catalogue: only six new opus-numbered works followed his departure, alongside revisions and transcriptions. The Fourth Concerto and Three Russian Songs, both completed in 1926, broke a long interruption; major scores remained infrequent.

A recorded composerRachmaninoff recorded for Edison in 1919 and signed with Victor in 1920. His discs preserve all four concertos and the Paganini Rhapsody with Philadelphia, and his conducting of the Third Symphony, Isle of the Dead and Vocalise. They let us hear his interpretations.

His unusually large hands facilitated wide chord spacings, but the recordings document skills that anatomy cannot explain: projecting an inner melody, distinguishing bass from accompaniment, and accelerating through a passage without making every note equally heavy. The Second Concerto recordings with Leopold Stokowski and the later concertos with Eugene Ormandy preserve a pianist willing to move quickly through material that subsequent performers often broaden. His repertory included Chopin, Schumann and Liszt as well as his own music. Transcription remained a form of composition: he redistributed another composer’s voices across the keyboard, deciding which note would sing and which would supply resonance.

1933–43Senar and the last dances

Villa Senar, his new house at Hertenstein on Lake Lucerne, restored a summer base in the 1930s. Its name combines Sergei, Natalia and the initial of Rachmaninoff. Here he wrote the Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini in 1934 and the Third Symphony in 1935–36. The Rhapsody turns twenty-four variations into something like a three-movement concerto played continuously. Its celebrated eighteenth variation inverts a cell from Paganini’s Twenty-fourth Caprice, transfers it from A minor to D♭ major and enlarges its rhythm. Elsewhere the Dies irae confronts Paganini’s theme. Counterpoint and transformation supply the design.

War ended the Swiss summers. In 1940, on Long Island, he composed the Symphonic Dances, dedicated to Ormandy and the Philadelphia Orchestra, who premiered them in January 1941. The first dance gives a central melody to alto saxophone and ends by recalling the First Symphony in a major-key transformation. Few listeners could then have identified that quotation from a score unheard since 1897. The last dance brings the Dies irae into conflict with material from the ninth movement of the Vigil; Rachmaninoff marks the latter’s return “Alliluya”. The orchestral colours are sharply separated, the attacks often dry, and the waltz of the second dance repeatedly unsettles its own pulse.

He moved to Beverly Hills in 1942 and became an American citizen in February 1943. His last recital, in Knoxville on 17 February, included Chopin’s B♭ minor Sonata with its funeral march. Cancer ended the tour; he died at home on 28 March, four days before his seventieth birthday, and was buried at Kensico Cemetery in New York state. The First Symphony returned to performance in 1945, after its surviving parts had been recovered. By then its opening material had already reached an American audience, transformed in the work with which Rachmaninoff closed his catalogue.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝅘𝅥Melody & breathing

A small intervallic vocabulary can sustain a very long phrase. The Third Concerto begins with a tune confined to a narrow register; sequences, altered endings and changing basses gradually increase its reach. In the Second Symphony’s Adagio, the clarinet solo crosses bar lines without treating each one as a new beginning. The accompaniment must keep the pulse audible while the melodic voice appears to breathe independently.

His songs explain the instrumental writing. Vocalise, Op. 34 No. 14, removes words but keeps the singer’s need to gather a phrase towards its high point; a pianist playing the same line has to conceal the instrument’s decaying sound.

𝄢Bells & resonance

The bell effect is structural: widely separated registers, repeated attacks and a bass that continues to sound while the harmony above it changes. The Second Concerto’s opening chords increase in weight before the orchestral theme begins. In the C♯ minor Prelude the descending A–G♯–C♯ signal survives the rapid middle section and returns in octave-doubled chords.

Russian bell-ringing supplies a model of simultaneous layers. The piano pedal can hold a low resonance under quicker attacks; a choir can imitate the accumulation by overlapping entries and expanding its spacing.

♮Harmony & inner voices

The bass usually establishes a tonal destination, but the route is full of chromatic voice-leading: one part moves by a semitone while others retain common notes. Added notes and suspensions delay the completion of a chord without abolishing its function. The Second Concerto places its E major slow movement a major third above the work’s C minor tonic, using orchestral transition chords to make the change audible.

Listen within the piano’s figuration. A thumb may carry a middle-register countermelody while the outer fingers continue the figuration; Taneyev’s counterpoint remains active within the span of a hand.

𝄋Chant & recollection

Two traditions must be distinguished. Znamenny and other Orthodox chants provide actual melodic material for the Vigil. The Latin Dies irae comes from Western plainchant and carries a separate history of death and judgement. In Isle of the Dead, the Paganini Rhapsody and Symphonic Dances, Rachmaninoff fragments, transposes and recombines it.

The last Dance sets this borrowed melody against a quotation from his own resurrection music, composing their opposition into the notes.

𝄾Rhythm & endings

Long melodies stand over persistent subdivisions: running quavers, triplet accompaniment, an ostinato. The 5/8 of Isle of the Dead refuses the symmetry of a two-beat rocking motion. In the late works, offbeat accents and sharply detached exchanges between piano, winds and strings make the rhythm less cushioned than in the Second Symphony.

The Second and Third Concertos share a recognisable four-attack closing tag. Related cadential punctuation also ends the Cello Sonata and Second Symphony. “Rach-man-in-off” serves as a mnemonic for this signature rhythm. The Paganini Rhapsody closes the climactic display with a short, quiet fragment.

𝄞Orchestration & scale

A full string section often supplies the long melodic span, but isolated wind colours organise its surroundings: clarinet in the Second Symphony, cor anglais in the funeral movement of The Bells, alto saxophone in the first Symphonic Dance. The late scores expose these colours against thinner accompaniments.

His large forms depend on return with alteration. A first-movement theme can enter a finale; a melody initially sung by the orchestra can return as a piano argument. The pianist is alternately soloist, accompanist and one register of the orchestra.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1891; revised 1917
    Piano Concerto No. 1 in F♯ minorOp. 1Piano and orchestra
    Dedicated to Siloti. The mature revision tightens the orchestration and piano writing of the student concerto; this is the version usually heard.
  2. 1892
    AlekoOne-act opera; soloists, chorus and orchestra
    The Conservatory examination opera, after Pushkin’s The Gypsies. Premiered at the Bolshoi in 1893; its extended bass cavatina gives the jealous outsider a history before the final murders.
  3. 1892
    Prelude in C♯ minorOp. 3 No. 2Piano
    From Morceaux de fantaisie. Three descending pitches, an agitated triplet middle section, and a return enlarged across four staves.
  4. 1893
    Trio élégiaque No. 2 in D minorOp. 9Piano, violin and cello
    Written in memory of Tchaikovsky. A substantial variation movement sits between the opening lament and a finale that ultimately returns to funeral music.
  5. 1895
    Symphony No. 1 in D minorOp. 13Orchestra
    A motto and related thematic cells connect all four movements. The failed 1897 premiere obscured a work whose sharp accents and compressed gestures differ markedly from the Second Symphony; revived in 1945.
  6. 1896
    Six Moments musicauxOp. 16Piano
    Six contrasted studies in sustained melody, repeated notes, counterpoint and keyboard span. No. 4 in E minor sets its melody above continuous left-hand semiquavers.
  7. 1900–01
    Piano Concerto No. 2 in C minorOp. 18Piano and orchestra
    Dedicated to Nikolai Dahl. The piano’s opening tolling gives way to an orchestral melody over arpeggios; the finale’s lyrical second subject eventually returns in C major.
  8. 1901
    Sonata in G minorOp. 19Cello and piano
    Four movements with an expansive, independent piano part. The E♭ major Andante makes its effect through the exchange of melody and accompaniment.
  9. 1901–03; 1910
    Ten Preludes · Thirteen PreludesOpp. 23 and 32Piano
    With Op. 3 No. 2, these cover all twenty-four major and minor keys. The G minor march, Op. 23 No. 5, and the B minor Prelude, Op. 32 No. 10, belong to collections ranging from two-part studies to densely voiced chordal writing.
  10. 1906–07
    Symphony No. 2 in E minorOp. 27Orchestra
    Premiered in 1908. Recurring material connects the slow introduction, scherzo, clarinet-led Adagio and E major finale; an uncut performance preserves the scale of these returns.
  11. 1909
    Isle of the DeadOp. 29Orchestra
    After Böcklin’s painting, first encountered as a monochrome reproduction. The recurring 5/8 motion, central expansion and Dies irae references make a single continuous movement of about twenty minutes.
  12. 1909
    Piano Concerto No. 3 in D minorOp. 30Piano and orchestra
    Dedicated to Josef Hofmann and premiered by the composer under Walter Damrosch. The unadorned opening theme supplies an unusually large first-movement argument, including two alternative cadenzas.
  13. 1911; 1916–17
    Études-tableauxOpp. 33 and 39Piano
    “Study-pictures”: technical problems become dramatic structures. Rachmaninoff usually withheld their programmes, but disclosed several to Respighi when the latter orchestrated a selection, including the sea and seagulls behind Op. 39 No. 2.
  14. 1913
    The BellsOp. 35Soprano, tenor, baritone, chorus and orchestra
    Four movements after Poe in Balmont’s Russian: sleigh, wedding, alarm and funeral bells. The chorus alone provides the vocal part of the third movement; the final lament begins with cor anglais.
  15. 1915
    All-Night Vigil (“Vespers”)Op. 37Unaccompanied mixed choir, alto and tenor solos
    Fifteen Church Slavonic settings, ten based on existing chant. Divided voices, changing registers and contrapuntal entries give a single melodic line the scale of an orchestral movement.
  16. 1926; revised 1928 and 1941
    Piano Concerto No. 4 in G minorOp. 40Piano and orchestra
    The first concerto completed in exile, dedicated to Nikolai Medtner. Its compressed statements, restless transitions and exposed exchanges between soloist and orchestra resist the continuous melodic breadth expected after the Second.
  17. 1934
    Rhapsody on a Theme of PaganiniOp. 43Piano and orchestra
    Twenty-four variations on Paganini’s last solo-violin Caprice. Variation 1 precedes the theme; No. 7 introduces the Dies irae; No. 18 turns a thematic cell upside down in D♭ major. Premiered by Rachmaninoff and Stokowski in Baltimore.
  18. 1940
    Symphonic DancesOp. 45Orchestra, including alto saxophone and piano; also a version for two pianos
    Three dances dedicated to Ormandy and Philadelphia. The First Symphony returns in the first; the Vigil and Dies irae contend in the last. His final completed composition.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

A boat bearing a white figure and coffin approaches rock, cypresses and openings cut into the island. Böcklin made five versions between 1880 and 1886; this is the first, in Basel. Rachmaninoff encountered the image through a black-and-white reproduction in Paris in 1907; which version it reproduced remains uncertain. His 1909 symphonic poem translates the approach into a repeated 5/8 figure.

Rachmaninoff stands second from the left in the back row. This is the Satin family’s estate near Tambov, where he first spent a summer in 1890 and returned to compose before 1917. The photograph places the music within the household where he worked. Ivanovka provided the working conditions for the First Concerto and much of the Third; it was also a farm on which he spent substantial earnings.

Somov, a member of the Mir iskusstva circle, painted Rachmaninoff during his own years abroad. The frontal head, dark coat and restrained background present the professional musician without a piano or score. Its date falls between the establishment of Rachmaninoff’s American touring career and his return to major composition with the Fourth Concerto in 1926.

Edgar Allan Poe

The Bells — opening section

1849
EnglishHear the sledges with the bells — Silver bells! What a world of merriment their melody foretells! How they tinkle, tinkle, tinkle, In the icy air of night! While the stars that oversprinkle All the Heavens, seem to twinkle With a crystalline delight; Keeping time, time, time, In a sort of Runic rhyme, To the tintinnabulation that so musically wells From the bells, bells, bells, bells, Bells, bells, bells — From the jingling and the tinkling of the bells.

Rachmaninoff set Konstantin Balmont’s Russian adaptation of Poe’s English in Op. 35. The four-part sequence remains, from these sleigh bells through wedding and alarm bells to the iron funeral bell. Repeated words already behave as rhythmic cells; Rachmaninoff gives repetition to the chorus and orchestral accompaniment as well as the soloist. The complete poem was published in 1849, after Poe’s death.

Alexander Pushkin

Не пой, красавица, при мне… (Do not sing, beautiful one, before me)

1828; set by Rachmaninoff 1892
RussianНе пой, красавица, при мне Ты песен Грузии печальной: Напоминают мне оне Другую жизнь и берег дальный. Увы! напоминают мне Твои жестокие напевы И степь, и ночь — и при луне Черты далекой, бедной девы.
Our translationDo not sing, beautiful one, before me those songs of sorrowful Georgia: they remind me of another life and a distant shore. Alas, your cruel melodies remind me of the steppe, the night — and, in the moonlight, the features of a distant, unhappy girl.

The first two of four stanzas. Rachmaninoff set the complete poem as Op. 4 No. 4, in A minor, and dedicated the song to Natalia Satina. The piano’s introduction establishes the returning music before the singer asks not to hear it: memory has already entered the room. The poem predates Rachmaninoff’s exile by almost ninety years, and his setting by twenty-five.

VIILineage

Drew fromPyotr Ilyich TchaikovskyFrédéric ChopinFranz LisztSergei TaneyevAnton ArenskyOrthodox chant and Russian bell-ringing
Rachmaninoff
Passed toVladimir HorowitzSviatoslav RichterVladimir AshkenazyThe twentieth-century piano-concerto repertory

Tchaikovsky supplied a model of long melody, orchestral climax and the memorial work; Taneyev supplied the discipline of independent voices. Rachmaninoff and Medtner dedicated a piano concerto to each other. Horowitz became a close friend and a major advocate of the Third Concerto. Performers and recordings have carried much of Rachmaninoff’s legacy.

Further reading

  • Sergei Bertensson and Jay Leyda, with the assistance of Sophia Satina, Sergei Rachmaninoff: A Lifetime in Music (New York University Press, 1956) — letters and testimony from the composer’s circle; distinguish recollection from contemporary documentation.
  • Barrie Martyn, Rachmaninoff: Composer, Pianist, Conductor (Scolar Press, 1990) — the three professions treated together, with detailed discussion of the works and recorded performances.
  • Max Harrison, Rachmaninoff: Life, Works, Recordings (Continuum, 2005) — a work-by-work account that takes the orchestral and choral writing as seriously as the piano music.
  • Tchaikovsky Research: Sergei Rachmaninoff — letters, dates and source notes for the older composer’s encouragement, including the 1893 Aleko premiere.
  • Rachmaninoff Network — research resources and the concert diary; Sergei Rachmaninoff Foundation — the documented history of Villa Senar.
  • Russian Virtual Library: Pushkin’s “Не пой, красавица, при мне…” and the Edgar Allan Poe Society’s manuscript text of “The Bells” — the original-language sources behind the song and choral symphony.
