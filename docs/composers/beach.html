Life

Amy Marcy Cheney was born on 5 September 1867 in Henniker, New Hampshire, the only child of a paper manufacturer and a singer-pianist. At four she spent a summer on her grandfather’s farm in West Henniker, where there was no piano, and composed three waltzes in her head; she played them when she got home. Her mother would not teach her the piano until she was six. “I was not allowed to climb up on her lap, or on the music stool,” Beach remembered. “I could only hear music, think music.”

1871–85The prodigy of Boston

The Cheneys moved to Chelsea, Massachusetts, in 1871 and to Boston in 1875. Ernst Perabo and then Carl Baermann, a pupil of Liszt, taught her the piano; Junius Hill gave her a year of harmony. Boston musicians told her parents to send her to Europe. They kept her at home. On 24 October 1883, aged sixteen, she played Moscheles’s G minor Concerto and Chopin’s E♭ Rondo at the Boston Music Hall. Her first song in print, The Rainy Day, a Longfellow setting, appeared the same year. On 28 March 1885 she played Chopin’s F minor Concerto with the Boston Symphony under Wilhelm Gericke.

Colour and keyBeach associated keys with colours from childhood. C was white, E yellow, G red, A green, A♭ blue, D♭ violet, E♭ pink, and F♯ minor and G♯ minor black. The list comes from her papers at the University of New Hampshire, as tabulated by Sabrina Clarke.

On 2 December 1885 she married Dr Henry Harris Aubrey Beach, a Boston surgeon and a widower more than twenty years her senior. From then on every title page read “Mrs H. H. A. Beach”. He did not want her to give up music, but he wanted her fees to go to charity and her recitals limited to a few a year. “I was happy and Dr. Beach was content,” she said later. She spent the time studying. She told the compiler Otto Ebel that her orchestration, counterpoint and fugue had been “acquired alone, without teacher”, from treatises, from the scores of the masters, and from translating Berlioz and Gevaert on instrumentation. On Gericke’s advice, she studied every work the Boston Symphony played before, during and after the performance, and kept this up for years.

1892–96A mass, an aria and a symphony

On 7 February 1892 the Handel and Haydn Society gave her Mass in E♭ at the Boston Music Hall under Carl Zerrahn, the first mass composed by an American woman. In December the New York Symphony Society under Walter Damrosch played Eilende Wolken, her concert aria on Schiller’s Mary Stuart, with the contralto Katie Alves. Festival Jubilate opened the Woman’s Building at the Chicago exposition on 1 May 1893, with Theodore Thomas conducting.

Dvořák had told the New York Herald in May 1893 that “In the Negro melodies of America I discover all that is needed for a great and noble school of music.” That December Beach heard the New World Symphony in Boston and wrote in her journal that Dvořák might have given more of “the dark, tragic, side!” She told the Boston Herald that Northerners “should be far more likely to be influenced by the old English, Scotch, or Irish songs inherited with our literature from our ancestors.” She wrote the “Gaelic” Symphony in 1894–96.

The Celtic cadenceThe Boston Symphony’s programme book for the first performance identified in the slow movement “the characteristic Keltic closing cadence, from the third degree of the scale to the tonic”. In E minor that is G falling to E.

Emil Paur conducted the first performance at the Friday-afternoon rehearsal on 30 October 1896; the Saturday-evening concert followed. The symphony opens pianissimo, with a tremulous chromatic passage in the strings that grows for sixteen bars before the first theme enters in the trumpets. Philip Hale reviewed it in the Boston Journal: “The only trace of woman that I find in this symphony is this same boisterousness.” George Whitefield Chadwick called her “one of the boys”.

1897–1910Sonata, concerto, quintet

The Violin Sonata in A minor was new to the Kneisel Quartet’s fourth concert at Association Hall on 4 January 1897, with Mrs Beach at the piano. On 6 April 1900, in the old Music Hall, she played her own Piano Concerto in C♯ minor with the Boston Symphony under Gericke, the conductor whose advice had sent her to study the orchestra’s concerts. Hale was less favourable this time and called it “a disappointment in nearly every way”. On 27 February 1908 she and the Hoffmann Quartet gave the first performance of her Piano Quintet in F♯ minor at Potter Hall, Boston.

A librarian writesOn 7 January 1911 Oscar Sonneck of the Library of Congress told her it seemed a pity the Library held so many European piano concertos and not hers, “which easily holds its own against so many works that have been published in Europe of late years”. She sent the manuscript of her Service in A as well.

1910–14Europe

Dr Beach died on 28 June 1910, after a fall; her mother died on 18 February 1911. On 5 September 1911 Beach sailed for Europe. She was a bad sailor, and she told an interviewer she had half hoped to cross “by some other way—perhaps by a flying machine”. From the autumn of 1912 she played her concerto, sonata and quintet in German cities, and the “Gaelic” Symphony was performed in Leipzig and Hamburg. A photograph from Munich, dated 14 February 1913, shows her with the soprano Marcella Craft, who had sung her songs in European recitals for years. After the Hamburg concert in December 1913, Dr Ferdinand Pfohl wrote in the Hamburger Nachrichten that “one need only mention the names of Amelie Nikisch and Amy Beach in order to refute this foolish prejudice concerning women composers.” She was home in the autumn of 1914.

1921–44The thrush at the MacDowell Colony

In the summer of 1921 she began the first of eighteen residencies at the MacDowell Colony in Peterborough, New Hampshire, working in the Watson studio. A hermit thrush sang near her. She wrote down its songs and based two piano pieces on them, A Hermit Thrush at Eve and A Hermit Thrush at Morn, Op. 92. A footnote to the second reads: “These bird-calls are exact notations of hermit thrush songs, in the original keys, but an octave lower, obtained at MacDowell Colony, Peterborough, N.H.” Younger composers there called her Aunt Amy.

She became the first president of the Society of American Women Composers, made New York her winter home from 1930, and wrote her only opera, Cabildo, in 1932; it reached the stage at Athens, Georgia, on 27 February 1945. Beach died of heart failure in her New York apartment on 27 December 1944. She left her copyright to the MacDowell Colony, and her royalties still support it. “My work has always been judged from the beginning by work as such, not according to sex,” she said near the end.

The Sound

♩Songs as source material

The first movement of the “Gaelic” Symphony draws on her own song Dark Is the Night (1890), a Henley setting. She reused tunes across her songs, piano pieces and orchestral works.

♮The falling third

The 1896 programme book identified a “Keltic” cadence in the slow movement, in which the third degree falls to the tonic. The slow movement also sets two folk-like themes against each other so that they sound together.

𝄢Orchestration

The symphony is scored for pairs of flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons, four horns, two trumpets, three trombones, tuba, three timpani and strings. In the concerto she added a bass clarinet, and a triangle in the scherzo.

𝄾Repeated chords

In The Year’s at the Spring (Op. 44 No. 1) the piano plays repeated chords under nearly every bar. The voice holds long notes over them, with a tied B♭ on “year’s” and a held note at the climax on “right”.

𝄞Birdsong

According to her own footnote, the thrush calls in Op. 92 are exact notations, written an octave lower than the bird sang them. She had been taking down birdsong since childhood.

♯Brahms

The Piano Quintet’s main theme, first heard on the first violin, derives from the second theme in the finale of Brahms’s Piano Quintet. Falling phrases from it recur in all three movements, and two F♯ major chords end the work.

Sister Arts

This is the bird Beach heard at the MacDowell Colony in 1921. Her Op. 92 pieces set its songs in notation, and her footnote states that they are exact. Audubon’s plate 58 shows the male and the female, lettered “Male, 1. Female, 2.” in its title.

Craft sang with the Munich opera and had given Beach’s songs in European recitals for years. The picture dates from Beach’s German season of 1912–14, when her concerto, sonata and quintet were played in German cities.

Left to right: Phyllis Fergus, Ethel Glenn Hier, Amy Beach, Harriet Ware and Gena Branscombe. Beach was first president of the Society of American Women Composers.

Robert Browning

The Year’s at the Spring

1841
EnglishThe year’s at the spring, And day’s at the morn; Morning’s at seven; The hillside’s dew-pearled; The lark’s on the wing; The snail’s on the thorn: God’s in his heaven— All’s right with the world!

Pippa’s song from Pippa Passes. Beach set it as Op. 44 No. 1 and printed the text on the page opposite the music. She sets the first four lines, slows at “dew-pearled”, then starts again, drops “Morning’s at seven” and “The hillside’s dew-pearled”, and runs on to “All’s right with the world”.

Lineage

Drew fromAntonín DvořákJohannes BrahmsRobert BrowningFriedrich Schiller
Beach
Passed toMarcella Craft

Dvořák’s call for an American music prompted the “Gaelic” Symphony. Brahms’s quintet supplied the theme of her own quintet, and she set texts by Browning and Schiller. The soprano Marcella Craft sang her songs in Munich.

Further reading

  • Adrienne Fried Block, Amy Beach, Passionate Victorian: The Life and Work of an American Composer, 1867–1944 (Oxford University Press, 1998).biography

    The standard life, covering how a prodigy with one year of formal harmony came to compose a symphony. It is the source of the Amy Beach Project’s works list.

  • Walter S. Jenkins, The Remarkable Mrs. Beach, American Composer (Harmonie Park Press, 1994).biography

    A life drawn from her scrapbooks and letters, now at the University of New Hampshire, covering the marriage, the Cape Cod cottage and the MacDowell summers.

  • Jeanell Wise Brown, Amy Beach and Her Chamber Music (Scarecrow Press, 1994).work study

    A study of the chamber music, including the Violin Sonata, the Piano Quintet and the late trio.

  • Boston Symphony Orchestra, programme book for the third rehearsal and concert, 30–31 October 1896 (Season 16, subscription series).primary document

    The programme that listeners read at the first performance of the “Gaelic” Symphony gives Beach’s training, the movements, the scoring and the Celtic cadence.
