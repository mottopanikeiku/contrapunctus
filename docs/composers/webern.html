ILife

Anton Webern was born in Vienna and grew up partly in Graz, Klagenfurt and at the family’s Carinthian estate, Preglhof. He learned the piano and cello, playing in the Klagenfurt concert society’s orchestra. The mountains and the family home recur in his letters as specific places of memory, not merely as images attached to his music by later commentators. In 1902 he began studying musicology with Guido Adler at the University of Vienna. His doctoral work, completed in 1906, edited part of Heinrich Isaac’s Choralis Constantinus, bringing him into close contact with Renaissance contrapuntal technique.

He began composition lessons with Schoenberg in 1904, alongside Berg. The orchestral idyll Im Sommerwind, written that year after Bruno Wille, still belongs to the world of late-Romantic tone poetry. The unnumbered String Quartet of 1905 was planned in relation to Giovanni Segantini’s Alpine triptych. Webern’s later miniature forms therefore did not arise from ignorance of expansive, pictorial music. He had written it, studied it and continued to conduct nineteenth-century repertory after his own compositional language changed.

1908–13The size of an utterance

The Passacaglia, Op. 1, completed in 1908 at the end of his formal study, places an eight-bar bass theme beneath a continuous sequence of variations. Its D minor and large orchestra make it recognisably related to Brahms and Mahler. Within a year the Five Movements for String Quartet, Op. 5, had replaced that sustained span with sharply differentiated short movements. Pizzicato, harmonics and playing near the bridge are not decorative effects added to a pre-existing tune: a change of attack can mark the arrival of a new event as decisively as a change of pitch.

Before the rowThe Six Bagatelles, Op. 9, are freely atonal, not twelve-tone pieces. Their extreme brevity predates Webern’s systematic use of ordered rows in the 1920s.

The Six Bagatelles for String Quartet, composed in 1911–13, reduce the duration of a complete movement to less than a minute in several cases. A held note, a falling interval or a change from muted bowing to pizzicato acquires weight because so little surrounds it. Yet the Six Orchestral Pieces, Op. 6, written in 1909, include a funeral march that builds to a violent percussion climax. Webern’s concision does not mean uninterrupted softness. The pieces’ original large orchestra was later reduced in his 1928 revision, making individual lines easier to balance.

His professional life was less concentrated. He took conducting posts in theatres including Teplitz, Danzig, Stettin and Prague, repeatedly finding routine operatic work unsatisfactory. In 1911 he married Wilhelmine Mörtl; they had four children. Military service interrupted his work during the First World War. After 1918 he helped prepare performances for Schoenberg’s Society for Private Musical Performances, whose rehearsals treated difficult new pieces as music to be learned rather than as provocations. Precision in balance, phrasing and tempo was part of his daily work with other composers’ scores.

Words and counterpointA way out of the miniature

Vocal composition supplied a means of sustaining longer structures. The Six Songs on poems by Georg Trakl, Op. 14, composed in 1917–21, use a voice and changing groups of chamber instruments. Trakl’s broken syntax and recurring images give successive gestures something to answer. Later, the poetry of Hildegard Jone became central to Webern’s output. Her texts appear in songs and the two cantatas. The move towards larger vocal works should not be mistaken for a return to conventional song accompaniment: a word may pass through several registers while instrumental fragments continue its intervals.

In the 1920s Webern adopted Schoenberg’s twelve-tone method and developed a preference for rows whose internal groups are related by inversion, reversal or transposition. In the Symphony, Op. 21, completed in 1928, reversing the whole row gives the same sequence transposed by a tritone. The first movement uses double canon in contrary motion; the second is a set of variations. These procedures connect his modern pitch language to the polyphony he had studied as a musicologist, though the widely separated registers and changing instruments make the sound unlike an Isaac motet.

A line can change instrumentIn the Symphony, a canonic voice can move from horn to clarinet to cello. Following only one instrumental part therefore gives an incomplete account of the melodic line.

As a conductor Webern worked with workers’ choirs and the Social Democratic concert movement, and from 1927 with Vienna radio. His programmes included major classical and Romantic works, not only the music of his own circle. The suppression of Austrian Social Democracy in 1934 destroyed an important part of this employment. He continued private teaching and editorial work. The Concerto for Nine Instruments, Op. 24, completed in 1934, makes four related three-note cells unusually easy to distinguish in its opening: different instruments, registers and rhythmic values separate them before the piano answers.

The last decadePolitics and reception

Webern’s political beliefs complicate any account of him as simply an artist persecuted by Nazism. He expressed admiration for Hitler and the project of a greater Germany in private letters, including correspondence with Joseph Hueber in 1940. At the same time, his atonal music and association with Schoenberg placed him outside the regime’s approved musical culture. He did not join the Nazi Party. His nationalism, his long loyalty to Jewish colleagues and his marginalisation as a composer coexist in the documentary record; one does not cancel the others.

The late works expand beyond the early aphorisms. The Piano Variations, Op. 27, have three movements, and the Second Cantata, Op. 31, completed in 1943, has six. In 1935 he orchestrated the six-part Ricercar from Bach’s Musical Offering, dividing phrases among instruments so that entries and motivic connections became differences of colour. The operation clarifies an older contrapuntal score by changing its medium. It also provides a direct comparison with his own writing: both require listeners to hear continuity across interruptions in a single instrument’s part.

His son Peter died in the war in February 1945. Webern and his wife left the Vienna area for Mittersill that April. On 15 September an American occupation soldier shot him during an operation involving his son-in-law’s black-market activities. He was sixty-one. After the war, Boulez, Stockhausen and other younger composers treated his economical distribution of pitch, register, duration and colour as a starting point for further serial organisation. That reception emphasised structure; Webern’s own rehearsal practice and letters also insist on expressive phrasing, changes of tempo and the shaping of individual notes.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄾Concentration

A rest is a measured part of the phrase, not empty space between phrases. In the Bagatelles, a single change of register or articulation can perform the work of a new theme. The small number of events makes their order and duration unusually exposed; an extra beat changes the scale of the whole movement.

𝄢Counterpoint

Canons may proceed in contrary motion or backward order. Webern’s study of Isaac and his arrangement of Bach are practical antecedents, but the later canons disperse their voices among widely separated pitches and different instruments. The listener follows an intervallic pattern rather than a continuously singing section of the orchestra.

𝄆Row construction

Op. 21 is built on a row equivalent to its own retrograde at a tritone’s distance. Op. 24 divides into four versions of one three-note shape. Such internal relationships reduce the number of genuinely different row forms available, allowing the same few intervals to connect small gestures and whole movements.

𝄞Instrumental colour

Muted brass, harp attacks, string harmonics and changing bow positions distinguish notes that would merge on a piano. A melodic line can continue across these colours. The result is not arbitrary pointillism: in the Symphony, sequences of instruments can themselves reflect the music’s canonic and symmetrical organisation.

𝆏Dynamics & phrasing

Very soft playing and short crescendos give an isolated note a beginning, direction and end. Webern also wrote sudden fortes and large climaxes, notably in Op. 6. Accounts of his work with performers describe expressive inflection, not a refusal of it; exact notation is the condition for shaping these short phrases, not a substitute for phrasing.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1908
    Passacaglia in D minorOp. 1Orchestra
    An eight-bar bass theme and continuous variations conclude Webern’s study with Schoenberg. The tonal language and orchestral scale distinguish it from his later numbered works.
  2. 1909
    Five MovementsOp. 5String quartet; later arranged for string orchestra
    Short, freely atonal movements in which harmonics, pizzicato and bow position help define the formal divisions.
  3. 1909; revised 1928
    Six Orchestral PiecesOp. 6Large orchestra; later reduced scoring
    The fourth piece is a funeral march with a mounting percussion climax. Compare the original and revised versions for Webern’s changing treatment of orchestral mass.
  4. 1911–13
    Six BagatellesOp. 9String quartet
    Six movements lasting only a few minutes altogether. The music is freely atonal and should not be analysed as a set of twelve-tone rows.
  5. 1917–21
    Six Songs after Poems by Georg TraklOp. 14Voice and changing chamber ensemble
    The three Abendland settings stand among poems whose images of twilight and decay are separated by precise instrumental gestures.
  6. 1928
    SymphonyOp. 21Clarinet, bass clarinet, two horns, harp and strings
    Two movements: a double canon in contrary motion and a variation movement. Its row has retrograde symmetry at the tritone.
  7. 1934
    Concerto for Nine InstrumentsOp. 24Flute, oboe, clarinet, horn, trumpet, trombone, violin, viola and piano
    Three movements. The opening separates the row into four related three-note cells; the piano’s reply makes the title’s concerto principle audible.
  8. 1936
    Variations for PianoOp. 27Piano
    Three movements, the last an explicit variation sequence. Symmetries and canons are articulated through register, dynamics, rests and contrasting kinds of attack.
  9. 1939–40
    Variations for OrchestraOp. 30Orchestra
    A continuous chain of short sections projects linked motivic shapes through changing instrumental groups. First performed in Winterthur in 1943 under Hermann Scherchen.
  10. 1941–43
    Cantata No. 2Op. 31Soprano, bass, mixed chorus and orchestra
    Six movements on texts by Hildegard Jone. Webern’s last numbered work gives serial counterpoint a vocal and choral span far beyond the early miniatures.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Webern’s plan for his unnumbered String Quartet of 1905 refers to Segantini’s triptych, whose panels were known to him as Werden, Sein and Vergehen — becoming, being and passing away. This central panel places cattle and figures within the Engadine mountains at sunset. The connection is documented in the early work’s conception; it does not establish a pictorial programme for the later Symphony or Concerto.

Georg Trakl

Abendland II

Published in Sebastian im Traum, 1915; set by Webern 1919
GermanSo leise sind die grünen Wälder Unsrer Heimat, Die kristallne Woge Hinsterbend an verfallner Mauer Und wir haben im Schlaf geweint; Wandern mit zögernden Schritten An der dornigen Hecke hin Singende im Abendsommer, In heiliger Ruh Des fern verstrahlenden Weinbergs; Schatten nun im kühlen Schoß Der Nacht, trauernde Adler. So leise schließt ein mondener Strahl Die purpurnen Male der Schwermut.
Our translationSo quiet are the green woods Of our homeland, The crystalline wave Dying against a ruined wall, And we have wept in our sleep; Walking with hesitant steps Along the thorny hedge, Singers in the summer evening, In the holy stillness Of the vineyard shining from afar; Shadows now in the cool lap Of night, mourning eagles. So quietly a moonbeam closes The purple wounds of melancholy.

Webern set this as the third of the Six Songs, Op. 14, for voice, clarinet, violin and cello. Trakl’s clauses do not settle into a continuous narrative: homeland, ruined wall, vineyard and eagles succeed one another without explanatory links. The score gives those transitions space through sparse instrumental entries. The German is the version used in the song; the English here is a new translation.

VIILineage

Passed toPierre BoulezIgor StravinskyGyörgy LigetiKarlheinz StockhausenLuigi Dallapiccola

Webern’s symmetrical rows and separation of musical parameters became central reference points after 1945. Stravinsky studied his scores during his turn towards serial composition; Boulez conducted and recorded the complete works. Ligeti learned from the control of instrumental detail while developing dense textures very different from Webern’s isolated events.
