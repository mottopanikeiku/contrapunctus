Life

When Peter Stadlen prepared the first performance of Webern’s Piano Variations in Vienna in 1937, the composer covered his score with instructions about expression. A phrase needed more vigour; another was an echo. Webern compared the opening movement to a late Brahms intermezzo. In the preface to his annotated edition, published in 1979, Stadlen remembered a “fervently lyrical mind bent on expressiveness”. The printed notes occupy remarkably little paper. Stadlen’s copy is crowded with the work of making them speak.

1883–1908A cellist at the desk

Webern was born in Vienna on 3 December 1883 and grew up in Graz and Klagenfurt, with holidays at Preglhof, the family estate in Carinthia. His mother, Amalie, played the piano and sang; he learnt piano and cello, joining the local concert society’s orchestra. The family made chamber music together. Later, living away from home, he returned in his letters to the woods and meadows around Preglhof, and to his mother’s grave at nearby Schwabegg.

In 1902 he entered the University of Vienna to study musicology with Guido Adler. His doctorate of 1906 was an edition of music from Heinrich Isaac’s Choralis Constantinus: sacred vocal parts crossing and answering one another on the page. Composition lessons with Schoenberg began in 1904, when Berg also joined the class. That summer Webern wrote Im Sommerwind, an orchestral idyll after Bruno Wille. His unnumbered String Quartet of 1905 took its three-part design from Giovanni Segantini’s Alpine triptych.

AmalieHis mother died in 1906. Writing to Schoenberg in January 1913, Webern explained the Six Orchestral Pieces through his memories of her death, including the heather he laid on her bier.

The Passacaglia, Op. 1, completed in 1908, was his last major assignment with Schoenberg. Plucked strings lay out an eight-bar theme in D minor; the orchestra builds variations above it, swelling into a sound still close to Mahler. A year later the Six Orchestral Pieces, Op. 6, made room for grief in much shorter spans. The fourth is a funeral march whose percussion gathers towards a violent climax. Webern returned to the score in 1928, cutting instruments and revising its phrasing as well as its sound.

1909–13A movement in a breath

In the Five Movements for String Quartet, Op. 5, the bow can move to the bridge, strike with its wood or release a harmonic; a few seconds bring a new voice from the same four instruments. The Six Bagatelles, Op. 9, written in 1911–13, go further. Several movements last less than a minute. Recalling them in a Vienna lecture in 1932, Webern said: “I had the feeling: once the twelve notes have run their course, the piece is finished.” In the fifth, faint pizzicatos and bowing near the bridge make even the pitch seem precarious.

Meanwhile he was earning money in theatre pits. Engagements took him to Teplitz, Danzig, Stettin and Prague; operetta filled much of the work, and he repeatedly resigned. In February 1911 he married Wilhelmine Mörtl, known as Minna. They had four children. The First World War brought intermittent military service and little time to compose. He came back to the Vienna area, settling first in Mödling, where Schoenberg also lived, and later in Maria Enzersdorf.

After 1918Music at work

At Schoenberg’s Society for Private Musical Performances, Webern supervised rehearsals and arranged orchestral scores for the players available. Critics were excluded, and applause was forbidden. Beyond those private rooms, he conducted workers’ choirs and the Social Democratic Workers’ Symphony Concerts. His programmes included Mahler and Brahms; from 1927 Vienna radio carried his conducting too. Between 1925 and 1931 he also taught piano and directed a choir at Vienna’s Jewish Institute for the Blind.

Words helped him write beyond the instrumental miniature. The Six Songs, Op. 14, occupied him between 1917 and 1921: Georg Trakl’s forests and ruined walls, set among changing combinations of clarinets and strings. In Abendland II, the singer has a clarinet, violin and cello for company. Later Hildegard Jone’s poetry supplied songs and both cantatas. Her words could bring a chorus into music that had once fitted inside a quartet’s fleeting exchange.

Before twelve-tone compositionThe Bagatelles belong to Webern’s freely atonal years. He adopted Schoenberg’s ordered twelve-note rows in the 1920s; the 1932 recollection looks back across that change.

In the Symphony, Op. 21, completed in 1928, a horn begins a line that continues in the clarinet and then the cello. Another horn answers with the intervals turned upside down. These two voices belong to a double canon: two pairs of lines imitating one another, dispersed through a small orchestra. The second movement is a theme and seven variations. Its clarinet theme divides into equal halves around the middle of the sixth bar.

The Concerto for Nine Instruments, Op. 24, followed in 1934. Its opening lays out four three-note groups, separated by instrument and by rhythm; after a silence, the piano presents the row on its own. Each group contains the same semitone and major third, turned or reversed. The exchange between ensemble and piano makes the construction sound like conversation.

1934–45The country closing in

In 1934 the Dollfuss dictatorship crushed Austria’s Social Democratic movement and dismantled the organisations that had employed Webern. His domestic conducting career collapsed. Private pupils and editorial work became increasingly important. Schoenberg had left for America in 1933; Berg died in 1935. Under Nazi rule Webern’s modern music was branded degenerate, and performances of his mature works largely moved outside the Reich.

Webern supported Hitler. On 2 May 1940 he wrote to his friend Josef Hueber celebrating the National Socialist state: “Created by this one man!” Hueber, then serving in the army, had sent him Mein Kampf; Webern’s reply of 4 March welcomed the book and foresaw the pacification of the whole world, perhaps as far as the Pacific. He also helped Jewish friends: he wrote a recommendation for Otto Jokl to emigrate and remained in contact with Josef Polnauer. His devotion to Schoenberg continued; in 1944 he sent birthday greetings through Willi Reich.

The late scoresVoices coming together

A melody dividedIn Webern’s Symphony, one canonic voice passes from horn to clarinet to cello. A single player’s part contains only stretches of the tune.

In 1935 he applied that way of hearing to Bach’s six-part Ricercar from The Musical Offering. Orchestral instruments take turns within the subject itself, changing colour as its chromatic notes descend. Webern conducted the première in London that April. At the piano, his Variations of 1936 brought the same attention to short phrases: in Stadlen’s working score, arrows and slurs join notes across the spaces, and the final movement begins with an instruction to play elegiacally.

The Variations for Orchestra, Op. 30, grow from two ideas in the opening bars, given to double bass and oboe. Webern heard Hermann Scherchen conduct them in Winterthur in 1943. That year he finished his Second Cantata, Op. 31, setting Jone for soprano, bass, mixed chorus and orchestra in six movements. The orchestral palette includes an alto saxophone and celesta. He never heard the cantata performed; its première took place in Brussels in 1950.

September 1945Outside the house

His son Peter was killed in an air attack in February 1945. In April Webern and Minna fled to Mittersill, joining their family. On the evening of 15 September he went outside to smoke. American soldiers were carrying out an operation against the black-market dealings of his son-in-law, Benno Mattel. One of them, Raymond Bell, shot Webern. He was sixty-one. Minna survived him until 1949, helping to secure publication of the music left behind.

After the war, Boulez and Stockhausen studied the canons and tiny instrumental exchanges as starting points for their own music. Performances remained scarce. Robert Craft’s recordings, made in Los Angeles in 1954–56, put the entire numbered output within reach on records: the large orchestra of the Passacaglia, the almost weightless Bagatelles and the voices of the late cantatas.

The Sound

𝄾Short phrases

In the Six Bagatelles, a quartet movement can be over in less than a minute. A plucked note has time to die away before a bowed one answers; a harmonic changes the sound without adding another player. The fifth piece is especially fragile, with soft pizzicato and bowing near the bridge. Its quiet is made from particular actions by four string players.

𝄢Canons

The Symphony’s first movement sets two canons going at once, with answering lines turning the intervals upside down. One melody travels from horn to clarinet to cello; its partner moves from the other horn through bass clarinet to viola. The matching routes help hold the lines together. Webern had spent his student years editing Isaac’s interwoven vocal parts; here the imitating voices keep changing instrument.

𝄆Twelve-note rows

The Concerto for Nine Instruments opens with four groups of three notes, each built from a semitone and a major third. Webern turns these groups upside down or backwards to make the row. Different instruments and rhythms separate them, then the whole ensemble rests. The piano answers alone with another statement of the row: a small, audible exchange at the start of a twelve-tone concerto.

𝄞String playing

The Five Movements, Op. 5, ask the quartet to change the sound at its source. Harmonics let a lightly touched string ring high; bowing near the bridge brings a wiry edge; the wood of the bow can strike or stroke the string. These changes happen inside short phrases. A violin can sound startlingly unlike itself before the cello has finished answering it.

𝆏Piano and expression

The Piano Variations leave much of the keyboard empty, then stretch a phrase across distant notes. Stadlen’s annotated score preserves Webern’s slurs and arrows, with an echo marked in the opening and an elegiac character at the start of the finale. His model for the first movement was a late Brahms intermezzo. The rests belong inside phrases whose changing warmth must survive the gaps between attacks.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1908
    Passacaglia in D minorOp. 1Orchestra
    Plucked strings introduce the eight-bar theme, then the orchestra builds a continuous span of variations. Webern completed it at the end of his lessons with Schoenberg.
  2. 1909
    Five MovementsOp. 5String quartet; later arranged for string orchestra
    The four players switch between ordinary bowing, harmonics and the wood of the bow within tightly compressed movements. Webern expanded the score for string orchestra in 1928–29.
  3. 1909; revised 1928
    Six Orchestral PiecesOp. 6Large orchestra; later reduced scoring
    The fourth piece is a funeral march, with percussion gathering into its climax. The 1928 revision changes phrasing and dynamics as well as reducing the orchestra.
  4. 1911–13
    Six BagatellesOp. 9String quartet
    Six movements occupy only a few minutes, their string sounds often reduced to a pluck or a breath of bow. The Amar Quartet, with Hindemith playing viola, gave the première in 1924.
  5. 1917–21
    Six Songs after Poems by Georg TraklOp. 14Voice and changing chamber ensemble
    Three successive songs set the three parts of Abendland. In its second part, the singer’s companions are clarinet, violin and cello.
  6. 1928
    SymphonyOp. 21Clarinet, bass clarinet, two horns, harp and strings
    Two interlocking canons open the first movement, their voices passing between instruments. The second has a clarinet theme, seven variations and a coda.
  7. 1934
    Concerto for Nine InstrumentsOp. 24Flute, oboe, clarinet, horn, trumpet, trombone, violin, viola and piano
    In the opening of this three-movement concerto, the ensemble shares out the row in four three-note groups. A collective rest separates that statement from the piano’s answer.
  8. 1936
    Variations for PianoOp. 27Piano
    Webern wrote the final movement first: a theme with five variations. Peter Stadlen prepared the 1937 première with him and preserved his detailed instructions in an annotated score.
  9. 1940–41
    Variations for OrchestraOp. 30Orchestra
    Double bass and oboe introduce the ideas that feed the theme and six variations. Webern attended the Winterthur première under Hermann Scherchen in 1943.
  10. 1941–43
    Cantata No. 2Op. 31Soprano, bass, mixed chorus and orchestra
    Six movements set Hildegard Jone, with alto saxophone and celesta among the orchestral instruments. The first performance came in Brussels in 1950, five years after Webern’s death.

Sister Arts

A couple drives cows and a calf home along a mountain path as the sun sets beyond the Engadine peaks. Webern planned his String Quartet of 1905 around Segantini’s three panels, which he knew as Werden, Sein and Vergehen — becoming, being and passing away. The quartet’s three sections fit into a single movement; a three-note idea runs through them.

Georg Trakl

Abendland II

Published in Sebastian im Traum, 1915; set by Webern 1919
GermanSo leise sind die grünen Wälder Unsrer Heimat, Die kristallne Woge Hinsterbend an verfallner Mauer Und wir haben im Schlaf geweint; Wandern mit zögernden Schritten An der dornigen Hecke hin Singende im Abendsommer, In heiliger Ruh Des fern verstrahlenden Weinbergs; Schatten nun im kühlen Schoß Der Nacht, trauernde Adler. So leise schließt ein mondener Strahl Die purpurnen Male der Schwermut.
Our translationSo hushed are the green forests Of our homeland, The crystal wave Dying away against a crumbling wall And in our sleep we have wept; Wandering with hesitant steps Along the thorn hedge, Singing in the summer evening, In the sacred stillness Of the vineyard shining far away; Shadows now in the cool lap Of night, grieving eagles. So gently a moonlit ray closes The purple marks of melancholy.

Webern set these fourteen lines in 1919 as the third song of Op. 14, giving the voice a clarinet, violin and cello. Trakl moves from the woods of home to a crumbling wall, then into the vineyard and the night. The people wander and sing in the middle of the poem; by its end they have become shadows and grieving eagles.

The early Im Sommerwind also makes the distance to the Bagatelles startling.
  • Variations for Piano, Op. 27Maurizio PolliniDeutsche GrammophonMy choice for the piano work: Pollini balances the clean edge of each attack with the need to carry a phrase across the rests. The few notes keep their poise without losing their capacity to sing.

Lineage

Webern
Passed toPierre BoulezIgor StravinskyGyörgy LigetiKarlheinz StockhausenLuigi Dallapiccola

Webern learnt counterpoint at two desks: editing Isaac for Guido Adler and composing under Schoenberg. His orchestration of Bach’s Ricercar makes that inheritance audible in changing instruments. After his death, Boulez carried the scores into concerts and two complete recording surveys; Robert Craft’s advocacy helped bring them to Stravinsky during his turn to serial composition.
