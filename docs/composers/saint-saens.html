CamilleSaint-Saëns

Born 9 October 1835, Paris, FranceDied 16 December 1921, Algiers, French Algeria

A pianist at ten and an organist for twenty years, he wrote the Organ Symphony, a dance of skeletons with a xylophone, and a zoo he forbade anyone to print.

Life

Camille Saint-Saëns was born at 3 rue du Jardinet in Paris on 9 October 1835. His father, a clerk in the Ministry of the Interior, died of consumption on 30 December, when the boy was not yet three months old. Two widows raised the sickly boy: his mother Clémence and her aunt Charlotte Masson, who taught him piano. At two and a half he pressed the keys one at a time and waited for each note to die away before the next. By his own account he already listened to the kettle on the drawing-room fire, a “microscopic oboe” that swelled until the water boiled.

The oboe in the kettleIn Musical Memories Saint-Saëns says Berlioz must have heard the same oboe, because he found it again in the ride to hell in La Damnation de Faust.

Children’s pieces gave the left hand nothing to do. “The bass doesn’t sing,” he told his teachers in disgust, and was given Haydn and Mozart. On 6 May 1846, aged ten, he played Beethoven’s Third Piano Concerto and a Mozart concerto in B♭ at the Salle Pleyel, with the whole programme from memory. Someone asked his mother what he would play when he was twenty. “He will play his own,” she said.

1848–1861The organ loft and the library

At the Conservatoire he sat in François Benoist’s organ class as an auditor until the day Benoist, with no one else prepared, put him at the instrument. Halévy, his composition teacher, often sent word that he would not come. Saint-Saëns went to the library: “what I devoured there of old and modern music is unimaginable.” In December 1853 François Seghers’s Société Sainte-Cécile played a symphony in E♭ that Seghers had presented as the work of an unknown German. Berlioz and Gounod discussed it at the rehearsal. When the composer’s name came out, Gounod wrote that he had taken on “the obligation of becoming a great master”.

At Seghers’s house in 1854 he first heard Liszt. The dreams of his youth, he wrote, were “only prose beside the Dionysian poem” that Liszt’s supernatural fingers conjured up. In December 1857 he took over the organ of La Madeleine, where Paris came to hear him improvise. He stayed twenty years.

Berlioz’s verdictTurned down for the 1864 Prix de Rome as too old, at twenty-eight, Saint-Saëns is reported to have heard Berlioz, on the jury, say: “He knows everything, but he lacks inexperience.” The Société Camille Saint-Saëns gives the remark as hearsay.

1861–1875Ars Gallica and a dance of death

In March 1861 he took over the piano class at the École Niedermeyer, where he thought himself “more pupil than teacher” and played the boys new scores by Chopin, Liszt, Schumann and Wagner. Gabriel Fauré, one of the boys, remembered it. During the siege of Paris his friend, the painter Henri Regnault, was killed at Buzenval on 19 January 1871. On 25 February Saint-Saëns and Romain Bussine founded the Société nationale de musique, motto Ars Gallica, to get new French music performed. When the Commune was proclaimed in March, Saint-Saëns, a former national guardsman and the Madeleine’s organist, left for London and stayed until the end of May. On 24 May the Commune shot the curé of the Madeleine, the abbé Deguerry.

Ars GallicaIn 1886 Vincent d’Indy wanted foreign and older composers on the programmes. Bussine and Saint-Saëns resigned, and César Franck became president.

On 24 January 1875 Édouard Colonne conducted Danse macabre at the Châtelet. A harp strikes the twelve strokes of midnight; a solo violin, its E string tuned down to E♭, scrapes out a tritone; a xylophone rattles the skeletons’ bones. The reviews were mixed. By March 1876 he could tell his publisher, Auguste Durand, that violinists no longer wanted their top strings tuned any other way. Ten days after the première he married Marie-Laure Truffot at Le Cateau-Cambrésis. She was nineteen, twenty years his junior.

1877–1881Weimar, and two cradles

In 1877 a legacy of 100,000 francs from his friend Albert Libon, left to free him from “the servitude of the Madeleine organ”, let him resign the loft. Paris opera directors had turned down Samson et Dalila, a biblical subject. Liszt, he wrote, “did not know a note of it” but had promised in 1870 to stage it at Weimar. On 2 December 1877 the court theatre gave it in German under Eduard Lassen. The Paris Opéra waited until 23 November 1892.

In 1878 both his sons died. André, two and a half, fell from the fourth-floor window of their flat on 28 May. Jean-François, seven months old, died on 7 July. In July 1881, at La Bourboule, the couple separated for good. They never divorced; he paid her a pension all his life, and she attended his funeral.

1886A symphony for Liszt and a zoo for friends

His book Harmonie et mélodie (1885) said of Wagner: “I have never been, I am not, I shall never be of the Wagnerian religion.” In January 1886 a Berlin concert was disrupted, and German theatres dropped his works. He withdrew to Chrudim in Bohemia and in a few days wrote a zoological fantasy for the Mardi Gras concert of his friend, the cellist Charles Lebouc. Le Carnaval des animaux was played on 9 March 1886. He allowed a few performances and forbade publication, except for Le Cygne; the rest appeared in 1922.

FossilsThe xylophone of Danse macabre returns in the ‘Fossils’ movement of the Carnival, which quotes that dance.

On 19 May 1886, at St James’s Hall in London, he played a Beethoven concerto, then conducted his Third Symphony for the Royal Philharmonic Society. He had meant to dedicate it to Liszt, who died that July; the dedication reads “to the memory of Franz Liszt”. Paris heard it that winter. Gounod called it “the French Beethoven”. “I have given it all that I had to give,” Saint-Saëns said. “What I have done I shall never do again.”

1888–1914Charles Sannois

His mother died on 18 December 1888. He gave much of his collection to the town of Dieppe and, for some fifteen years, kept no fixed address. In December 1889 he vanished. Newspapers reported him dead or mad. He had written to Louis Gallet that he wanted to look far away, under other skies, for “the means of making myself another self”. In April 1890 a newspaper portrait found him in the Canary Islands, travelling as Charles Sannois. Winter after winter he went south: Algiers, Las Palmas, Ceylon, Cairo.

In February 1896 he was in Egypt, at Luxor and Karnak with the painter Georges Clairin. On Sunday 22 March he reached Cairo, and the next day began to fill manuscript paper; by 26 March he had 35 pages of the Fifth Piano Concerto. He finished it on 15 April. At the Salle Pleyel on 2 June, fifty years after his debut, he played it himself. Its slow movement uses what he said was a Nubian love song, heard from boatmen on the Nile. In 1908 he wrote the music for a short film, L’Assassinat du duc de Guise, which the Société Camille Saint-Saëns calls the first film score.

1914–1921The last recital

In 1915 he sailed for California as a representative of French artists at the Panama–Pacific Exposition, taking Hail California, a large new piece for organ, orchestra and military band. At San Francisco he gave recitals and conducted three concerts. In 1916, at eighty, he toured France and then South America. “They will say that I cannot sit still,” he wrote; “I should love to stay at home and work in peace. Fate does not want it.”

On 6 August 1921 he gave his farewell recital at the Dieppe casino, after seventy-five years before the public. That November the Opéra revived Ascanio with the role of Scozzone again sung by a contralto. On 4 December he reached Algiers and took rooms at the Hôtel Oasis. He died there on the evening of 16 December. In his last year he had written sonatas for oboe, clarinet and bassoon. A national funeral followed at La Madeleine, and he was buried at Montparnasse, in the chapel he had built for his children.

The Sound

♮Two parts, four movements

The Third Symphony has two large movements, each hiding a slow movement or scherzo inside it. In Part I the Adagio introduction, with sighs from oboe, cor anglais and bassoon, leads into a restless Allegro in the strings; the organ first enters with soft chords in D♭ major under the slow melody. In Part II a scherzo in C minor has a brilliant Presto in C major for its middle section, with scales for the piano, and a huge C major chord on the organ opens the finale. The organ is silent for much of the work.

𝄢Colour borrowed from elsewhere

Danse macabre adds a xylophone for bones and retunes the solo violin’s E string to E♭ so that its open A and E♭ make a tritone. The Third Symphony brings in a piano duet in the upper register, a sonority he learned from Berlioz’s Lélio.

♭Journeys eastward

The slow movement of the Fifth Piano Concerto passes from an Arabic-sounding theme to a Nubian love song in G, then to quiet chirruping in F♯ major with a Chinese-sounding tune over tam-tam strokes. A cadenza follows that sounds like a Spanish guitar. Saint-Saëns called the movement a voyage to the Orient.

𝄞The piano as an equal

His concertos treat piano and orchestra as partners and are full of cadenza-like passages. The Second opens with a long solo that began in his organ improvisations, a homage to Bach, and its main theme came from Fauré.

♩A long song over small notes

In Le Cygne a cello sings a slow melody in G major while the accompaniment never stops moving: broken chords in six crotchet beats to the bar.

Sister Arts

Skeletons dance in the Nuremberg Chronicle’s woodcut of the same tradition that Saint-Saëns inherits. Danse macabre was a medieval allegory of death dancing with the Pope, the emperor and the pauper alike before it reached orchestra and xylophone.

Regnault was Saint-Saëns’s friend, and the two travelled in Brittany with other painters. He was killed at Buzenval on 19 January 1871. Saint-Saëns dedicated the fourth of his Mélodies persanes, Sabre en main, and the Marche héroïque to him.

Saint-Saëns reached Luxor and Karnak in February 1896 with the painter Georges Clairin and finished a violin sonata there. The Fifth Piano Concerto followed in Cairo. Its slow movement is built around a love song he said he heard sung by boatmen on the Nile.

Henri Cazalis (as Jean Lahor)

Égalité, Fraternité…

Mélancholia, 1868
FrenchZig et zig et zig, la mort en cadence Frappant une tombe avec son talon, La mort à minuit joue un air de danse, Zig et zig et zag, sur son violon. Mais psit ! tout à coup on quitte la ronde, On se pousse, on fuit, le coq a chanté. Oh ! la belle nuit pour le pauvre monde ! Et vive la mort et l’égalité !
Our translationZig and zig and zig, Death keeping time, Striking a tomb with his heel, Death plays a dance tune at midnight, Zig and zig and zag, on his violin. But hush! All at once the round breaks up, They jostle, they flee, the cock has crowed. Oh, what a fine night for the poor world! And long live death and equality!

The first and last stanzas of the poem Saint-Saëns set as a song in 1872 and then turned into Danse macabre. The solo violin takes Death’s fiddle, and an oboe takes the cock that ends the night.

Lineage

Saint-Saëns
Passed toGabriel FauréAndré MessagerEugène Gigout

His great-aunt gave him his first piano lessons, Stamaty the Kalkbrenner method and Benoist the organ. Liszt championed him from the 1850s and had Samson et Dalila staged. Fauré, Gigout and, later, Messager were his pupils at the École Niedermeyer.

    How did Saint-Saëns remember his childhood, the old Conservatoire, Liszt and the road to Samson et Dalila? His own account, with the story of the kettle’s oboe.

    What did his own circle say about the Egyptian Concerto? Dandelot records the composer’s remarks on its Andante and the first reviews, as cited in Henle’s preface (linked).
