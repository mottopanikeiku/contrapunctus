ILife

Charles Ives grew up in Danbury, Connecticut, where his father George directed bands, played the cornet and taught music. George had served as a Union bandmaster during the American Civil War. The son’s later recollections describe experiments with bands playing different tunes, quarter-tone instruments and singing in one key against an accompaniment in another. These accounts are filtered through a son’s memory of his father, but the musical questions they pose recur in the scores: how far apart can independent parts move before they stop making a perceptible whole?

Ives was also trained in conventional musicianship. He became a paid church organist as a teenager and wrote the organ Variations on America in 1891. At Yale, from 1894 to 1898, Horatio Parker taught him counterpoint, orchestration and large-scale form. His First Symphony belongs to that apprenticeship, with audible debts to European symphonic writing. George died in 1894, shortly after Charles entered Yale. The practical musical culture of Danbury subsequently became both a source of material and a standard against which Ives judged the professional concert world.

New YorkTwo kinds of work

After graduating in 1898, Ives entered the insurance business in New York rather than pursuing a full-time musical career. He continued as an organist until 1902, when he gave up his final church post. In partnership with Julian Myrick he built a successful insurance agency. Composition occupied evenings, weekends and holidays, but the work was not an untrained amateur’s private pastime. His organ playing, Yale studies and experience arranging music for practical use supplied a substantial technical base. Business income also allowed him to pay copyists, publish scores and later support other experimental musicians.

His Second Symphony combines familiar symphonic procedures with American tunes; the Third, subtitled The Camp Meeting, draws on Protestant hymnody. A quoted hymn can be divided among instruments, heard incompletely or delayed until the end of a movement. This reverses the familiar pattern in which a theme is announced clearly and then developed. The listener may first hear scraps whose identity becomes apparent only when the tune finally assembles. Ives’s music therefore relies on recognition across time as well as on simultaneous clashes between tunes.

Dating IvesSketch, first complete score, later revision and publication often carry different dates. The Unanswered Question is assigned to 1906 or 1908 in different accounts and was revised in the 1930s; a single early date does not describe every note of the familiar version.

Central Park in the Dark and The Unanswered Question organise separate musical layers. In the former, quiet strings persist while other instruments introduce the noises and popular music of a city night. In the latter, strings maintain a slow background, a trumpet repeatedly asks its short question and woodwinds offer increasingly impatient replies. The trumpet does not join the wind group’s accelerating argument. That independence is more exact than the word “chaos” suggests: each group has a defined role, and the ending depends on their refusal to arrive at a common answer.

Concord and New EnglandMemory as construction

Ives married Harmony Twichell in 1908. She shared his literary interests and supported his composing; they later adopted a daughter, Edith. The following decade produced much of Three Places in New England, the Holidays Symphony, the Second String Quartet, the Fourth Symphony and the Concord Sonata. The New England settings are particular rather than interchangeable. The “St Gaudens” in Boston Common refers to the monument to Robert Gould Shaw and the Black soldiers of the 54th Massachusetts Regiment; Putnam’s Camp imagines a child’s Fourth of July outing; The Housatonic at Stockbridge recalls a walk with Harmony.

The four movements of the Concord Sonata concern Emerson, Hawthorne, the Alcotts and Thoreau. They do not provide musical biographies. Ives takes characteristic modes of thought — Emerson’s argumentative leaps, Hawthorne’s fantastic stories, domestic music-making at the Alcotts’ home, Thoreau’s listening at Walden — as ways to organise musical behaviour. The first four notes of Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony recur within a larger network of themes. In Hawthorne, a board depresses a cluster of black keys; near the end of Thoreau, an optional flute carries a melody above the piano.

Not four independent portraitsA recurring theme, called the “human faith melody” in discussions of the Concord, links its movements. It becomes unusually plain in The Alcotts, clarifying fragments already heard in more crowded textures.

The Fourth Symphony makes similar questions orchestral. Its opening chorus asks for guidance in the hymn Watchman, tell us of the night. The second movement subjects march, hymn and popular material to competing rhythmic systems. A comparatively restrained fugue follows, before the finale separates a percussion process from the main orchestral movement and eventually recalls Nearer, My God, to Thee. The score’s practical difficulties are real: subsidiary conductors can be needed to coordinate simultaneous tempos. Such division is not a performance accident that should be tidied into one beat.

Publication and recognitionThe scores find performers

A serious illness in 1918 reduced Ives’s activity. He nevertheless prepared the Concord Sonata for printing in 1920 and distributed copies in January 1921, accompanied by his separately published Essays Before a Sonata. 114 Songs followed in 1922. That collection deliberately places conventional love songs, hymns, political statements and experimental pieces together. The variety prevents a neat division between an early tonal Ives and a later atonal one. He could still choose a plain diatonic setting when the text or remembered occasion called for it.

By about 1927 he had largely ceased new composition, though he continued revising earlier music; he retired from insurance in 1930. Henry Cowell promoted his work, Nicolas Slonimsky performed his orchestral scores, and John Kirkpatrick gave the complete public premiere of the Concord Sonata in New York in 1939. Lou Harrison conducted the first performance of the Third Symphony in 1946; it received the Pulitzer Prize the next year. These events came decades after the works’ beginnings, making reception history especially easy to confuse with compositional chronology.

Ives died in New York in 1954. The complete Fourth Symphony was not performed until 1965, when Leopold Stokowski conducted it with assistants in New York. The delayed performance matters materially: a work conceived through layered rhythms had to wait for the rehearsal time, editorial preparation and conducting arrangements that could make them audible. The insurance executive had not merely stored finished concert pieces in a drawer; manuscripts, revisions, practical readings and later editorial work all contributed to the performing texts through which the music became known.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄋Quotation

Hymns, patriotic tunes and popular songs enter as material already carrying associations. A tune may be fragmented before it is identified, or appear in one instrument against a different tune elsewhere. In cumulative forms, a complete melody arrives near the end after its components have been explored; recognition becomes a formal event.

♮Harmony

Independent tonal parts can produce a dissonant total without ceasing to be tonal individually. Ives also uses clusters, whole-tone formations and quarter-tones, but does not apply one system to every piece. The Three Quarter-Tone Pieces require two pianos tuned a quarter-tone apart; an ordinary single piano cannot reproduce their tuning.

𝅘𝅥Rhythm

One group may keep a march while another changes speed or subdivides the beat differently. Ragtime supplies syncopation, but some passages go further and maintain simultaneous tempos. The conflict is between identifiable rhythmic behaviours, not simply a succession of irregular bars. The Fourth Symphony requires those behaviours to remain distinct.

𝄞Space & orchestration

Distance is part of the musical design: a background string group, an isolated trumpet or an offstage ensemble need not sound as though they occupy the same acoustic plane. The Unanswered Question gives its three instrumental groups different musical times as well as different colours. Increased loudness in one layer need not change the others.

𝄐Form & memory

A remembered public event supplies more than a title. Decoration Day moves from mourning towards a returning march, while Putnam’s Camp interrupts the child’s outing with imagined historical scenes. Sudden cuts can function as changes of attention. Recurrent hymn fragments prevent these shifts from becoming an arbitrary sequence.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1891
    Variations on “America”Organ
    A teenager’s set of variations on the tune also used for God Save the King. Ives later revised the piece; its orchestral arrangement is by William Schuman, not Ives.
  2. 1897–1902
    Symphony No. 2Orchestra
    European symphonic writing and American tunes share the same five-movement structure. Leonard Bernstein conducted the first performance in 1951.
  3. 1901–04; revised 1909
    Symphony No. 3 “The Camp Meeting”Chamber orchestra
    Three movements draw on hymn tunes and earlier church music. Lou Harrison conducted the premiere in 1946; the work won the 1947 Pulitzer Prize.
  4. 1904–13
    A Symphony: New England HolidaysOrchestra; chorus in the final movement
    Washington’s Birthday, Decoration Day, The Fourth of July and Thanksgiving and Forefathers’ Day. Separate occasions retain their distinct musical procedures within the assembled cycle.
  5. 1906
    Central Park in the DarkChamber orchestra, including two pianos
    Quiet strings provide a continuing background beneath accumulating urban sounds and quoted popular music. The foreground disperses without converting the background into its own tempo.
  6. c. 1906–08; revised 1930s
    The Unanswered QuestionTrumpet, four woodwinds and strings
    Three layers: the slow strings, the trumpet’s repeated question and increasingly agitated wind answers. Early datings differ; the familiar version includes later revisions.
  7. c. 1908–14; revised 1929
    Three Places in New EnglandOrchestra; also a reduced-orchestra version
    Boston’s Shaw memorial, a child at Putnam’s Camp and the Housatonic River. The set reworks earlier material and exists in more than one orchestral version.
  8. 1910–25
    Symphony No. 4Large orchestra, chorus, keyboards and separately coordinated groups
    Most composition belongs to the 1910s; the wider date range includes revision. Its complete premiere took place in 1965, eleven years after Ives’s death.
  9. 1911–19; revised to 1947
    Piano Sonata No. 2 “Concord, Mass., 1840–60”Piano; optional additional flute and viola passages
    Emerson, Hawthorne, the Alcotts and Thoreau, linked by recurrent thematic material. Printed in 1920 and circulated in 1921; a substantially revised edition appeared in 1947.
  10. 1922
    114 SongsVoice and piano
    A self-published collection, not a single song cycle or a year’s output. It assembles decades of work, retaining tonal songs beside experimental and political ones.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Homer’s boys play a chain game outside a rural schoolhouse in the years after the Civil War. Ives’s childhood in Danbury supplied comparable games, parades and community music-making for later scores, including the child’s outing in Putnam’s Camp. This is a historical comparison, not a painting Ives is known to have used as a source. Homer’s strained line of connected bodies offers a visual counterpart to coordinated activity that threatens to pull apart.

Ralph Waldo Emerson

Self-Reliance — excerpt

1841
EnglishThe virtue in most request is conformity. Self-reliance is its aversion. Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist. He who would gather immortal palms must not be hindered by the name of goodness, but must explore if it be goodness. Nothing at last is sacred but the integrity of our own mind.

The opening movement of the Concord Sonata is named for Emerson, and Ives discusses his writing at length in Essays Before a Sonata. The connection is more specific than shared independence of temperament. Ives’s themes interrupt and qualify one another, as arguments do in the essays; the movement’s continuity does not depend on a tidy sequence of regularly balanced periods. This is prose, line-broken here for reading.

Henry David Thoreau

Walden, “Sounds” — excerpt

1854
EnglishThe whistle of the locomotive penetrates my woods summer and winter, sounding like the scream of a hawk sailing over some farmer’s yard, informing me that many restless city merchants are arriving within the circle of the town, or adventurous country traders from the other side.

The Concord Sonata’s last movement concerns Thoreau at Walden and includes an optional flute, the instrument Thoreau himself played. This passage makes mechanical and natural sound comparable without erasing their different sources. Ives similarly allows a church bell, hymn or march to enter an outdoor setting without requiring it to become conventional pictorial accompaniment. The excerpt is prose, not a poem or a text sung in the sonata.

VIILineage

Ives
Passed toHenry CowellElliott CarterJohn CageJohn AdamsCharles Ruggles

Ives’s influence travelled through performers, editors and the new-music organisations he supported as much as through formal teaching. Cowell promoted the scores; Carter engaged with their rhythmic independence; Cage valued their challenge to a single, orderly musical foreground. John Adams later used quotation and recognisable vernacular idioms in quite different harmonic and rhythmic settings.
