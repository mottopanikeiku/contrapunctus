69.1Life

Thomas Adès was born in London in 1971, the son of art historian Dawn Adès and poet and translator Timothy Adès. Piano study came before the large orchestral commissions: at the Guildhall School he worked with Paul Berkowitz on piano and Robert Saxton on composition, then studied at King’s College, Cambridge, from 1989 to 1992. Alexander Goehr and Robin Holloway were among his teachers there. His continuing work as a pianist matters to the notation: a passage that sounds like an uncontrolled rush may depend on an exact distribution of attacks between hands, registers or instrumental groups.

Chamber-music study with György Kurtág supplied a different kind of discipline, concentrating attention on the weight and duration of individual events. In Adès’s own early pieces, a few intervals often sustain sharply contrasting textures. Living Toys (1993), for fourteen players, combines high trumpet writing, low instrumental growls and rapidly changing ensemble configurations. In Darknesse Visible (1992), the notes of a Dowland song are redistributed into piano tremolos and separated registers, dismantling the historical material’s ordinary melodic continuity.

The early worksRecognisable objects under pressure

An interval cycleRepeated major thirds divide the octave into three equal parts; minor thirds divide it into four, whole tones into six. Several such lines moving together produce changing chords even when each line follows a simple rule.

The string quartet Arcadiana (1994) makes memory part of its structure. Seven movements refer to vanishing places or states: Venice at night, Mozart’s enchanted landscape, Schubert’s water song, Arcadia, Watteau’s Cythera, England and the river Lethe. The central movement, a distorted tango, brings death into those imagined retreats through Poussin’s tomb inscription. Sliding pitch, artificial harmonics and conflicting rhythmic layers can make a waltz or chorale lose its expected footing while its source remains partly recognisable.

Powder Her Face, premiered at the Cheltenham Festival in 1995, applies that method to opera. Philip Hensher’s libretto draws on Margaret, Duchess of Argyll, whose sexual life became evidence in a publicly reported divorce. Four singers take the roles, supported by fifteen instrumentalists. Tangos, waltzes and period popular-song styles establish the social rituals surrounding the Duchess, but the instrumental writing continually distorts them. The explicit sexual scene attracted much of the initial publicity; the score also makes audible the change from privilege to exclusion, with the Duchess increasingly separated from the people who judge and serve her.

Asyla (1997), commissioned for Simon Rattle and the City of Birmingham Symphony Orchestra, expands these contrasts across four orchestral movements. The Latin title can mean refuges or places of confinement. The scoring includes bass oboe, six percussionists and an upright piano tuned a quarter-tone flat. The third movement, Ecstasio, takes the repetitions and accumulating layers of dance-club music into an orchestra whose players must reproduce every accent. The surrounding movements give that mechanical activity a different frame, including the bass oboe’s exposed line and textures that seem to withdraw from the foreground.

Institutions and stagesComposing from the keyboard and podium

Adès’s Hallé residency in the 1990s was followed by the artistic directorship of the Aldeburgh Festival from 1999 to 2008. Like Britten, whose festival he inherited, he worked simultaneously as composer, pianist, conductor and programmer. His repertory as a performer has included Couperin, Janáček and contemporary music alongside his own scores. The Piano Quintet, premiered in 2001, uses a repeated sonata exposition. Within that old formal frame, the instruments can proceed through different metres and harmonic processes, converging at a cadence.

Shakespeare reshapedMeredith Oakes wrote a new libretto for The Tempest, condensing and reshaping the play’s language. Its short lines leave room for musical repetition.

The Tempest opened at the Royal Opera House in February 2004 with Adès conducting. Oakes’s text gives Prospero, a baritone, a conflict between control of the island and the release of his daughter Miranda. Ariel inhabits an extreme soprano register, making the spirit’s physical difference audible before any stage machinery intervenes. Caliban’s lyrical tenor writing complicates Prospero’s account of the island. Recurring harmonic processes connect the orchestral storm, the lovers’ music and the later ensemble scenes.

The Violin Concerto Concentric Paths followed in 2005, written for Anthony Marwood. Its three movements place the greatest weight in the middle, Paths, where changing harmonic cycles support the soloist’s wide-ranging lines and multiple-stopped chords. The outer movements, Rings and Rounds, turn more rapidly. Orchestral parts can traverse their repeating patterns at different rates. In the string quartet The Four Quarters (2010), the cycle of a day becomes a study in rhythmic recurrence, ending with the unequal twenty-five-semiquaver bars of The Twenty-Fifth Hour.

Death and repetitionThe room that cannot be left

Totentanz, premiered at the 2013 Proms, sets German words associated with the dance-of-death painting formerly in Lübeck’s Marienkirche. A baritone remains Death while a mezzo-soprano assumes the successive human identities he summons, from positions of authority towards the infant at the end. The large orchestra changes the character of each encounter without breaking the sequence. The final lullaby brings the last person in the procession before Death: his relationship to the infant requires a different musical scale.

For The Exterminating Angel, first staged at Salzburg in 2016, Adès and librettist Tom Cairns turned to Luis Buñuel’s 1962 film. Dinner guests find themselves unable to leave a room although no physical barrier confines them. Repetitions of gestures and social rituals become compositional material: recurring music can expose the failure of an attempted escape. The score includes ondes Martenot, and the soprano Leticia’s extreme register places her apart from the other guests.

Adès became an artistic partner of the Boston Symphony Orchestra in 2016. His Concerto for Piano and Orchestra, completed in 2018 and premiered there by Kirill Gerstein in March 2019, uses a recognisable fast–slow–fast design while placing formidable rhythmic demands on the soloist. The complete Dante Project reached the Royal Ballet in 2021, with Wayne McGregor’s choreography and Tacita Dean’s designs. Its score, Dante, moves through Inferno, Purgatorio and Paradiso; the successive sections require changes in the organisation of orchestral time as well as changes of scenery.

69.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Harmony

Individual lines can move by different fixed intervals: one by whole tones, another by minor thirds, another by major thirds. The vertical result changes from familiar triads to unfamiliar combinations without any line abandoning its pattern. The Piano Quintet and The Tempest both use this procedure.

𝅘𝅥Rhythm & metre

Parts can share a passage without sharing the same metrical organisation. Carefully notated changes make a line seem to hesitate, hurry or slip against its neighbours. In The Four Quarters, recurring patterns are heard through changing frames; the final movement’s 25/16 bars make an irregular cycle explicit.

𝄞Register & colour

Widely separated registers can create the impression of distinct physical spaces. Bass oboe, low winds and percussion occupy one extreme in Asyla; high string harmonics and bright keyboard attacks occupy another. Ariel’s soprano writing in The Tempest makes register a dramatic property.

𝄋Form & recurrence

Passacaglia, canon and sonata form remain working techniques. The central movement of Concentric Paths renews its harmonic cycles under changing violin figuration; the Piano Quintet retains an exposition repeat. Recurrence can also become a trap, as musical returns in The Exterminating Angel accompany the guests’ failure to leave.

𝄡Quotation & genre

A tango, chorale or waltz can be identifiable while its pitch structure and metre are being altered. Arcadiana and Powder Her Face alter such types while keeping them recognisable. Darknesse Visible goes further, rebuilding Dowland’s song from within.

69.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1993
    Living ToysChamber ensemble of fourteen players
    A child’s imagined adventures take instrumental form through rapidly changing textures and registers. The trumpet part and the abrupt contrasts between solo and ensemble writing make this a concentrated introduction to the early style.
  2. 1994
    ArcadianaString quartet
    Seven linked images of lost or threatened idylls. Watteau, Poussin, Mozart and Schubert are among the sources; the English chorale O Albion precedes the disappearing lines of Lethe.
  3. 1995
    Powder Her FaceChamber opera in two acts; four singers and fifteen players
    Hensher’s libretto draws on the Duchess of Argyll. Period dances and popular-song references are fractured by the chamber ensemble; the four singers cover a much larger set of social roles.
  4. 1997
    AsylaOp. 17Large orchestra with bass oboe, six percussionists and keyboards
    Four movements, including the dance-club-derived Ecstasio. A quarter-tone-flat upright piano belongs to the orchestral palette; first performed in Birmingham under Simon Rattle.
  5. 2004
    The TempestOpera in three acts; soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Meredith Oakes’s new text after Shakespeare. Prospero’s baritone, Ariel’s high soprano and Caliban’s tenor establish different claims on the island; the lovers’ music gradually escapes Prospero’s control.
  6. 2005
    Violin Concerto “Concentric Paths”Violin and orchestra
    Three movements, Rings, Paths and Rounds. The central movement carries the most weight, with harmonic cycles beneath multiple-stopped chords and sustained solo lines; written for Anthony Marwood.
  7. 2010
    The Four QuartersString quartet
    Premiered by the Emerson Quartet in 2011. A cycle of night, morning, day and a fictive twenty-fifth hour; in the finale, 25/16 metre is grouped 8 + 3 + 8 + 6, giving the recurring dance an unequal internal structure.
  8. 2013
    TotentanzMezzo-soprano, baritone and large orchestra
    Death, assigned to the baritone, addresses successive human figures sung by the mezzo. The German text is associated with Lübeck’s dance-of-death tradition; the final encounter is with an infant.
  9. 2016
    The Exterminating AngelOpera in three acts; large cast, chorus and orchestra with ondes Martenot
    Cairns’s libretto after Buñuel and Luis Alcoriza’s film. An unexplained inability to leave a room converts social repetitions into a mechanism of confinement; premiered at Salzburg under the composer.
  10. 2018
    Concerto for Piano and OrchestraPiano and orchestra
    Written for Kirill Gerstein and premiered in Boston in 2019. Three movements — Allegramente, Andante gravemente, Allegro giojoso — retain a conventional concerto outline while continuously unsettling its local rhythms.

69.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Hogarth gathers Ferdinand, Miranda, Prospero, Ariel and Caliban into an imagined scene. The link is Shakespeare’s Act I; no influence on the score is documented. Adès’s opera assigns this same set of relations distinct vocal registers and forms of musical agency. Hogarth places a controlling father, an invisible musician and two lovers in a single artistic space.

William Shakespeare

Ariel’s song: Full fathom five

The Tempest, c. 1610–11; printed 1623
EnglishFull fathom five thy father lies. Of his bones are coral made. Those are pearls that were his eyes. Nothing of him that doth fade But doth suffer a sea change Into something rich and strange.

Ariel sings to Ferdinand about his supposedly drowned father in Act I, scene 2. Meredith Oakes rewrites Shakespeare’s song for the opera. Its dramatic function remains musical deception, bringing Ferdinand towards Miranda. The very high soprano register gives the voice an audible distance from ordinary human speech; the transformation in the poem has a counterpart in the treatment of the singer.

This is a direct source for L’Embarquement, the fifth movement of Arcadiana. Watteau’s couples occupy the island associated with Venus while the ship and attendant cupids make departure part of the image. Adès places the movement among the quartet’s aquatic scenes; its high string writing and changing figuration also glance towards Debussy’s L’Isle joyeuse. The reference concerns an idyll already in the act of disappearing.

69.8Lineage

Adès
Passed toContemporary British operaInterval-cycle harmonyTwenty-first-century concerto writing

The Dowland reconstruction Darknesse Visible and the Schubert reference in Arcadiana make historical music part of the compositional material. Kurtág taught Adès as a chamber musician; Nancarrow’s player-piano studies offer a related model of simultaneous rhythmic rates, and Adès has arranged two of them for two pianos. None of these connections makes the resulting music a stylistic continuation of a single predecessor.
