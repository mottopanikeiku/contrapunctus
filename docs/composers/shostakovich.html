57.1Life

Dmitri Shostakovich was born in St Petersburg in 1906, the son of an engineer and a pianist. His mother, Sofiya, gave him his first piano lessons; in 1919 he entered the Petrograd Conservatory, where Leonid Nikolayev taught him piano and Maximilian Steinberg composition. The director, Alexander Glazunov, supported the young musician through the shortages following revolution and civil war. His father’s death in 1922 worsened the family’s finances, and Shostakovich earned money accompanying silent films. He graduated in piano in 1923 and composition in 1925: the graduation score was his First Symphony, written before his nineteenth birthday.

Nikolai Malko conducted its première in Leningrad on 12 May 1926. The orchestra includes a piano, whose abrupt interventions contribute to the symphonic argument. Clarinet, bassoon and trumpet exchange short phrases; a waltz can become a march without a change of personnel. International performances followed. Shostakovich still considered a performing career and received an honourable mention at the first Warsaw Chopin Competition in 1927. Thereafter his public piano appearances increasingly centred on his own works, including the concertos, chamber music and songs.

The experimental stageA nose with official rank

In the late 1920s he worked with theatre and film directors, including Vsevolod Meyerhold, Grigori Kozintsev and Leonid Trauberg. His score for The New Babylon (1929) juxtaposed dances, marches and quotations against images of the Paris Commune. The Nose, completed in 1928 and staged in 1930, made Gogol’s escaped body part a tenor who outranks its owner. Speech rhythms, overlapping ensembles and an interlude for percussion alone replace much conventional operatic continuity. The Second and Third symphonies, meanwhile, ended with revolutionary choruses: experimentation still flourished within public political commissions.

Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District, composed in 1930–32 after Nikolai Leskov, placed a merchant’s dissatisfied wife at its centre. Katerina’s sustained lyrical lines contrast with the clipped exchanges, orchestral violence and sexual caricature around her. The opera opened in Leningrad in January 1934 and soon travelled abroad. Shostakovich had married the physicist Nina Varzar in 1932; their daughter Galina was born in 1936 and son Maxim in 1938. Amid this international success, the household remained vulnerable to the political reversal that began in 1936.

FormalismIn Soviet cultural campaigns the term condemned supposedly self-serving technical experiment and insufficient accessibility. Its force was political; its musical meaning shifted with the accusation.

Stalin attended Lady Macbeth at the Bolshoi Theatre on 26 January 1936. Two days later an unsigned Pravda editorial, usually translated as “Muddle Instead of Music”, attacked its dissonance, dramatic treatment and audience appeal. Another editorial condemned the ballet The Limpid Stream. The opera disappeared from Soviet stages. The wider purges imprisoned or killed people in Shostakovich’s circle, including his patron Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky, executed in 1937. His correspondence records distress and reduced income.

The Fourth Symphony, completed in 1936, remained a large-scale, three-movement work indebted to Mahler, with abrupt changes of character, extreme orchestral demands and a long, quiet ending. Shostakovich withdrew it during preparations for its December première. Isaak Glikman later recalled that the Philharmonic’s director had pressed him to do so; accounts of the rehearsals also discuss practical difficulties. When Kirill Kondrashin finally conducted it in Moscow on 30 December 1961, Shostakovich allowed the music to stand without wholesale revision.

1937–45Public success and wartime witness

The Fifth Symphony restored his public position in November 1937. Yevgeny Mravinsky conducted the première; Steinberg’s diary records an exceptional ovation, while other witnesses understood the applause as solidarity with the persecuted composer. Its four movements and D minor framework offered a more recognisable symphonic design than the Fourth. The Largo excludes brass and divides the strings into multiple parts; the finale eventually reaches D major over insistently repeated notes. Official rehabilitation and listeners’ experiences of grief coexisted. The ending admits competing interpretations: a celebration of victory, or a portrayal of compulsory celebration.

He began teaching at the Leningrad Conservatory in 1937 and wrote his First String Quartet in 1938. The Piano Quintet of 1940 brought the piano into a sequence beginning with a prelude and fugue; it received a Stalin Prize in 1941. Such honours show how official and private music overlapped. He performed the quintet with the Beethoven Quartet, whose players became central collaborators in his chamber works. The quartets, too, were composed for named performers and heard in public concert halls.

After Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, Shostakovich remained in Leningrad, undertook civil-defence duties and composed the first three movements of the Seventh Symphony. Evacuated in October, he completed the finale in Kuibyshev in December. Samuil Samosud conducted the world première there on 5 March 1942. The performance inside besieged Leningrad followed on 9 August under Karl Eliasberg, with the depleted radio orchestra reinforced by military musicians. Broadcasts and foreign performances made the symphony an Allied emblem. Its first movement’s repeated march grows through instrumentation and accumulation, heard against the documented circumstances of the siege.

The Eighth Symphony of 1943 answered wartime circumstances with a vast C minor opening and a passacaglia. The Ninth, completed in 1945, was unexpectedly compact, with piccolo and trombone giving its opening a brittle ceremonial character. Chamber music registered particular losses. The Second Piano Trio, completed in 1944, commemorates his friend Ivan Sollertinsky. It begins with high cello harmonics and later combines a repeated chordal lament with a dance finale using Jewish-inflected melodic and rhythmic gestures.

1948 and afterWorks held back, forms renewed

The Party’s February 1948 resolution again condemned Shostakovich, alongside Prokofiev, Khachaturian and others, for formalism. He lost his positions at both the Moscow and Leningrad conservatories and made public statements of self-criticism. Film commissions helped support him. He also completed the First Violin Concerto and From Jewish Folk Poetry, both first publicly performed in 1955. The song cycle sets Russian translations of Jewish texts for soprano, contralto, tenor and piano. Its domestic laments and final songs of Soviet prosperity belong to the same eleven-song sequence, holding public rejoicing beside poverty and bereavement.

Jewish subjects were a sustained interest. He completed and orchestrated his pupil Veniamin Fleischmann’s unfinished opera Rothschild’s Violin in 1944 after Fleischmann’s wartime death, and maintained a close musical friendship with Mieczysław Weinberg. During the post-war antisemitic campaigns, the decision to compose From Jewish Folk Poetry carried particular weight. In the Thirteenth Symphony of 1962, the bass soloist and male chorus sing Yevgeny Yevtushenko’s Babi Yar, directly confronting antisemitism in the sung words.

At the Bach celebrations in Leipzig in 1950, Shostakovich heard Tatiana Nikolayeva and served on the competition jury. His Twenty-four Preludes and Fugues followed in 1950–51, moving through all major and minor keys in a circle of fifths, each major key followed by its relative minor. Bach supplied a formal precedent, but the voices include his own modal turns, repeated notes and violent contrasts of register. The Tenth Symphony, completed after Stalin’s death in 1953, made a four-note musical signature conspicuous. The reading of its second movement as a portrait of Stalin rests on contested memoir testimony.

Signing in notesGerman notation gives D, Es, C, H: D–E♭–C–B. The final pitch is B natural, not B♭; “S” is heard as Es, the name of E♭.

The Eighth Quartet, written at Gohrisch near Dresden in July 1960, combines this DSCH figure with quotations from earlier works, including the Second Piano Trio and Lady Macbeth. Its published dedication commemorates victims of fascism and war. In a letter to Glikman of 19 July, however, Shostakovich described it as a memorial to himself and identified its autobiographical references. He joined the Communist Party in 1960, an event friends associated with severe distress. The quartet’s personal testimony and his official responsibilities existed at the same time.

The final decadeDeath named, memory quoted

Illness increasingly restricted his mobility and piano playing; his third wife, Irina, whom he married in 1962, helped sustain his working life. The Fourteenth Symphony of 1969, dedicated to Britten, sets eleven poems by Lorca, Apollinaire, Küchelbecker and Rilke for soprano, bass, strings and percussion. Death is its stated subject, without a concluding resurrection. The Fifteenth Symphony of 1971 brings back a full orchestra and quotes Rossini’s William Tell and Wagner. The quotations’ narrative significance remains open to interpretation. Percussion and isolated instrumental lines repeatedly interrupt its tonal continuity.

The Fifteenth Quartet of 1974 consists of six linked Adagios, with movements named Elegy, Serenade, Intermezzo, Nocturne, Funeral March and Epilogue. The Viola Sonata, his last completed work, followed in 1975 for Fyodor Druzhinin. Its final Adagio recalls Beethoven’s Moonlight Sonata through the piano’s accompaniment and harmonic pacing. Shostakovich died in Moscow on 9 August, before the sonata’s public première. Reduced forces and exposed lines distinguish the textures of these late works.

Memoirs and documentsLetters, dated manuscripts, contemporary reviews and later recollections answer different questions. A recollection can preserve genuine experience without supplying a reliable transcript of a conversation.

Solomon Volkov’s Testimony, published in 1979 as Shostakovich’s memoirs, transformed Western interpretations of the composer. Laurel Fay demonstrated that passages on the signed chapter-opening pages reused previously published material, challenging the claim that the signatures authenticated the whole narrative. Some friends and musicians defended the book’s portrait; its provenance remains disputed, as do its accounts of the Fifth, Seventh and Tenth symphonies. The Glikman letters and other documents provide independent evidence of persecution and private criticism.

57.2The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Monogram & memory

DSCH is D–E♭–C–B: an ascending semitone, descending minor third and descending semitone. The Tenth Symphony and Eighth Quartet place this recognisable pitch order in changing rhythms and textures. In the quartet, quotations from earlier compositions make memory a structural device: a returning theme can refer both to an earlier movement and to a different work.

♭Tonal pressure

Keys remain audible even when chromatic lines obstruct their cadences. In the Fifth Symphony, D minor becomes D major at the end, but the prolonged repeated A prevents the change of mode from being a simple release of tension. Major and minor thirds, semitone neighbours and stubborn bass pedals can make an apparently plain triad unstable without abandoning a tonal centre.

𝅘𝅥Rhythm & repetition

The Seventh’s first-movement march develops through repeated statements over a snare-drum pattern, steadily adding instrumental weight. Elsewhere a waltz, polka or gallop can be isolated and exaggerated. Listen for the difference between regular metre and displaced accent: a steady pulse can support phrases that stumble across its strong beats.

𝄞Orchestration

Large orchestras repeatedly contract to a single exposed player. In the Ninth Symphony’s Largo, the solo bassoon answers a brass proclamation; register, articulation and numbers deepen the contrast in loudness. The Fourteenth removes winds and brass entirely, allowing strings, voices and percussion to articulate the poems without the usual symphonic mass.

𝄢Counterpoint & ground bass

The Twenty-four Preludes and Fugues range from two-voice counterpoint to five-voice writing; the final D minor fugue combines two subjects. A passacaglia works differently: a recurring bass phrase supports changing material above it. The First Violin Concerto’s third movement uses that principle before the soloist’s extended cadenza leads into the finale. Repetition measures the variation.

𝄾Voices & declamation

The Nose gives Gogol’s bureaucratic exchanges sharply profiled speech rhythms and sets competing voices against one another. Lady Macbeth contrasts Katerina’s longer lyrical spans with the surrounding caricatures. From Jewish Folk Poetry uses solos and vocal combinations to distinguish lullaby, lament and public rejoicing; the Thirteenth Symphony makes the bass soloist’s words collective through a male chorus.

57.3Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1924–25
    Symphony No. 1 in F minorOp. 10Orchestra with piano
    The conservatory graduation work, first performed in 1926. Short wind solos and a prominent orchestral piano interrupt the four-movement design with changes of pace and character.
  2. 1927–28
    The NoseOp. 15Opera in three acts; soloists, chorus and chamber orchestra
    Gogol’s story of a civil servant and his independent nose. First staged in 1930; rapid scene changes, speech-derived singing and a percussion interlude carry the satire.
  3. 1930–32
    Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk DistrictOp. 29Opera in four acts; soloists, chorus and orchestra
    After Leskov. Successful from its 1934 première until the 1936 attack; later revised as Katerina Izmailova, Op. 114.
  4. 1935–36
    Symphony No. 4 in C minorOp. 43Large orchestra
    Three movements, with a short central intermezzo between two extended outer movements. Withdrawn before its planned 1936 première and first heard in 1961; the finale subsides into a quiet, unresolved aftermath.
  5. 1937
    Symphony No. 5 in D minorOp. 47Orchestra
    Four movements from a tightly argued opening through a scherzo and string-dominated Largo to a D major conclusion. The first performance under Mravinsky produced an exceptional public ovation.
  6. 1941
    Symphony No. 7 in C major “Leningrad”Op. 60Large orchestra with expanded brass and percussion
    Dedicated to Leningrad and completed after evacuation to Kuibyshev. Its accumulating first-movement march is only one part of a four-movement symphony that also contains extended passages of lament.
  7. 1943–44
    Piano Trio No. 2 in E minorOp. 67Violin, cello and piano
    Dedicated to Sollertinsky’s memory. High cello harmonics open the work; a repeated piano-chord sequence supports the Largo, and a Jewish-inflected dance drives the finale.
  8. 1948
    From Jewish Folk PoetryOp. 79Soprano, contralto, tenor and piano; orchestral version Op. 79a
    Eleven settings of Russian translations of Jewish folk texts. Written during the renewed anti-formalist campaign and first performed publicly in its piano version in January 1955.
  9. 1950–51
    Twenty-four Preludes and FuguesOp. 87Solo piano
    All major and minor keys, ordered by the circle of fifths with relative minors. Tatiana Nikolayeva’s Bach playing at Leipzig was a stimulus; the cycle ends in a D minor double fugue.
  10. 1953
    Symphony No. 10 in E minorOp. 93Orchestra
    An extended opening Moderato precedes a short, concentrated scherzo. The DSCH signature becomes explicit in the later movements and survives the finale’s changes of tempo and character.
  11. 1960
    String Quartet No. 8 in C minorOp. 110Two violins, viola and cello
    Five connected movements built around DSCH and quotations from earlier works. The public dedication to victims of fascism and war coexists with the autobiographical explanation in his letter to Glikman.
  12. 1969
    Symphony No. 14Op. 135Soprano, bass, string orchestra and percussion
    Eleven settings concerned with death, using poems by Lorca, Apollinaire, Küchelbecker and Rilke. Dedicated to Britten; the abrupt ending refuses a conventional symphonic apotheosis.

57.5Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Kustodiev drew Shostakovich in the year he entered the Petrograd Conservatory and inscribed the portrait to his young friend. The memorial apartment’s catalogue distinguishes this 1919 drawing from a second portrait made at Gaspra in 1923; the reproduced Commons file is the earlier work. It documents an actual connection with the painter, before the First Symphony made the student an internationally performed composer.

Nikolai Gogol

The Nose — the nose refuses its owner

1836
RussianВы ошибаетесь, милостивый государь. Я сам по себе. Притом между нами не может быть никаких тесных отношений. Судя по пуговицам вашего виц-мундира, вы должны служить в сенате или, по крайней мере, по юстиции. Я же по ученой части.
Our translationYou are mistaken, sir. I exist in my own right. Besides, there can be no close relations between us. Judging by the buttons on your undress uniform, you must serve in the Senate or at least in the judiciary. I, however, belong to the learned professions.

In the cathedral encounter, Kovalyov’s nose defeats his claim through the language of official rank and departmental boundaries. Shostakovich’s opera preserves that collision between an impossible event and exact bureaucratic procedure, giving the nose a tenor voice and Kovalyov a baritone. The extract follows the Russian text in the Academy of Sciences collected edition; the English translation is ours. Leskov provides the separate source for Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District.

57.7Lineage

Drew fromJohann Sebastian BachLudwig van BeethovenModest MussorgskyGustav MahlerAlban Berg
Shostakovich
Passed toBenjamin BrittenMieczysław WeinbergBoris TishchenkoAlfred Schnittke

Bach supplied contrapuntal models, Beethoven the precedent of a sustained quartet sequence, and Mussorgsky models for Russian declamation and songs about death. Mahler’s large orchestral forms were especially important to the Fourth Symphony. Weinberg was a close friend and musical interlocutor; Tishchenko was a composition pupil. His friendship with Britten is marked publicly by the dedication of the Fourteenth Symphony.
