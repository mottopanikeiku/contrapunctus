10.1Life

François Couperin belonged to a family whose connection with the organ of Saint-Gervais in Paris lasted for generations. His father Charles died in 1679, when François was ten; Michel-Richard de Lalande temporarily held the post intended for the boy. Couperin took it up in 1685. His uncle Louis, who had died before François was born, had already established a distinctive harpsichord language of broken chords, dance rhythms and unmeasured preludes. The younger Couperin inherited a profession and a repertory. His own published harpsichord music would increasingly substitute named character pieces for a predictable succession of dances.

In 1693 he won one of the four organist positions at Louis XIV’s royal chapel. Serving by quarter, he combined court employment with his Parisian responsibilities. He taught members of the royal family and took part in chamber performances; in 1717 he became a royal chamber harpsichordist. These overlapping appointments help explain his range: a musician who supplied an organ verset for a service might also teach the fingering of an ornament or play a concert with violin and viol.

Church and chamberVoices above a bass

The two organ masses published in 1690 belong to the alternatim practice in which organ pieces alternate with sung liturgical sections. Their titles distinguish parish use from religious communities. Registrations such as tierce en taille identify both a colour and a position: a solo melody in the tenor register speaks through a reed-like combination of organ stops while the other parts accompany it. The long concluding pieces require an ear for contrasting masses of sound. Couperin’s organ writing addresses the instrument’s sustained tone, registrations and ceremonial functions.

TenebraeThe offices for the last three days of Holy Week were commonly anticipated on the preceding afternoons. Couperin’s title pour le Mercredy belongs to that practice.

The three surviving Leçons de Ténèbres, published in 1714, set the opening of the Lamentations of Jeremiah for the Wednesday of Holy Week. The first two require one high voice, the third two, all above continuo. Couperin gives the Hebrew letters that mark the verses extended melismas, then makes the Latin words more syllabic and rhetorically directed. The service’s ritual darkness frames writing that tests breath, ornament and the ability to turn a dissonance into an expressive syllable. In the third lesson, two voices imitate and suspend against one another without the support of a large ensemble.

The clavecinTwenty-seven ordres

His four books of Pièces de clavecin appeared in 1713, 1717, 1722 and 1730. The groups are called ordres: a common tonal centre can hold together portraits, dances, scenes and pieces with deliberately puzzling names. Major and minor versions of the same tonic often coexist. Some titles point to people or theatrical types; others resist secure identification. Les Barricades mystérieuses, in the sixth ordre, has acquired many explanations, none established by the score. The music combines a low register, overlapping parts, repeated harmonic patterns and phrase boundaries softened by suspensions.

An ornament is a noteCouperin supplied a table explaining his signs and insisted that performers observe them. A trill beginning above the written note can place a dissonance on the beat; omitting it changes the harmony as well as the decoration.

L’Art de toucher le clavecin, first published in 1716 and revised in 1717, addresses posture, fingering, articulation and the execution of ornaments. A harpsichord cannot sustain a note indefinitely or make an already struck note swell. Couperin therefore controls perceived accent through timing, overlap and release. His aspiration creates a small separation; his suspension delays the attack of a note. These instructions shape a phrase while preserving the underlying pulse. The book’s preludes give players material through which to practise that speech-like delivery.

French and Italian styles were political as well as musical categories. Couperin admired Corelli’s trio sonatas and initially circulated some of his own under an Italian disguise, an episode he describes in the preface to Les Nations. The four Concerts royaux, published in 1722, grew from performances for Louis XIV in 1714–15. Their scoring is flexible; the same lines can be distributed among violin, flute, oboe, viol and continuo. Les Goûts-réunis and the apotheoses of Corelli and Lully make the proposed reconciliation explicit: Italian sonata writing and French dance should be usable within one musical language.

The last publicationsSmall ensembles, exact instructions

The two suites of Pièces de violes appeared in 1728. Their sustained bowed lines expose the dissonances that a harpsichord can only suggest through decay and renewal; La Pompe funèbre concentrates its weight in repeated gestures and dark register. Ill health gradually reduced Couperin’s duties, and his daughter Marguerite-Antoinette became a royal harpsichordist. He died in Paris in 1733. Ravel’s Le Tombeau de Couperin, written almost two centuries later, honours the older French suite through dances, ornaments and sharply separated instrumental lines.

10.2The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄾Touch & articulation

A plucked string makes the beginning and end of a note unusually important. Couperin specifies little separations, delayed attacks and fingerings that allow neighbouring parts to overlap. On a modern piano, the sustaining pedal can blur the very distinction the notation makes; finger legato and carefully timed releases reveal more.

♮Harmony & suspension

The expressive note is often the one that holds its pitch when the bass changes. It becomes a dissonance, then resolves by step. In Les Barricades mystérieuses, the parts overlap so that the chord seems continually to be arriving after its bass. The effect depends on independent durations.

𝅘𝅥Ornament

Trills, mordents, slides and appoggiaturas are written into the grammar of the phrase. An ornament can identify a cadence, stretch a stressed syllable in a vocal line, or give a short keyboard note an apparent continuation. Couperin’s ornament table is part of the score’s instructions.

𝄋Dance & character

The title may name a person or a scene, but the phrase still has a physical rhythm. Sarabandes distribute weight differently from courantes; a rondeau returns to its refrain after contrasting couplets. The ordres allow these forms to coexist with other pieces, held together by key and by contrasts of texture and register.

𝄞The reunited tastes

Italian imitation and sequences meet French dance metres and ornament. In Les Nations, an Italianate sonata precedes a French suite within each large group. The combination is audible in how two upper voices pursue one another over continuo, then return to the accent pattern and phrase length of a dance.

10.3Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1690
    Messe pour les paroisses · Messe pour les couventsOrgan, alternating with sung liturgy
    Two sequences of liturgical organ pieces. Registrations and the position of the solo line determine the sound as directly as the notes.
  2. 1713
    Premier Livre de pièces de clavecinHarpsichord
    Ordres 1–5. Conventional dances coexist with named pieces; Couperin supplies a detailed table of ornaments.
  3. 1714
    Leçons de Ténèbres pour le MercredyOne or two high voices and continuo
    Three surviving lessons on the Lamentations of Jeremiah. Elaborate Hebrew-letter melismas frame more direct declamation of the Latin verses.
  4. 1716–17
    L’Art de toucher le clavecinHarpsichord; pedagogical treatise with pieces
    Fingering, touch and ornament explained through practical instructions, an allemande and eight preludes.
  5. 1717
    Second Livre de pièces de clavecinHarpsichord; two instruments in selected pieces
    Ordres 6–12, including Les Barricades mystérieuses and the two-harpsichord Allemande à deux clavecins.
  6. 1722
    Troisième Livre de pièces de clavecin · Concerts royauxHarpsichord; flexible chamber ensemble for the concerts
    Ordres 13–19 and four chamber concerts. The thirteenth ordre includes the sequence of masked characters Les Folies françoises, ou les Dominos.
  7. 1724–25
    Les Goûts-réunis · L’Apothéose de Corelli · L’Apothéose de LullyChamber ensemble with continuo
    French and Italian practices are joined through dance, sonata and explicitly programmatic tributes to the two composers.
  8. 1726
    Les NationsTwo treble instruments and continuo
    Four large ordres, each coupling an Italianate sonata with French dances: La Françoise, L’Espagnole, L’Impériale and La Piémontoise.
  9. 1728
    Pièces de violesBass viol and continuo
    Two suites; the second ends with La Pompe funèbre and La Chemise-blanche. Bowed sustain gives the suspensions a different physical presence from the keyboard pieces.
  10. 1730
    Quatrième Livre de pièces de clavecinHarpsichord
    Ordres 20–27. The final book continues the mixture of social portrait, dance and instrumental character piece.

10.5Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

A dancer performs before a seated company while musicians occupy the architectural setting. The painting dates from the years of Couperin’s first two harpsichord books, whose dances and social portraits similarly depend on a small audience’s knowledge of gesture and deportment. The link is their shared Parisian culture. Watch the difference between the central dance and the spectators’ individual poses; listen for the comparable distinction between a piece’s dance pattern and its local ornaments.

Watteau submitted this painting to the Académie royale in 1717; its lovers and theatrical landscape helped establish the category fête galante. Couperin’s second harpsichord book appeared in the same year. Both works belong to a culture in which pastoral costume and gestures of courtship could be presented as art for private spectators. No documented programme connects this painting with Les Barricades mystérieuses.

Passed toJean-Philippe RameauClaude DebussyMaurice Ravel

Corelli and Lully are named participants in Couperin’s imagined reconciliation of national styles. Rameau inherited the French keyboard vocabulary but redirected it towards more demonstrative technique and harmonic argument. Ravel’s Le Tombeau de Couperin commemorates the older French suite without quoting Couperin’s pieces.

Questions to take away

  1. How do three musical lines become a conversation?

    Couperin places an Italian-style trio sonata before the French dances of La Piémontaise, played here by four musicians sharing three written parts. How do the two upper lines exchange the lead above their shared bass?
