Life

Ravel’s father took his sons to factories to see the machinery, and his mother sang to them. Pierre-Joseph was a Swiss-born engineer; Marie Delouart was Basque and had grown up in Madrid. Maurice was born in Ciboure on 7 March 1875, and the family moved to Paris three months later. He went back to the Basque coast many times. Spain appears often in his music, in the plucked-guitar attacks of Alborada del gracioso, the habanera in the Rapsodie espagnole and the clocks of a Toledo clockmaker’s shop in L’heure espagnole.

1889–1905Conservatoire, Apaches and Debussy

He entered the Paris Conservatoire at fourteen, studied piano with Charles-Wilfrid de Bériot and lost his place in 1895 for failing to win the required prizes. André Gedalge gave him counterpoint lessons from 1897, and in January 1898 he entered the composition class of Fauré. Ricardo Viñes, his friend since adolescence, gave the first performances of Jeux d’eau, Miroirs and Gaspard de la nuit, pieces he could not have played himself with comparable command.

Les ApachesRavel, Viñes, the poet Tristan Klingsor, the composer Maurice Delage and the critic Calvocoressi belonged to this informal circle. The name was Parisian slang for street hooligans. Their enthusiasms included Poe, Russian music and Debussy.

In 1902 Ravel attended every performance in the first run of Debussy’s Pelléas et Mélisande. The two men were friends, though never intimate. Their supporters then began arguing over who had invented what. Ravel’s Habanera dated from 1895 and his Jeux d’eau from 1901, both before Debussy’s Estampes. The comparisons soured the friendship. Ravel also helped support Lilly Debussy after her husband left her. His admiration for the music continued. “For Debussy the musician and the man I have had profound admiration,” he told an audience in Houston in 1928, “but by nature I am different from him.”

A retirement already requestedThéodore Dubois had asked to retire before the 1905 scandal. Fauré succeeded him as director of the Conservatoire that year and began reforming the institution.

Ravel tried five times for the Prix de Rome. In 1901 he won a lesser prize; in 1905, with the Quartet and Jeux d’eau already published, he failed the preliminary round. Every one of the six candidates admitted to the final was a pupil of Charles Lenepveu, who sat on the jury. Pierre Lalo, usually a hostile critic, protested. Ravel spent part of that summer cruising the Rhine on the yacht of Misia and Alfred Edwards.

1901–12Piano, chamber and orchestral works

Jeux d’eau, dedicated to Fauré, sets arpeggios over held bass notes. Liszt had shown a way; Ravel worked out his own fingering and pedalling. The five Miroirs, each dedicated to an Apache, range from solitary birdcalls to a boat pitched on immense waves. In Gaspard de la nuit he printed Bertrand’s poems beside the music. Ondine sings within the rippling chords, the bell of Le Gibet tolls the same B♭ throughout, and Scarbo moves abruptly between loud attacks and silence.

The String Quartet of 1902–03 links its four movements through returning themes; its scherzo begins with all four players plucking, two against three. In the orchestral songs Shéhérazade, to poems by Klingsor, a traveller imagines Asia, a woman hears a flute through a wall, and a beautiful stranger declines an invitation.

In December 1907 the young Ralph Vaughan Williams arrived in Paris and took lessons from Ravel four or five times a week until the end of February. The Rapsodie espagnole was first played in March 1908 by Colonne’s orchestra at the Châtelet. Four years later Ravel took the piano part in the French première of Vaughan Williams’s On Wenlock Edge.

Ma mère l’Oye began as piano duets for the children of Cipa and Ida Godebski. Ravel drew on Perrault, Madame d’Aulnoy and Madame Leprince de Beaumont. Laideronnette, Empress of the Pagodas is built on a pentatonic scale to suggest an imagined China, and in the orchestration the Beast is given to a contrabassoon and, once he becomes a prince, to a solo violin. He expanded the suite into a ballet in 1912. On 8 June that year, Nijinsky and Karsavina danced Daphnis et Chloé for Diaghilev, with Fokine’s choreography, Bakst’s scenery and Pierre Monteux conducting. In his autobiographical sketch of 1928 Ravel said he had wanted “a vast musical fresco”, and that he had been concerned with “faithfully reproducing the Greece of my dreams” more than with archaism. A wordless chorus joins the orchestra. The daybreak scene is scored with rushing figures for water and birdsong in the woodwind, and the final dance is in five beats to the bar.

His other stage work was a smaller comedy. L’heure espagnole, composed in 1907 and staged in 1911, is set in a clockmaker’s shop, where the clockmaker’s wife entertains several would-be lovers. The men hide inside clocks, and a muleteer carries the clocks up and down the stairs.

1914–19War and Le Tombeau de Couperin

The outbreak of war found him at Saint-Jean-de-Luz, finishing his Piano Trio, which he described as “de couleur basque”. The opening divides eight quavers into 3+2+3, a rhythm that also appears in his sketches for Zazpiak-Bat, a Basque piano concerto he abandoned. The scherzo takes the name Pantoum from a Malay verse form in which lines return; the slow movement is built on an eight-bar bass theme. He hurried to complete the trio so that he could enlist. Rejected for aviation service, he became a lorry driver and carried supplies near Verdun in 1916. Illness and an operation interrupted his service. His mother died in January 1917.

He refused to join the wartime league campaigning against contemporary German and Austrian music. French composers, he told its committee in 1916, would impoverish themselves by refusing to hear foreign colleagues. He had studied Schoenberg’s Pierrot lunaire and collaborated with Stravinsky on Mussorgsky’s Khovanshchina in 1913.

DedicationsThe Rigaudon of Le Tombeau de Couperin is dedicated to the brothers Pierre and Pascal Gaudin. The Toccata names Joseph de Marliave, husband of Marguerite Long. Each other movement bears another dead friend’s name.

Le Tombeau de Couperin, begun before the war, was finished in the summer of 1917 at Lyons-la-Forêt, in the villa of Madame Fernand Dreyfus. Written in memory of the dead, it consists of a prelude, a fugue, dances and a toccata. The piano writing calls for a light touch. Long gave the first performance of the piano suite in 1919. Ravel orchestrated four movements that year and gave the prelude’s quick notes to the oboe, in an exposed part. Rhené-Baton conducted the première of the orchestral version in February 1920.

1920–28Le Belvédère and La Valse

Diaghilev rejected La Valse in 1920; Camille Chevillard conducted the Lamoureux Orchestra in its concert première that December. The music opens on low rumblings in the orchestra, out of which a Viennese ballroom takes shape; the waltz fills out and then breaks apart. Ravel had conceived the project before the war. In 1922 he told Maurice Emmanuel that it expressed “an upward progression of sound”, to which the stage would add light and movement. The score places its imperial court around 1855.

At Le Belvédère, the house he bought in Montfort-l’Amaury in 1921, he collected mechanical toys and coffee cups with holes. “Does it never occur to these people that I may be artificial by nature?” Calvocoressi recalled him asking, in his 1933 memoir. In the Sonata for Violin and Cello the two instruments do the work of an ensemble. His 1922 orchestration of Mussorgsky’s Pictures at an Exhibition, commissioned by Koussevitzky, gives the old castle a saxophone and the ox-cart a high tuba solo.

Colette wrote the libretto of L’Enfant et les sortilèges, with its disobedient child and talking furniture; the opera was first performed in Monte Carlo in 1925 under Victor de Sabata. The score includes a foxtrot, coloratura writing and imitations of animal cries, and at the end the child binds an injured squirrel’s paw. The Chansons madécasses followed. They set Parny’s poems of desire and colonial violence for voice, flute, cello and piano.

Jazz was already in his Violin Sonata before his North American tour of 1928. On 7 March, his fifty-third birthday, he was at Eva Gauthier’s home in New York with Gershwin among the guests; the next day he recommended Gershwin to Nadia Boulanger. That year Ida Rubinstein commissioned Boléro. When plans to orchestrate Albéniz’s Iberia fell through, Ravel made a ballet from two tunes and a two-bar pattern on the snare drum. Nijinska put Rubinstein on a tavern table. On the night of the Opéra première, 22 November 1928, Ravel was in Madrid, on a concert tour of Spain and Portugal. The orchestra grows, the pulse stays steady, and near the end the music moves abruptly from C major to E major. Toscanini conducted the concert première in New York in November 1929; in January 1930 Ravel recorded the piece with the Lamoureux Orchestra, which Albert Wolff had prepared.

1929–37The two piano concertos

Paul Wittgenstein, who had lost his right arm in the war, commissioned the Left-Hand Concerto. It begins low in the strings and contrabassoon, and the soloist enters with a cadenza only after a long orchestral climb. Ravel told Calvocoressi he wanted to avoid “the impression of insufficient weight in the sound-texture” beside a two-handed solo. The piano part leaps between bass and melody while the pedal holds the notes together. Ravel noted that the later “jazz section” is built from the themes of the first section. Wittgenstein gave the first performance in Vienna on 5 January 1932. In Paris that month, Marguerite Long introduced the Concerto in G with Ravel conducting, and the two toured it across Europe until May. In Vienna in February, Ravel heard Wittgenstein play the Left-Hand Concerto with a second piano in place of the orchestra, and was scandalised. Between the jazz-inflected outer movements of the G major Concerto is an Adagio in E major, in which the piano plays alone at length before passing its melody to the cor anglais.

The diagnosisRavel lost language and the ability to carry out learned actions. The underlying disease remains unidentified, as there was no post-mortem examination. His symptoms had begun before his 1932 taxi accident.

Don Quichotte à Dulcinée sets three poems by Paul Morand: a Chanson romanesque in the form of a guajira, a Chanson épique in the form of a Basque zortziko, and a Chanson à boire in the form of an Aragonese jota. The cycle was written for a Chaliapin film, which used Ibert’s music instead. It was Ravel’s last completed composition, and his dispute with the film company reached court in June 1933. Martial Singher sang the première of the orchestral version on 1 December 1934. A few days earlier Ravel had conducted Boléro at the Opéra-Comique, his last known appearance as a conductor. A neurological illness gradually left him unable to write or play. In tests with Théophile Alajouanine and Manuel Rosenthal he could still detect mistakes in his music and hear when a piano was out of tune. Clovis Vincent operated on his brain in December 1937. Ravel died on the 28th, aged sixty-two.

Ravel in the Web of Influence

The Sound

♮Three-hand piano texture

A bass note rings under the pedal while a tune is played in the middle of the keyboard and quick notes move above it. Jeux d’eau and Ondine require the pianist to keep these three layers separate with two hands. The chords keep their added sevenths and ninths, so the harmony stays dense even in a passage held over one bass note.

♭Fixed note

The B♭ bell in Le Gibet continues to toll while the chords around it change. In the Prélude à la nuit of the Rapsodie espagnole, a descending four-note figure returns throughout the movement. Ravel often held a single note or a short phrase in place while the harmony around it changed.

𝄞Orchestration

In Boléro, a horn, celesta and two piccolos play the tune together at different pitches, reinforcing the horn’s overtones until they sound like a new instrument. Elsewhere Ravel gave an existing instrument an unexpected role. The contrabassoon plays the Beast in Ma mère l’Oye, and in his orchestration of Mussorgsky the melody of the old castle goes to an alto saxophone.

𝅘𝅥Dance and imbalance

The Piano Trio opens in 8/8, divided 3+2+3, with a shorter group in the middle. Daphnis et Chloé ends with a dance in 5/4. In La Valse, the three-beat ballroom rhythm takes increasingly violent accents until it comes apart. In Boléro the drum repeats its two bars while the orchestra adds more and more instruments on top.

𝄢Spanish idiom in the keyboard writing

In Alborada del gracioso the piano plucks, strums and repeats single notes in the manner of a guitarist; its central song is written in the style of an unaccompanied singer’s phrase. The Rapsodie espagnole incorporates Ravel’s 1895 Habanera. His last songs, Don Quichotte à Dulcinée, move from the guajira’s shifting groups of beats to a Basque zortziko, and use a jota for the drinking song.

𝄋Older forms and precise fingering

The Tombeau contains a three-part fugue and dances whose steps remain distinct under their ornaments. The Trio’s passacaglia begins with an eight-bar theme low in the piano and builds upwards through the strings. Ravel used these old forms throughout his life. The Menuet antique dates from 1895, and the title Valses nobles et sentimentales refers to Schubert.

Works

A selection from the composer’s works.

  1. 1899; orch. 1909
    Pavane pour une infante défunte (Pavane for a Dead Princess)Piano; later orchestra
    An imagined Spanish court, with a G major melody that returns between episodes. Ravel gave the opening of the orchestral version to a solo horn.
    Recordings in the Hall
  2. 1901
    Jeux d’eauPiano
    Ravel dedicated the piece to Fauré. It is built on two themes, and Ravel never orchestrated it. The water figuration is written for struck notes and the sustaining pedal.
  3. 1902–03
    String Quartet in F majorTwo violins, viola and cello
    Themes from the opening return in all four movements. In the scherzo the quartet plays largely pizzicato, with groupings of two and three beats set against each other.
    Recordings in the Hall
  4. 1903
    ShéhérazadeVoice and orchestra; poems by Tristan Klingsor
    The three songs are about a dream of Asia, a flute heard through a wall and a refused invitation. The long Asie takes up more than half the cycle. An earlier orchestral overture has the same title.
  5. 1904–05
    Miroirs (Mirrors)Piano
    Five pieces, each dedicated to a member of Les Apaches. Ravel orchestrated the boat on the ocean and the Spanish morning song; the moths, birds and bells remained piano pieces.
    Recordings in the Hall
  6. 1907–08
    Rapsodie espagnoleOrchestra
    Prelude to the night, malagueña, habanera and fair. The Habanera had existed as a piece for two pianos since 1895; the descending four-note figure of the prelude returns later in the score.
  7. 1908
    Gaspard de la nuitPiano; after Aloysius Bertrand
    The three movements depict a water spirit, a gallows and a tormenting dwarf, and Bertrand’s poems are printed before them. In Le Gibet, placed between Ondine and Scarbo, a B♭ is repeated throughout.
    Recordings in the Hall
  8. 1908–10; ballet 1911–12
    Ma mère l’Oye (Mother Goose)Piano duet; orchestral suite and expanded ballet
    Written for the Godebski children, then orchestrated and enlarged into a ballet. Sleeping Beauty’s pavane and the fairy garden frame tales by Perrault, Madame d’Aulnoy and Madame Leprince de Beaumont.
    Recordings in the Hall
  9. 1909–12
    Daphnis et ChloéBallet: large orchestra and wordless mixed chorus
    Diaghilev’s ballet of shepherds and pirates is Ravel’s largest orchestral score. Suite No. 2 takes the dawn, the lovers’ pantomime and the final dance in five beats from the complete ballet.
  10. 1914
    Piano Trio in A minorPiano, violin and cello
    Finished as war broke out. It has an opening in uneven Basque-inflected rhythm, a scherzo named after the Malay pantoum verse form, a passacaglia and a finale in changing metres. The strings often play far apart, with the piano in the register between them.
  11. 1914–17; orch. 1919
    Le Tombeau de CouperinPiano; four-movement orchestral version
    Six piano movements bearing the names of seven men killed in the war. The orchestral version keeps the Prélude, Forlane, Menuet and Rigaudon; the Fugue and Toccata remain piano-only.
    Recordings in the Hall
  12. 1919–20
    La ValseOrchestra; also versions for piano and for two pianos
    Waltzing couples are seen through clouds in an imperial ballroom around 1855. In a letter of 1922 to Maurice Emmanuel, Ravel rejected a political reading of the work’s collapse.
    Recordings in the Hall
  13. 1922
    Pictures at an Exhibition — orchestration of MussorgskyOrchestra, including alto saxophone, celesta, two harps and percussion
    Koussevitzky commissioned this orchestration of Mussorgsky’s 1874 piano suite. The solo trumpet in the Promenade, the saxophone in The Old Castle and the high tuba in Bydło were Ravel’s choices.
  14. 1917–25
    L’Enfant et les sortilèges (The Child and the Spells)One-act opera: soloists, chorus and orchestra; libretto by Colette
    A destructive child is confronted by the furniture, crockery and animals he has harmed, which are now able to answer back. First performed in Monte Carlo in 1925 under Victor de Sabata.
  15. 1928
    BoléroBallet for large orchestra, including saxophones and two snare drums
    Two tunes and a two-bar drum pattern, repeated through a long crescendo. After a long stretch in C major, the music turns to E major near the end. Nijinska’s production for Ida Rubinstein opened at the Paris Opéra on 22 November 1928.
    Recordings in the Hall
  16. 1929–30
    Piano Concerto for the Left Hand in D majorLeft-hand piano and orchestra
    Commissioned by the war-wounded Paul Wittgenstein, who played the first performance in Vienna on 5 January 1932. Low strings and contrabassoon open a single movement in which the piano part carries bass, chords and melody with one hand and the pedal.
  17. 1929–31
    Piano Concerto in G majorPiano and orchestra
    Dedicated to Marguerite Long, who gave the first performance in January 1932. The E major Adagio opens with a long piano solo; later the cor anglais plays the melody while the piano accompanies with running figuration.
  18. 1932–33
    Don Quichotte à DulcinéeBaritone and piano or orchestra; poems by Paul Morand
    A lover’s pledge (a guajira), a prayer (a Basque zortziko) and a drinking song (an Aragonese jota), intended for Chaliapin’s film of Don Quixote. The film used Ibert’s music; these three songs became Ravel’s last completed work.

Aloysius Bertrand

Ondine — opening and closing paragraphs

1842; text from the 1895 edition
French— « Écoute ! — Écoute ! — C’est moi, c’est Ondine qui frôle de ces gouttes d’eau les losanges sonores de ta fenêtre illuminée par les mornes rayons de la lune ; et voici, en robe de moire, la dame châtelaine qui contemple à son balcon la belle nuit étoilée et le beau lac endormi. […] Et comme je lui répondais que j’aimais une mortelle, boudeuse et dépitée, elle pleura quelques larmes, poussa un éclat de rire, et s’évanouit en giboulées qui ruisselèrent blanches le long de mes vitraux bleus.
Our translation“Listen! — Listen! — It is I, Ondine, brushing these drops of water against the resonant diamond panes of your window, lit by the dull rays of the moon; and here, in a watered-silk dress, the lady of the castle watches from her balcony the beautiful starry night and the beautiful sleeping lake. […] And when I answered that I loved a mortal woman, sullen and vexed, she shed a few tears, burst out laughing, and vanished in showers that streamed white down my blue stained-glass windows.

Ondine taps on a man’s window, offers him her underwater kingdom and is rejected in favour of a mortal woman. She cries, laughs and turns into rain. Ravel printed Bertrand’s poem before the first movement of Gaspard de la nuit. Near the end of the movement the rapid water figuration stops briefly for an unaccompanied melodic line. The omitted paragraphs contain her invitation; the French follows the 1895 edition.

Doré shows Perrault’s seven children gathered under a lantern at the ogre’s door. For Petit Poucet Ravel set an earlier moment in the same tale, when the children try to find their way home after birds have eaten the trail of breadcrumbs. The phrases run through changing bar lengths, and birdcalls in the orchestra break in on them repeatedly.

Gause’s painting shows uniforms, chandeliers and a crowded floor in an imperial ballroom of the kind named in La Valse. Before the war Ravel had planned a tribute to Johann Strauss called Wien. In the finished score the couples first appear through clouds. By the end the three-beat dance rhythm has broken down.

Maurice Ravel

Preface to the score of La Valse

1920
FrenchDes nuées tourbillonnantes laissent entrevoir, par éclaircies, des couples de valseurs. Elles se dissipent peu à peu : on distingue une immense salle peuplée d’une foule tournoyante. La scène s’éclaire progressivement. La lumière des lustres éclate au ff. Une Cour impériale, vers 1855.
Our translationWhirling clouds give glimpses, through gaps, of waltzing couples. Little by little they scatter: one makes out an immense hall filled with a turning crowd. The stage grows gradually brighter. The light of the chandeliers bursts out at the ff. An imperial court, about 1855.

Ravel’s scenario includes a lighting cue: the chandeliers blaze at ff. The waltz begins much more quietly, with divided double basses and fragments in the bassoons. The dancers come into view gradually.

Fauré and Gedalge taught him; Chabrier and Satie showed him that a short piece could have a distinct character. Liszt’s water music lies behind Jeux d’eau, and Couperin’s name heads the memorial suite. Ravel and Debussy admired each other before their supporters made a rivalry of it.

Vaughan Williams studied with Ravel in 1907–08; Manuel Rosenthal became both pupil and interpreter. Gershwin asked for lessons and was refused, Ravel wanting him to remain himself. The later links to Messiaen and Boulez concern particular chords and instrumental sounds; Boulez’s recordings show his sustained engagement with Ravel.

Further reading

  • Roger Nichols, Ravel (Yale University Press, 2013 paperback).biography

    A biography based on written and oral evidence, including testimony from the composer’s associates.

  • Arbie Orenstein, ed., A Ravel Reader (Dover, 2003).letters

    Documents, correspondence and interviews, as primary sources, can be read alongside later interpretations.

  • Deborah Mawer, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Ravel (Cambridge University Press, 2000).survey

    Discusses piano, chamber, orchestral and stage works, with chapters on performance and early recordings.
