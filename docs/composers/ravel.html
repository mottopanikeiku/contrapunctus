Composers / V. The French Line

MauriceRavel

Born 7 March 1875, Ciboure, French Basque CountryDied 28 December 1937, Paris

An engineer of instrumental colour who made a piano imitate water, an orchestra imitate a machine, and old dances carry the names of the dead.

ILife

Ravel was born in Ciboure, across the harbour from Saint-Jean-de-Luz, to Marie Delouart, a Basque woman who had lived in Madrid, and Pierre-Joseph Ravel, a Swiss-born engineer. The family moved to Paris when Maurice was three months old. His father encouraged music as well as an interest in machinery; his mother’s songs gave him an early acquaintance with Spanish idioms. Basque ancestry and Spanish musical theatre remained distinct resources: the uneven pulse of the Piano Trio is not the habanera of the Rapsodie espagnole. He returned to the Basque coast for holidays and composition, but received his musical education in Paris.

1889–1905A student the examinations could not accommodate

He entered the Conservatoire in 1889, initially as a pianist, and studied with Charles-Wilfrid de Bériot. Failure to win the required prizes cost him his place in 1895. Readmitted in 1897 to Fauré’s composition class, he also learnt counterpoint from André Gedalge. Fauré encouraged the individual ear without relaxing the demands of construction; Gedalge supplied the discipline behind the fugues and independent inner parts. Ravel did not become a virtuoso pianist. Ricardo Viñes, his friend since adolescence, introduced much of the difficult piano music, including Jeux d’eau, Miroirs and Gaspard de la nuit.

Outside the Conservatoire, Ravel belonged to Les Apaches, an informal circle whose name borrowed Parisian slang for street gangs. The poet Tristan Klingsor, the critic Michel-Dimitri Calvocoressi and the composer Maurice Delage were among its members. They read Poe and contemporary poetry, played Russian music and defended Debussy’s Pelléas et Mélisande in 1902. Ravel’s admiration for that opera did not make him Debussy’s pupil. His early Habanera for two pianos dated from 1895; Jeux d’eau preceded Debussy’s Estampes. Arguments about who had borrowed which sonority eventually damaged the composers’ relations.

Not a forced resignationDubois had requested retirement before the 1905 scandal. The familiar story that Ravel’s rejection directly forced him out compresses separate events. Marc Rigaudière distinguishes them in his account of the competition.

Between 1900 and 1905 Ravel made five attempts at the Prix de Rome, winning a lesser prize in 1901 but never the first. In 1905 he was eliminated before the final cantata round, although the String Quartet and Jeux d’eau were already in circulation. All six successful preliminary candidates were pupils of the juror Charles Lenepveu. The resulting press controversy concerned institutional partiality as much as musical modernism. Fauré succeeded Théodore Dubois as director that year and reformed the Conservatoire. Ravel continued outside it; the institution’s refusal had not prevented him from learning its craft.

The piano laboratoryWater, mirrors and nocturnal apparitions

Jeux d’eau (1901), dedicated to Fauré, made pianistic figuration carry the harmony. Its arpeggios spread added-note chords across registers; sustained bass notes allow the upper notes to change without a conventional chord progression at every attack. Liszt’s water pieces supplied a precedent, but Ravel organised the work around two themes rather than a succession of effects. The title refers to fountains and playing water, not an orchestral seascape. He never orchestrated it: the damper pedal, the decay of each struck note and the interference between registers belong to its construction.

The String Quartet in F major (1902–03) combined recurring themes with four recognisable movement types. The second movement’s pizzicato writing turns four bowed instruments into a plucked ensemble, with triple and compound-duple groupings competing inside the pulse. In the orchestral songs Shéhérazade (1903), Klingsor’s imagined Asia became a matter of French verbal rhythm, solo woodwinds and sustained orchestral colour. The long first song accumulates a traveller’s catalogue; the two shorter songs reduce the scale to a flute heard through a wall and an encounter with an unresponsive stranger. Neither work abandons proportion for atmosphere.

Three layers, two handsRavel’s piano writing frequently separates bass, melody and figuration into different registers. The pianist must give each layer a different weight. The apparently orchestral result depends on balance and pedalling, not on making every note equally loud.

Each of the five pieces of Miroirs (1904–05) was dedicated to an Apache. Oiseaux tristes, for Viñes, places isolated calls against a nearly motionless background; Une barque sur l’océan expands arpeggios into overlapping waves; Alborada del gracioso turns repeated notes and glissandi into a guitar-like mechanism. In Gaspard de la nuit (1908), Ravel printed Aloysius Bertrand’s three prose poems with the music. Ondine carries a singing line inside rapid repeated sonorities. Le Gibet keeps returning to the same B♭ while harmonies change around it. Scarbo makes its dwarf appear through repetitions, sudden silences and violent changes of register.

1908–12Children’s books and the Ballets Russes

Ma mère l’Oye began as five piano duets for the children of Ravel’s friends Cipa and Ida Godebski. Perrault supplied Sleeping Beauty and Little Thumb; other stories came from Madame d’Aulnoy and Madame Leprince de Beaumont. Ravel restricted the original keyboard writing without simplifying the harmonic ear: pentatonic figures, modal turns and widely spaced registers make small means sufficient. He orchestrated the suite in 1911, then expanded it into a ballet premiered in 1912. The transformation of the Beast is an instrumental event: the low contrabassoon’s character gives way to a solo violin.

Two larger stage works required different kinds of precision. L’heure espagnole, completed in 1907 and first staged in 1911, is a bedroom farce in a Toledo clockmaker’s shop; men hide inside clocks while the orchestral mechanisms keep running. For Diaghilev’s Daphnis et Chloé, composed in 1909–12, Ravel worked with the choreographer Michel Fokine and designer Léon Bakst. Nijinsky and Karsavina danced the lovers at the Paris premiere on 8 June 1912, conducted by Pierre Monteux. Ravel called the score a choreographic symphony: recurring themes connect the action, and a wordless chorus enlarges the orchestra rather than narrating it.

The dawn music in Daphnis begins with moving figures before the broad melody becomes audible: changes of register, density and instrumental entry produce the sunrise. The final dance is principally in 5/4, making collective celebration depend on an asymmetrical bar. This was an invented antiquity, not an attempt to recover ancient Greek music. Ravel’s later autobiographical sketch described a Greece of his imagination. Bakst’s painted rocks, trees and architecture provided another constructed version of the same landscape; the music and designs belonged to the same theatrical commission, not a resemblance discovered afterwards.

1914–17A trio, a lorry and a memorial suite

At Saint-Jean-de-Luz in 1914, Ravel was working on the Piano Trio when war broke out. He hurried to finish it before entering military service. Its opening 8/8 metre divides into 3+2+3, a personal adaptation of Basque rhythmic practice rather than a transcription of a village dance. A scherzo called Pantoum alludes to a Malay verse form; the slow Passacaille builds over an eight-bar theme first heard in the piano’s bass. The finale uses irregular metres and harmonics to obtain an orchestral spread from three players.

Rejected for aviation service, Ravel became an army driver and carried supplies near Verdun in 1916. Illness and an operation interrupted his service; his mother’s death in January 1917 added a separate bereavement. His patriotism did not entail a musical boycott. In 1916 he refused to join a league campaigning against contemporary German and Austrian music, arguing that French composers would impoverish themselves by refusing to hear foreign colleagues. This was the composer who had recently studied Schoenberg’s Pierrot lunaire and worked with Stravinsky on Mussorgsky’s Khovanshchina.

Six movements, seven menThe Rigaudon commemorates the brothers Pierre and Pascal Gaudin. The other five piano movements each name one man; the Toccata remembers Joseph de Marliave, husband of the pianist Marguerite Long.

Le Tombeau de Couperin, begun before the war and completed in 1917, is a six-movement piano suite in which every movement commemorates friends killed in the fighting. Its title also invokes the French tradition of the tombeau, a musical memorial. Prelude, fugue, dances and toccata offer no continuous funeral narrative: ornament, counterpoint and precisely weighted dissonance do the commemorative work. Long gave the first performance in 1919. Ravel’s orchestral version of that year selects four movements and changes their order, transferring much of the keyboard’s light articulation to exposed woodwind lines.

After the warWaltzes, miniatures and a house of objects

La Valse (1920) returns to a project conceived before the war: a homage to the Viennese waltz. Fragments emerge from low orchestral turbulence, assemble into ballroom phrases, then become overloaded by acceleration and displaced accents. Diaghilev declined to stage it. The interpretation of its ending as the destruction of pre-war Europe is compelling, but it is not an authorised programme: in a letter to Maurice Emmanuel in 1922, Ravel rejected readings based on the collapse of the Habsburg monarchy. The score specifies a Viennese imperial court around 1855, not a battlefield.

In 1921 Ravel settled at Le Belvédère in Montfort-l’Amaury. Its small rooms held his piano, ornaments, mechanical toys and objects whose functions were deliberately deceptive, including cups with holes. The house survives with these collections. Calvocoressi recalled Ravel’s insistence that artifice could be natural to him; the distinction matters in music that constructs a fairytale or a dance without pretending to document it. At the same time, the Sonata for Violin and Cello (1920–22) reduced the available resources to two lines, with biting intervals and little room for harmonic padding.

Serge Koussevitzky commissioned the 1922 orchestration of Mussorgsky’s Pictures at an Exhibition. Ravel did not simply distribute piano chords among instrumental families. A solo trumpet establishes the opening Promenade; alto saxophone gives The Old Castle a sustained, unfamiliar voice; the high tuba solo in Bydło tests the boundary between weight and lyricism. His colours are so familiar that they can conceal the distinction between Mussorgsky’s composition and Ravel’s instrumentation. The piano original remains the necessary comparison, particularly where attack, register and resonance alter the apparent character of a passage.

Colette’s libretto for L’Enfant et les sortilèges let Ravel place incompatible musical kinds inside one short opera. A child’s damaged possessions and injured animals acquire voices: the score moves between an eighteenth-century dance, coloratura, a foxtrot and animal cries. These are specific dramatic characters, not an indiscriminate mixture of styles. The work was first performed in Monte Carlo on 21 March 1925 under Victor de Sabata. Its ending turns from the child’s acts of destruction to care for an injured squirrel; the change is expressed through the behaviour of the ensemble as well as the plot.

1926–31Jazz, repetition and two concertos

The Chansons madécasses (1925–26), for voice, flute, cello and piano, use restricted forces for erotic intimacy and an accusation against colonial violence. Their texts are Évariste de Parny’s eighteenth-century French literary constructions, not direct transcriptions of Malagasy song. Ravel’s Violin Sonata, completed in 1927, includes a movement explicitly titled Blues. His North American tour of 1928 brought further encounters with jazz and with George Gershwin, but he had heard jazz in Paris before crossing the Atlantic. The violin’s slides and the piano’s syncopations do not require an American revelation to explain them.

Boléro was written in 1928 for Ida Rubinstein after an intended orchestration of Albéniz’s Iberia proved problematic. At its Paris Opéra premiere on 22 November, Bronislava Nijinska’s choreography set the dance on a tavern table. Ravel reduced the musical process to two repeated melodies, a two-bar snare ostinato and an accumulating orchestra. C major holds almost to the end; the abrupt excursion to E major arrives when ordinary development has become nearly inconceivable. The piece requires a steady pulse because the changing instrumentation, not an accelerando, supplies the growth. Ravel recorded it with the Lamoureux Orchestra in 1930.

The Piano Concerto for the Left Hand (1929–30) was commissioned by Paul Wittgenstein, who had lost his right arm in the war. Ravel made the limitation a problem of register and continuity: the left hand leaps from bass to treble while the pedal sustains what it has left behind. The work begins with divided low strings and contrabassoon, and its single continuous span incorporates a fast, march-like section. Wittgenstein gave the premiere in Vienna in January 1932. The apparent fullness of the piano part is an illusion achieved by timing, not by secretly supplying a second hand.

The Concerto in G major (1929–31), premiered by Marguerite Long with Ravel conducting in Paris on 14 January 1932, takes a different route. Its three movements retain a concerto’s conventional proportions; piccolo, harp and sharply articulated brass surround the piano without permanently doubling it. The E major Adagio assai begins with an extended piano solo whose continuous melody crosses the accompaniment’s regular divisions. When the cor anglais later takes that melody, the piano supplies decoration rather than another competing tune. Long and Ravel toured the concerto together; physical exhaustion increasingly limited his work.

1932–37The loss of musical writing

A diagnosis left openAphasia and apraxia describe losses of language and learned action, not their underlying cause. No post-mortem examination established Ravel’s disease. The clinical evidence is reviewed in Darvesh and colleagues’ study; a confident retrospective diagnosis would exceed it.

Don Quichotte à Dulcinée (1932–33), three songs to Paul Morand’s texts, was his last completed composition. Intended for a film starring Chaliapin, the songs were not used; Jacques Ibert supplied the film’s music. Ravel’s progressive neurological illness impaired writing, speech and coordinated movement while much of his musical judgement remained. A taxi accident in 1932 complicated the history but does not establish the illness’s cause. The neurologist Théophile Alajouanine documented the separation between musical understanding and the ability to express it. Clovis Vincent operated on Ravel’s brain on 17 December 1937; the operation brought no lasting improvement. He died on 28 December, aged sixty-two.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Added notes & parallel chords

Sevenths, ninths and added sixths need not behave as dissonances waiting for resolution. Ravel can let them remain part of a chord’s identity while the bass holds still, as in the water figures of Jeux d’eau. A melody can therefore move inside a stable harmonic field without every note demanding a new functional chord.

In parallel writing, or planing, several voices move together so that a chord’s spacing becomes a recognisable shape. Exact transposition preserves its intervals; diatonic planing changes some intervals to remain within a scale. Ravel uses both alongside ordinary dominant–tonic motion. Colour does not abolish harmonic function; the contrast between colour and function becomes audible.

♭Modes & tonal anchors

Modal scales give Ravel ways of retaining a tonal centre without continually using the major scale’s leading note. A flattened second can suggest Phrygian colour; a pentatonic collection removes the semitones that ordinarily sharpen cadences. In Laideronnette from Ma mère l’Oye, pentatonic figuration contributes to an imagined musical China, not an ethnographic transcription.

The pedal note is another anchor. Le Gibet keeps its repeated B♭ audible beneath and within changing harmonies; in the Prélude à la nuit of the Rapsodie espagnole, a descending four-note figure continues to measure the space while orchestral events accumulate around it.

𝄞Orchestration as engineering

Register, attack and decay are compositional materials. The contrabassoon is not merely a low oboe; in Ma mère l’Oye it supplies a character that a different instrument cannot preserve unchanged. The alto saxophone in Pictures at an Exhibition carries a line whose continuity differs from the struck notes of Mussorgsky’s piano original.

Boléro makes orchestration the principal variable. At one statement, horn, celesta and two piccolos combine at different intervals and octaves, approximating a composite tone with reinforced upper partials. In Daphnis, divided strings, harps, winds and wordless voices can remain separately legible inside a large crescendo. Precision concerns balance, not simply the number of instruments.

𝅘𝅥Dance & controlled displacement

Pavane, minuet, forlane, rigaudon, waltz, habanera, foxtrot: a dance supplies an expected pattern of weight before Ravel displaces it. Valses nobles et sentimentales names Schubert as a precedent, but its accented dissonances prevent the harmony from behaving like a nineteenth-century salon accompaniment.

The opening of the Concerto in G and the Blues of the Violin Sonata use syncopation without dissolving the beat. In La Valse, competing accents eventually threaten the three-beat measure itself. Boléro adopts the opposite discipline: the snare’s repeated two-bar pattern makes an unchanged metre resist everything the growing orchestra does above it.

𝄢Spain is not the Basque Country

The Habanera of 1895, later incorporated into the Rapsodie espagnole, uses a dance with Cuban origins that circulated through Spain and France. Alborada del gracioso suggests guitar attacks, strumming and the inflections of a theatrical singer. These pieces transform idioms rather than presenting a collection of folk tunes.

The Piano Trio’s 3+2+3 opening gives a different account of Ravel’s Basque inheritance. A conventional zortziko is commonly notated in 5/8; the Trio’s 8/8 should not be labelled a literal specimen. Don Quichotte à Dulcinée brings several dance types into one cycle: the guajira’s changing divisions, a zortziko in the prayer, and a drinking song based on the jota.

𝄋Artifice & classical construction

The ‘artificial by nature’ aesthetic is not a claim that the music lacks feeling. A clockwork effect, an old dance or a fairy-tale character gives feeling a manufactured object through which to speak. Ravel’s friend Calvocoressi remembered his insistence that being naturally artificial was possible; Peter Jost discusses the testimony and its limits in his account of Ravel’s aesthetic.

Sonata form, recurring themes and older dance forms organise much of the work. The Quartet recalls material across movements; the Trio uses a passacaglia; Le Tombeau de Couperin contains a three-part fugue. This neoclassical clarity predates the post-war fashion: the Menuet antique was written in 1895. Ornament and added-note harmony modify inherited structures rather than concealing their outlines.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1899; orch. 1910
    Pavane pour une infante défunte (Pavane for a Dead Princess)Piano; later orchestra
    A G major processional dance with a recurring refrain. The title imagines a past Spanish court, not the funeral of a named princess; the orchestral opening gives the melody to a solo horn.
  2. 1901
    Jeux d’eauPiano
    Dedicated to Fauré. Added-note arpeggios, pedal resonance and widely separated registers make water an exercise in keyboard acoustics; two thematic ideas give the short work its formal organisation.
  3. 1902–03
    String Quartet in F majorTwo violins, viola and cello
    Four movements connected by recurring thematic material. Listen for the second movement’s pizzicato attacks and its friction between groups of two and three; the slow movement stretches the same instrumental family across widely separated registers.
  4. 1903
    ShéhérazadeVoice and orchestra; poems by Tristan Klingsor
    Three songs: Asie, La flûte enchantée and L’indifférent. The first expands a fantasy of travel; the others narrow the frame to overheard music and an unresolved encounter. Not the earlier orchestral overture of the same name.
  5. 1904–05
    Miroirs (Mirrors)Piano
    Five pieces dedicated to members of Les Apaches: moths, birds, a boat, a Spanish morning song and bells. Ravel later orchestrated Une barque sur l’océan and Alborada del gracioso, but not the complete set.
  6. 1907–08
    Rapsodie espagnoleOrchestra
    Four movements: Prélude à la nuit, Malagueña, Habanera and Feria. The Habanera reuses the 1895 two-piano piece; the opening descending ostinato returns within a score whose colours depend on sharply controlled instrumental registers.
  7. 1908
    Gaspard de la nuitPiano; after Aloysius Bertrand
    Ondine, Le Gibet, Scarbo: a water spirit, a hanged man and a nocturnal dwarf. The central movement’s persistent B♭ bell is a test of evenness and balance; the outer pieces demand rapid repeated notes without losing the melodic layer.
  8. 1908–10; ballet 1911–12
    Ma mère l’Oye (Mother Goose)Piano duet; orchestral suite and expanded ballet
    Five original pieces, from Sleeping Beauty’s pavane to Le Jardin féerique. The ballet adds a prelude, a spinning-wheel scene and connecting music; it should not be confused with the shorter concert suite.
  9. 1909–12
    Daphnis et ChloéBallet: large orchestra and wordless mixed chorus
    A choreographic symphony in three parts for Diaghilev. Suite No. 2 joins Lever du jour, Pantomime and Danse générale; the complete ballet supplies the abduction, the nocturnal intervention of Pan and the thematic connections omitted by the suite.
  10. 1914
    Piano Trio in A minorPiano, violin and cello
    A 3+2+3 opening, a Pantoum scherzo, an eight-bar passacaglia theme and a finale with changing metres. Ravel separates the string registers and places piano figuration between them to preserve balance.
  11. 1914–17; orch. 1919
    Le Tombeau de CouperinPiano; four-movement orchestral version
    The six piano movements memorialise seven men killed in the war. The orchestral sequence is Prélude, Forlane, Menuet, Rigaudon; the Fugue and Toccata remain piano pieces. The oboe’s rapid opening shows how articulation, not just pitch, governs the transfer.
  12. 1919–20
    La ValseOrchestra; also versions for piano and for two pianos
    A choreographic poem in which Viennese waltz fragments emerge, accumulate and finally lose their equilibrium. Ravel resisted the claim that it represented the fall of the Habsburg Empire; the score’s ballroom is dated around 1855.
  13. 1922
    Pictures at an Exhibition — orchestration of MussorgskyOrchestra, including alto saxophone, celesta, two harps and percussion
    Commissioned by Serge Koussevitzky. Compare the saxophone of The Old Castle, the high tuba of Bydło and the brass-and-bell architecture of the finale with Mussorgsky’s 1874 piano score.
  14. 1917–25
    L’Enfant et les sortilèges (The Child and the Spells)One-act opera: soloists, chorus and orchestra; libretto by Colette
    Furniture, crockery, fire, animals and trees answer a destructive child. A foxtrot, coloratura and older dance styles distinguish the characters. Premiered in Monte Carlo in 1925 under Victor de Sabata.
  15. 1928
    BoléroBallet for large orchestra, including saxophones and two snare drums
    Two melodies, a repeated two-bar rhythm and a sustained crescendo. The near-end shift from C major to E major is a formal event precisely because the preceding repetitions have withheld ordinary modulation and development.
  16. 1929–30
    Piano Concerto for the Left Hand in D majorLeft-hand piano and orchestra
    Written for Paul Wittgenstein. One continuous movement links slow, low-register music to a fast central section; pedalling and rapid leaps allow one hand to sustain bass, accompaniment and melody.
  17. 1929–31
    Piano Concerto in G majorPiano and orchestra
    The outer movements combine clear forms with jazz inflections and abrupt instrumental exchanges. The E major slow movement gives an extended melody first to the piano, later to the cor anglais. Dedicated to Marguerite Long.
  18. 1932–33
    Don Quichotte à DulcinéeBaritone and piano or orchestra; poems by Paul Morand
    Three songs: a lover’s pledge, a prayer and a drinking song. Originally intended for Pabst’s film with Chaliapin, but not used in it. Ravel’s last completed composition; the film received music by Jacques Ibert.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Bakst designed the scenery and costumes for the Ballets Russes production of Ravel’s ballet, choreographed by Fokine and first performed in Paris in June 1912. The drawing’s antiquity is a stage construction: monumental landscape surrounds the space in which human figures move. Ravel’s wordless chorus and slowly accumulated orchestral layers create a corresponding scale without imitating historical Greek instruments. Harvard identifies this sheet as Act I; it should not be confused with differently catalogued reproductions of the design.

Aloysius Bertrand

Ondine — opening and closing paragraphs

1842; text from the 1895 edition
French— « Écoute ! — Écoute ! — C’est moi, c’est Ondine qui frôle de ces gouttes d’eau les losanges sonores de ta fenêtre illuminée par les mornes rayons de la lune ; et voici, en robe de moire, la dame châtelaine qui contemple à son balcon la belle nuit étoilée et le beau lac endormi. […] Et comme je lui répondais que j’aimais une mortelle, boudeuse et dépitée, elle pleura quelques larmes, poussa un éclat de rire, et s’évanouit en giboulées qui ruisselèrent blanches le long de mes vitraux bleus.
Our translation“Listen! — Listen! — It is I, Ondine, brushing these drops of water against the resonant diamond panes of your window, lit by the dull rays of the moon; and here, in a watered-silk dress, the lady of the castle watches from her balcony the beautiful starry night and the beautiful sleeping lake. […] And when I answered that I loved a mortal woman, sullen and vexed, she shed a few tears, burst out laughing, and vanished in showers that streamed white down my blue stained-glass windows.

The intervening paragraphs, in which Ondine describes her underwater palace and proposes marriage, are omitted. Bertrand’s Gaspard de la nuit was published posthumously in 1842; Ravel’s 1908 piano triptych prints its three source poems. The first movement must distinguish the water’s continuous motion from the spirit’s song, then accommodate her laughter and disappearance. The French follows the 1895 Mercure de France edition; the translation here is our own.

The lantern isolates seven children beneath the ogre’s house; the architectural scale makes their physical vulnerability measurable. Ravel’s Petit Poucet, the second piece of Ma mère l’Oye, uses the same Perrault tale but an earlier incident: the lost breadcrumb trail. Wandering lines and changing bar lengths replace a secure processional pulse; birds enter the orchestral version as instrumental calls. The pairing is through a shared text, not a claim that this particular Doré impression stood on Ravel’s piano.

Hartmann’s unbuilt gate supplied the subject of the final movement of Mussorgsky’s Pictures at an Exhibition (1874). Ravel’s 1922 orchestration adds a second translation between media: architecture became piano music, then orchestral music. The large arch and helmet-shaped tower correspond to the score’s monumental scale, but the sounding bells belong to the musical realisation. Compare the brass chorales and percussion with the piano original rather than treating Ravel’s colour as part of Hartmann’s drawing.

VIILineage

Passed toRalph Vaughan WilliamsManuel RosenthalGershwin — an exchange, not a pupilOlivier Messiaenboulez

Fauré and Gedalge supplied training; Chabrier, Satie and the Russian composers supplied distinct alternatives to a German symphonic model. Liszt is an important predecessor for the piano’s water figuration, while Couperin stands for a broader French inheritance of ornament and dance rather than a single borrowed style.

Vaughan Williams studied with Ravel in 1907–08 and credited him with changing his approach to texture. Rosenthal was both pupil and interpreter. Gershwin met Ravel and sought instruction, but did not become his composition student. Later French composers inherited particular technical resources without adopting his whole aesthetic: Messiaen’s chord colours and Boulez’s attention to instrumental definition belong to a history in which Ravel remained audible. Boulez’s recordings provide a documented link stronger than a claim that the two composers shared a harmonic language.

  • Roger Nichols, Ravel (Yale University Press, 2011) — a documentary biography attentive to letters, performers and the gap between public reserve and private testimony.
  • Arbie Orenstein, ed., A Ravel Reader: Correspondence, Articles, Interviews (Columbia University Press, 1990) — the essential English-language collection of primary documents, including the autobiographical sketch dictated to Roland-Manuel in 1928.
  • Deborah Mawer, ed., The Cambridge Companion to Ravel (Cambridge University Press, 2000) — essays on the musical language, cultural setting and performance traditions; useful alongside scores rather than as a substitute for them.
  • Peter Kaminsky, ed., Unmasking Ravel: New Perspectives on the Music (University of Rochester Press, 2011) — analytical essays on form, harmony, literary models and the different kinds of artifice in the music.
  • The Musée Maurice Ravel at Le Belvédère — the surviving house, piano and collections; a material source for the miniature mechanisms and constructed interiors discussed in the biographies.
