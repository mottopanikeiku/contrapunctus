Key ideas

  • Added notes & parallel chords. Ravel can let sevenths, ninths and added sixths remain part of a chord’s identity while the bass holds still, as in the water figures of Jeux d’eau.
  • Modes & tonal anchors. Modal scales give Ravel ways of retaining a tonal centre without continually using the major scale’s leading note.
  • Orchestration as engineering. Register, attack and decay are compositional materials.

49.1Life

Ravel was born in Ciboure, across the harbour from Saint-Jean-de-Luz, to Marie Delouart, a Basque woman who had lived in Madrid, and Pierre-Joseph Ravel, a Swiss-born engineer. The family moved to Paris when Maurice was three months old. His father encouraged music as well as an interest in machinery; his mother’s songs gave him an early acquaintance with Spanish idioms. Basque ancestry and Spanish musical theatre remained distinct resources: the uneven pulse of the Piano Trio differs from the habanera of the Rapsodie espagnole. He returned to the Basque coast for holidays and composition, but received his musical education in Paris.

1889–1905A student the examinations could not accommodate

He entered the Conservatoire in 1889, initially as a pianist, and studied with Charles-Wilfrid de Bériot. Failure to win the required prizes cost him his place in 1895. Readmitted in 1897 to Fauré’s composition class, he also learnt counterpoint from André Gedalge. Fauré encouraged the individual ear without relaxing the demands of construction; Gedalge supplied the discipline behind the fugues and independent inner parts. Ravel did not become a virtuoso pianist. Ricardo Viñes, his friend since adolescence, introduced much of the difficult piano music, including Jeux d’eau, Miroirs and Gaspard de la nuit.

Outside the Conservatoire, Ravel belonged to Les Apaches, an informal circle whose name borrowed Parisian slang for street gangs. The poet Tristan Klingsor, the critic Michel-Dimitri Calvocoressi and the composer Maurice Delage were among its members. They read Poe and contemporary poetry, played Russian music and defended Debussy’s Pelléas et Mélisande in 1902. Ravel’s early Habanera for two pianos dated from 1895; Jeux d’eau preceded Debussy’s Estampes. Arguments about who had borrowed which sonority eventually damaged the composers’ relations.

Dubois’s retirementDubois had requested retirement before the 1905 scandal. Marc Rigaudière sets out the chronology in his account of the competition.

Between 1900 and 1905 Ravel made five attempts at the Prix de Rome, winning a lesser prize in 1901 but never the first. In 1905 he was eliminated before the final cantata round, although the String Quartet and Jeux d’eau were already in circulation. All six successful preliminary candidates were pupils of the juror Charles Lenepveu. The resulting press controversy concerned institutional partiality as much as musical modernism. Fauré succeeded Théodore Dubois as director that year and reformed the Conservatoire. Ravel continued outside it, equipped with its compositional craft.

The piano laboratoryWater, mirrors and nocturnal apparitions

Jeux d’eau (1901), dedicated to Fauré, made pianistic figuration carry the harmony. Its arpeggios spread added-note chords across registers; sustained bass notes allow the upper notes to change without a conventional chord progression at every attack. Liszt’s water pieces supplied a precedent; Ravel organised the work around two themes. The title refers to fountains and playing water. He never orchestrated it: the damper pedal, the decay of each struck note and the interference between registers belong to its construction.

The String Quartet in F major (1902–03) combined recurring themes with four recognisable movement types. The second movement’s pizzicato writing turns four bowed instruments into a plucked ensemble, with triple and compound-duple groupings competing inside the pulse. In the orchestral songs Shéhérazade (1903), Klingsor’s imagined Asia became a matter of French verbal rhythm, solo woodwinds and sustained orchestral colour. The long first song accumulates a traveller’s catalogue; the two shorter songs reduce the scale to a flute heard through a wall and an encounter with an unresponsive stranger.

Three layers, two handsRavel’s piano writing frequently separates bass, melody and figuration into different registers. The pianist must give each layer a different weight. The apparently orchestral result depends on balance and pedalling.

Each of the five pieces of Miroirs (1904–05) was dedicated to an Apache. Oiseaux tristes, for Viñes, places isolated calls against a nearly motionless background; Une barque sur l’océan expands arpeggios into overlapping waves; Alborada del gracioso turns repeated notes and glissandi into a guitar-like mechanism. In Gaspard de la nuit (1908), Ravel printed Aloysius Bertrand’s three prose poems with the music. Ondine carries a singing line inside rapid repeated sonorities. Le Gibet keeps returning to the same B♭ while harmonies change around it. Scarbo makes its dwarf appear through repetitions, sudden silences and violent changes of register.

1908–12Children’s books and the Ballets Russes

Ma mère l’Oye began as five piano duets for the children of Ravel’s friends Cipa and Ida Godebski. Perrault supplied Sleeping Beauty and Little Thumb; other stories came from Madame d’Aulnoy and Madame Leprince de Beaumont. Ravel restricted the original keyboard writing without simplifying the harmonic ear: pentatonic figures, modal turns and widely spaced registers make small means sufficient. He orchestrated the suite in 1911, then expanded it into a ballet premiered in 1912. The transformation of the Beast is an instrumental event: the low contrabassoon’s character gives way to a solo violin.

Two larger stage works required different kinds of precision. L’heure espagnole, completed in 1907 and first staged in 1911, is a bedroom farce in a Toledo clockmaker’s shop; men hide inside clocks while the orchestral mechanisms keep running. For Diaghilev’s Daphnis et Chloé, composed in 1909–12, Ravel worked with the choreographer Michel Fokine and designer Léon Bakst. Nijinsky and Karsavina danced the lovers at the Paris premiere on 8 June 1912, conducted by Pierre Monteux. Ravel called the score a choreographic symphony: recurring themes connect the action, and a wordless chorus enlarges the orchestra.

The dawn music in Daphnis begins with moving figures before the broad melody becomes audible: changes of register, density and instrumental entry produce the sunrise. The final dance is principally in 5/4, making collective celebration depend on an asymmetrical bar. Ravel’s later autobiographical sketch described a Greece of his imagination. Bakst’s painted rocks, trees and architecture provided another constructed version of the same landscape; the music and designs belonged to the same theatrical commission.

1914–17A trio, a lorry and a memorial suite

At Saint-Jean-de-Luz in 1914, Ravel was working on the Piano Trio when war broke out. He hurried to finish it before entering military service. Its opening 8/8 metre divides into 3+2+3, a personal adaptation of Basque rhythmic practice. A scherzo called Pantoum alludes to a Malay verse form; the slow Passacaille builds over an eight-bar theme first heard in the piano’s bass. The finale uses irregular metres and harmonics to obtain an orchestral spread from three players.

Rejected for aviation service, Ravel became an army driver and carried supplies near Verdun in 1916. Illness and an operation interrupted his service; his mother’s death in January 1917 added a separate bereavement. In 1916 he refused to join a league campaigning against contemporary German and Austrian music, arguing that French composers would impoverish themselves by refusing to hear foreign colleagues. This was the composer who had recently studied Schoenberg’s Pierrot lunaire and worked with Stravinsky on Mussorgsky’s Khovanshchina.

Six movements, seven menThe Rigaudon commemorates the brothers Pierre and Pascal Gaudin. The other five piano movements each name one man; the Toccata remembers Joseph de Marliave, husband of the pianist Marguerite Long.

Le Tombeau de Couperin, begun before the war and completed in 1917, is a six-movement piano suite in which every movement commemorates friends killed in the fighting. Its title also invokes the French tradition of the tombeau, a musical memorial. Prelude, fugue, dances and toccata commemorate through ornament, counterpoint and precisely weighted dissonance. Long gave the first performance in 1919. Ravel’s orchestral version of that year selects four movements and changes their order, transferring much of the keyboard’s light articulation to exposed woodwind lines.

After the warWaltzes, miniatures and a house of objects

La Valse (1920) returns to a project conceived before the war: a homage to the Viennese waltz. Fragments emerge from low orchestral turbulence, assemble into ballroom phrases, then become overloaded by acceleration and displaced accents. Diaghilev declined to stage it. In a letter to Maurice Emmanuel in 1922, Ravel rejected interpretations of its ending based on the collapse of the Habsburg monarchy. The score specifies a Viennese imperial court around 1855.

In 1921 Ravel settled at Le Belvédère in Montfort-l’Amaury. Its small rooms held his piano, ornaments, mechanical toys and objects whose functions were deliberately deceptive, including cups with holes. The house survives with these collections. Calvocoressi recalled Ravel’s insistence that artifice could be natural to him. At the same time, the Sonata for Violin and Cello (1920–22) reduced the available resources to two lines, with biting intervals and little room for harmonic padding.

Serge Koussevitzky commissioned the 1922 orchestration of Mussorgsky’s Pictures at an Exhibition. A solo trumpet establishes the opening Promenade; alto saxophone gives The Old Castle a sustained, unfamiliar voice; the high tuba solo in Bydło tests the boundary between weight and lyricism. His colours are so familiar that they can conceal the distinction between Mussorgsky’s composition and Ravel’s instrumentation. The piano original remains the necessary comparison, particularly where attack, register and resonance alter the apparent character of a passage.

Colette’s libretto for L’Enfant et les sortilèges let Ravel place incompatible musical kinds inside one short opera. A child’s damaged possessions and injured animals acquire voices: the score moves between an eighteenth-century dance, coloratura, a foxtrot and animal cries. Each style gives a character its voice. The work was first performed in Monte Carlo on 21 March 1925 under Victor de Sabata. Its ending turns from the child’s acts of destruction to care for an injured squirrel; the change is expressed through the behaviour of the ensemble as well as the plot.

1926–31Jazz, repetition and two concertos

The Chansons madécasses (1925–26), for voice, flute, cello and piano, use restricted forces for erotic intimacy and an accusation against colonial violence. Their texts are Évariste de Parny’s eighteenth-century French literary constructions. Ravel’s Violin Sonata, completed in 1927, includes a movement explicitly titled Blues. His North American tour of 1928 brought further encounters with jazz and with George Gershwin, but he had heard jazz in Paris before crossing the Atlantic.

Boléro was written in 1928 for Ida Rubinstein after an intended orchestration of Albéniz’s Iberia proved problematic. At its Paris Opéra premiere on 22 November, Bronislava Nijinska’s choreography set the dance on a tavern table. Ravel reduced the musical process to two repeated melodies, a two-bar snare ostinato and an accumulating orchestra. C major holds almost to the end; the abrupt excursion to E major arrives when ordinary development has become nearly inconceivable. The piece requires a steady pulse because the changing instrumentation, not an accelerando, supplies the growth. Ravel recorded it with the Lamoureux Orchestra in 1930.

The Piano Concerto for the Left Hand (1929–30) was commissioned by Paul Wittgenstein, who had lost his right arm in the war. Ravel made the limitation a problem of register and continuity: the left hand leaps from bass to treble while the pedal sustains what it has left behind. The work begins with divided low strings and contrabassoon, and its single continuous span incorporates a fast, march-like section. Wittgenstein gave the premiere in Vienna in January 1932. The apparent fullness of the piano part is an illusion achieved by timing.

The Concerto in G major (1929–31), premiered by Marguerite Long with Ravel conducting in Paris on 14 January 1932, takes a different route. Its three movements retain a concerto’s conventional proportions; piccolo, harp and sharply articulated brass surround the piano without permanently doubling it. The E major Adagio assai begins with an extended piano solo whose continuous melody crosses the accompaniment’s regular divisions. When the cor anglais later takes that melody, the piano supplies decoration. Long and Ravel toured the concerto together; physical exhaustion increasingly limited his work.

1932–37The loss of musical writing

A diagnosis left openAphasia and apraxia describe losses of language and learned action. No post-mortem examination established Ravel’s underlying disease. The clinical evidence is reviewed in Darvesh and colleagues’ study.

Don Quichotte à Dulcinée (1932–33), three songs to Paul Morand’s texts, was his last completed composition. Intended for a film starring Chaliapin, the songs were not used; Jacques Ibert supplied the film’s music. Ravel’s progressive neurological illness impaired writing, speech and coordinated movement while much of his musical judgement remained. A taxi accident in 1932 complicated the history but does not establish the illness’s cause. The neurologist Théophile Alajouanine documented the separation between musical understanding and the ability to express it. Clovis Vincent operated on Ravel’s brain on 17 December 1937; the operation brought no lasting improvement. He died on 28 December, aged sixty-two.

49.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Added notes & parallel chords

Ravel can let sevenths, ninths and added sixths remain part of a chord’s identity while the bass holds still, as in the water figures of Jeux d’eau. A melody can therefore move inside a stable harmonic field without every note demanding a new functional chord.

In parallel writing, or planing, several voices move together so that a chord’s spacing becomes a recognisable shape. Exact transposition preserves its intervals; diatonic planing changes some intervals to remain within a scale. Ravel uses both alongside ordinary dominant–tonic motion, making the contrast between colour and harmonic function audible.

♭Modes & tonal anchors

Modal scales give Ravel ways of retaining a tonal centre without continually using the major scale’s leading note. A flattened second can suggest Phrygian colour; a pentatonic collection removes the semitones that ordinarily sharpen cadences. In Laideronnette from Ma mère l’Oye, pentatonic figuration contributes to an imagined musical China.

The pedal note is another anchor. Le Gibet keeps its repeated B♭ audible beneath and within changing harmonies; in the Prélude à la nuit of the Rapsodie espagnole, a descending four-note figure continues to measure the space while orchestral events accumulate around it.

𝄞Orchestration as engineering

Register, attack and decay are compositional materials. The contrabassoon’s distinctive voice supplies a character in Ma mère l’Oye. The alto saxophone in Pictures at an Exhibition carries a line whose continuity differs from the struck notes of Mussorgsky’s piano original.

Boléro makes orchestration the principal variable. At one statement, horn, celesta and two piccolos combine at different intervals and octaves, approximating a composite tone with reinforced upper partials. In Daphnis, divided strings, harps, winds and wordless voices can remain separately legible inside a large crescendo. Precision rests on balance.

𝅘𝅥Dance & controlled displacement

Pavane, minuet, forlane, rigaudon, waltz, habanera, foxtrot: a dance supplies an expected pattern of weight before Ravel displaces it. Valses nobles et sentimentales names Schubert as a precedent, but its accented dissonances prevent the harmony from behaving like a nineteenth-century salon accompaniment.

The opening of the Concerto in G and the Blues of the Violin Sonata use syncopation without dissolving the beat. In La Valse, competing accents eventually threaten the three-beat measure itself. Boléro adopts the opposite discipline: the snare’s repeated two-bar pattern makes an unchanged metre resist everything the growing orchestra does above it.

𝄢Spanish idioms & Basque rhythm

The Habanera of 1895, later incorporated into the Rapsodie espagnole, uses a dance with Cuban origins that circulated through Spain and France. Alborada del gracioso suggests guitar attacks, strumming and the inflections of a theatrical singer. These pieces transform their source idioms.

The Piano Trio’s 3+2+3 opening gives a different account of Ravel’s Basque inheritance. The Trio uses 8/8; a conventional zortziko is commonly notated in 5/8. Don Quichotte à Dulcinée brings several dance types into one cycle: the guajira’s changing divisions, a zortziko in the prayer, and a drinking song based on the jota.

𝄋Artifice & classical construction

In the ‘artificial by nature’ aesthetic, a clockwork effect, an old dance or a fairy-tale character gives feeling a manufactured object through which to speak. Ravel’s friend Calvocoressi remembered his insistence that being naturally artificial was possible; Peter Jost discusses the testimony and its limits in his account of Ravel’s aesthetic.

Sonata form, recurring themes and older dance forms organise much of the work. The Quartet recalls material across movements; the Trio uses a passacaglia; Le Tombeau de Couperin contains a three-part fugue. This neoclassical clarity predates the post-war fashion: the Menuet antique was written in 1895. Ornament and added-note harmony modify inherited structures while preserving their outlines.

    Four movements connected by recurring thematic material. Listen for the second movement’s pizzicato attacks and its friction between groups of two and three; the slow movement stretches the same instrumental family across widely separated registers.
  4. 1903
    ShéhérazadeVoice and orchestra; poems by Tristan Klingsor
    Three songs: Asie, La flûte enchantée and L’indifférent. The first expands a fantasy of travel; the others narrow the frame to overheard music and an unresolved encounter. The earlier orchestral overture shares the title.
  5. 1904–05
    Miroirs (Mirrors)Piano
    Five pieces dedicated to members of Les Apaches: moths, birds, a boat, a Spanish morning song and bells. Ravel later orchestrated two of them: Une barque sur l’océan and Alborada del gracioso.
  6. 1907–08
    Rapsodie espagnoleOrchestra
    Four movements: Prélude à la nuit, Malagueña, Habanera and Feria. The Habanera reuses the 1895 two-piano piece; the opening descending ostinato returns within a score whose colours depend on sharply controlled instrumental registers.
  7. 1908
    Gaspard de la nuitPiano; after Aloysius Bertrand
    Ondine, Le Gibet, Scarbo: a water spirit, a hanged man and a nocturnal dwarf. The central movement’s persistent B♭ bell is a test of evenness and balance; the outer pieces demand rapid repeated notes without losing the melodic layer.
  8. 1908–10; ballet 1911–12
    Ma mère l’Oye (Mother Goose)Piano duet; orchestral suite and expanded ballet
    Five original pieces, from Sleeping Beauty’s pavane to Le Jardin féerique. The ballet expands the shorter concert suite with a prelude, a spinning-wheel scene and connecting music.
  9. 1909–12
    Daphnis et ChloéBallet: large orchestra and wordless mixed chorus
    A choreographic symphony in three parts for Diaghilev. Suite No. 2 joins Lever du jour, Pantomime and Danse générale; the complete ballet supplies the abduction, the nocturnal intervention of Pan and the thematic connections omitted by the suite.
  10. 1914
    Piano Trio in A minorPiano, violin and cello
    A 3+2+3 opening, a Pantoum scherzo, an eight-bar passacaglia theme and a finale with changing metres. Ravel separates the string registers and places piano figuration between them to preserve balance.
  11. 1914–17; orch. 1919
    Le Tombeau de CouperinPiano; four-movement orchestral version
    The six piano movements memorialise seven men killed in the war. The orchestral sequence is Prélude, Forlane, Menuet, Rigaudon; the Fugue and Toccata remain piano pieces. The oboe’s rapid opening shows how articulation governs the transfer alongside pitch.
  12. 1919–20
    La ValseOrchestra; also versions for piano and for two pianos
    A choreographic poem in which Viennese waltz fragments emerge, accumulate and finally lose their equilibrium. Ravel resisted the claim that it represented the fall of the Habsburg Empire; the score’s ballroom is dated around 1855.
  13. 1922
    Pictures at an Exhibition — orchestration of MussorgskyOrchestra, including alto saxophone, celesta, two harps and percussion
    Commissioned by Serge Koussevitzky. Compare the saxophone of The Old Castle, the high tuba of Bydło and the brass-and-bell architecture of the finale with Mussorgsky’s 1874 piano score.
  14. 1917–25
    L’Enfant et les sortilèges (The Child and the Spells)One-act opera: soloists, chorus and orchestra; libretto by Colette
    Furniture, crockery, fire, animals and trees answer a destructive child. A foxtrot, coloratura and older dance styles distinguish the characters. Premiered in Monte Carlo in 1925 under Victor de Sabata.
  15. 1928
    BoléroBallet for large orchestra, including saxophones and two snare drums
    Two melodies, a repeated two-bar rhythm and a sustained crescendo. The near-end shift from C major to E major is a formal event precisely because the preceding repetitions have withheld ordinary modulation and development.
  16. 1929–30
    Piano Concerto for the Left Hand in D majorLeft-hand piano and orchestra
    Written for Paul Wittgenstein. One continuous movement links slow, low-register music to a fast central section; pedalling and rapid leaps allow one hand to sustain bass, accompaniment and melody.
  17. 1929–31
    Piano Concerto in G majorPiano and orchestra
    The outer movements combine clear forms with jazz inflections and abrupt instrumental exchanges. The E major slow movement gives an extended melody first to the piano, later to the cor anglais. Dedicated to Marguerite Long.
  18. 1932–33
    Don Quichotte à DulcinéeBaritone and piano or orchestra; poems by Paul Morand
    Three songs: a lover’s pledge, a prayer and a drinking song. Originally intended for Pabst’s film with Chaliapin, but not used in it. Ravel’s last completed composition; the film received music by Jacques Ibert.

Ondine — opening and closing paragraphs

1842; text from the 1895 edition
French— « Écoute ! — Écoute ! — C’est moi, c’est Ondine qui frôle de ces gouttes d’eau les losanges sonores de ta fenêtre illuminée par les mornes rayons de la lune ; et voici, en robe de moire, la dame châtelaine qui contemple à son balcon la belle nuit étoilée et le beau lac endormi. […] Et comme je lui répondais que j’aimais une mortelle, boudeuse et dépitée, elle pleura quelques larmes, poussa un éclat de rire, et s’évanouit en giboulées qui ruisselèrent blanches le long de mes vitraux bleus.
Our translation“Listen! — Listen! — It is I, Ondine, brushing these drops of water against the resonant diamond panes of your window, lit by the dull rays of the moon; and here, in a watered-silk dress, the lady of the castle watches from her balcony the beautiful starry night and the beautiful sleeping lake. […] And when I answered that I loved a mortal woman, sullen and vexed, she shed a few tears, burst out laughing, and vanished in showers that streamed white down my blue stained-glass windows.

The intervening paragraphs, in which Ondine describes her underwater palace and proposes marriage, are omitted. Bertrand’s Gaspard de la nuit was published posthumously in 1842; Ravel’s 1908 piano triptych prints its three source poems. The first movement must distinguish the water’s continuous motion from the spirit’s song, then accommodate her laughter and disappearance. The French follows the 1895 Mercure de France edition; the translation here is our own.

The lantern isolates seven children beneath the ogre’s house; the architectural scale makes their physical vulnerability measurable. Ravel’s Petit Poucet, the second piece of Ma mère l’Oye, uses the same Perrault tale but an earlier incident: the lost breadcrumb trail. Wandering lines and changing bar lengths replace a secure processional pulse; birds enter the orchestral version as instrumental calls. The link with Doré is the shared text.

Ravel planned a homage to Johann Strauss as early as 1906, provisionally titled Wien; he finished it after the war, in 1919–20, as La Valse. Gause’s view of a Habsburg court ball — chandeliers, uniforms, a floor packed with turning couples — is the scene the score’s preface names. What Ravel did to it is the point: the waltz is assembled from fragments emerging out of murk in the double basses, built to a brilliant climax, and then driven past it until the three-beat metre breaks apart.

Maurice Ravel

Preface to the score of La Valse

1920
FrenchDes nuées tourbillonnantes laissent entrevoir, par éclaircies, des couples de valseurs. Elles se dissipent peu à peu : on distingue une immense salle peuplée d’une foule tournoyante. La scène s’éclaire progressivement. La lumière des lustres éclate au ff. Une Cour impériale, vers 1855.
Our translationWhirling clouds give glimpses, through gaps, of waltzing couples. Little by little they scatter: one makes out an immense hall filled with a turning crowd. The stage grows gradually brighter. The light of the chandeliers bursts out at the ff. An imperial court, about 1855.

Ravel printed this scenario at the head of the score of his “poème chorégraphique”. It is also a set of orchestration instructions in disguise: the clouds are the muted, divided double basses that open the piece; the gaps are the first waltz fragments surfacing in the bassoons; “éclate au ff” marks the exact dynamic at which the full orchestra arrives. Ravel insisted the setting was 1855, not 1914 — but the score’s violent ending has been heard as an epitaph for that court ever since.

Fauré and Gedalge supplied training; Chabrier, Satie and the Russian composers supplied distinct alternatives to a German symphonic model. Liszt is an important predecessor for the piano’s water figuration, while Couperin stands for a broader French inheritance of ornament and dance.

Vaughan Williams studied with Ravel in 1907–08 and credited him with changing his approach to texture. Rosenthal was both pupil and interpreter. Gershwin met Ravel and sought instruction, but did not become his composition student. Later French composers inherited particular technical resources: Messiaen’s chord colours and Boulez’s attention to instrumental definition belong to a history in which Ravel remained audible. Boulez’s recordings document his engagement with Ravel’s music.

Questions to take away

  1. How differently can the same accumulating rhythm occupy time?

    Ravel directs the Orchestre des Concerts Lamoureux in the 1930 performance identified by this transfer. BnF documents a published Polydor issue of the same Ravel/Lamoureux recording by 1935. It is compared with Fricsay’s1950s reading, not with the misattributed later Lamoureux files.

    Boléro

  2. What changes when the melodies and rhythm barely change?

    Two melodies and a two-bar drum rhythm survive an almost continuous crescendo. Ravel makes the changing instruments do the work that changing themes and keys usually do.
