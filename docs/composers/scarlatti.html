ILife

Domenico Scarlatti was born in Naples, the sixth child of Alessandro Scarlatti, an established composer of opera and sacred music. He entered professional musical life as a teenager, working as an organist and composing for the stage. The distinction between father and son matters: Alessandro’s large operatic career did not simply continue under another first name. Domenico eventually concentrated on keyboard music in courts far from Naples. The surviving sonatas make that later career look inevitable, but his first decades included church appointments, singers, theatrical commissions and the practical education of a musician expected to supply whatever his employer required.

In Rome he worked for the exiled Polish queen Maria Casimira and at St Peter’s, writing both opera and sacred music. The ten-part Stabat Mater, usually associated with these Roman years, combines four soprano lines with pairs of altos, tenors and basses. Its voices are not simply divided into two answering choirs: they enter in changing combinations, and the density of the counterpoint can contract around a particular text. The keyboard sonatas should therefore not be used to infer that Scarlatti knew only two-part textures or had no interest in sustained vocal writing.

LisbonA royal pupil

He entered Portuguese royal service in 1719. In Lisbon his responsibilities included teaching the Infanta Maria Bárbara, daughter of João V, whose keyboard playing became central to his later employment. This was a long association between a professional composer and a highly trained royal pupil, not a few childhood lessons. Portuguese court culture also had close connections with Italian musicians and instruments. Harpsichords and early pianos belonged to the wider Iberian court environment; the existence of those instruments does not identify a single intended instrument for every surviving sonata.

When Maria Bárbara married the Spanish heir, the future Ferdinand VI, in 1729, Scarlatti followed her to Spain. The court spent several years in Seville before returning to the Madrid area in 1733. Royal service involved movement between residences rather than uninterrupted life in one room of a palace. Scarlatti continued to teach and compose within that household. The sonatas’ guitar-like chords, repeated notes, dance rhythms and sudden modal turns belong to this Iberian setting, but few can be tied to a named street song or a particular dance witnessed on a documented occasion.

The keyboardThe hands become part of the form

In many sonatas, a physical task produces the musical material. Repeated notes require the player to renew the same pitch without losing pulse; hand crossings force a bass note and a high answer to be executed by one moving arm. Wide leaps can make registers sound like separate instruments. A phrase may begin as a conventional imitation, then collide with a repeated chord or a conspicuously foreign harmony. These gestures are not confined to a decorative cadenza. They can occupy the passage in which the piece establishes its new key, making technical risk and formal direction coincide.

Binary is not miniature sonata formMost sonatas have two repeated sections. The first leaves the tonic; the second works back to it. The later exposition–development–recapitulation scheme does not adequately describe all the different routes Scarlatti takes between those points.

The two halves often reach corresponding closing material in different keys. Before those arrivals, however, Scarlatti can insert apparently disproportionate blocks: a phrase repeated insistently, a sudden rest, a long sequence or a change from thin counterpoint to thick chords. Repetition makes the disturbance recognisable rather than smoothing it away. Ralph Kirkpatrick’s account of a structural “crux” draws attention to the point after which each half’s closing material follows a corresponding course. The useful listening question is therefore where the music becomes decisively committed to its destination, not whether the opening tune returns at the expected place.

The dissonances are equally physical. Closely packed added notes can resemble the crushed attacks of a plucked or strummed instrument, while a held bass note allows other harmonies to accumulate above it. In K. 141, the repeated-note attack is set against chords and abrupt changes of register; in K. 119, chordal collisions are part of the movement’s argument. The slow sonatas offer another kind of difficulty. K. 87 in B minor requires several voices to remain independently audible, and K. 208 in A major makes the shaping of a decorated singing line more important than speed.

Print and manuscriptDates of copying, not composition

The thirty Essercizi per gravicembalo, now K. 1–30, were published in London in 1738–39, with a dedication to João V. Scarlatti’s address to the reader proposes ingenious play and keyboard fluency rather than a learned theoretical exposition. The last piece is a fugue whose irregular subject later acquired the nickname “Cat’s Fugue”; the story that a cat supplied its notes is not a documented account of composition. The collection’s published date also does not prove that all thirty pieces were newly written together. Some of the music may have been part of Scarlatti’s working repertory for years.

K. numbersKirkpatrick’s catalogue is not a securely dated diary. The large collections now in Venice and Parma were copied over several years, and their order cannot be treated as a complete chronology of composition. Dates below identify copies or publication where the compositional date is unknown.

Much of the repertory survives in manuscript volumes associated with Maria Bárbara’s collection, rather than in Scarlatti’s autograph scores. Farinelli, who served the Spanish court from 1737, belonged to the same musical household; the sources’ later transmission also intersects with his collection. Scarlatti died in Madrid in 1757, a year before the queen. Later editors and pianists selected a small group of pieces for recitals, often adding performance directions not found in the sources. Complete recordings and renewed study of the manuscripts have restored the slow works, paired sonatas and less familiar experiments to a repertory formerly represented mainly by a handful of technical showpieces.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞Keyboard geography

Register is a compositional variable. One hand can alternate between a low bass and a high response while the other keeps a figure in the middle. Crossing is therefore audible as an exchange between positions on the keyboard, not merely visible as athletic movement. A modern piano changes the decay and balance of those positions.

𝄋Binary form

Both halves normally repeat. The first establishes a key away from the tonic; the second reaches corresponding closing material at home. Scarlatti varies the route through repetition, sequence, interruption and new material. A recognisable closing pattern may be more structurally decisive than the return of the opening gesture.

𝅘𝅥Attack & repetition

Repeated notes renew a harpsichord’s short-lived sound, while thick chords can imitate the density of plucked or strummed attacks. K. 141 makes the repeated note a sustained technical and rhythmic premise. The effect depends on a controlled pulse and changing harmony, not on playing every attack as loudly as possible.

♮Dissonance & tonal turns

Added notes can clash against a chord at the moment of attack; pedal notes can continue under changing harmonies. Sudden shifts between major and minor, or between familiar and remote tonal areas, interrupt an otherwise recognisable phrase. Removing the uncomfortable notes as supposed copying errors can remove the very effect the passage is built around.

𝆏The singing line

The slow sonatas require breath-like articulation, independent inner voices and carefully timed ornaments. K. 87 is contrapuntal; K. 208 is more openly cantabile. Their differences prevent “lyrical Scarlatti” from becoming a single soft, legato manner applied to every slow piece.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Maria Bárbara’s marriage carried Scarlatti’s employment from Portugal to Spain. The portrait presents her dynastic position, while the sonatas record a different part of her education: the keyboard skills cultivated through a long association with her teacher. Her ownership of instruments and manuscript music is a direct material link to the repertory. The painting is paired with the sonatas because of the sitter’s documented patronage, not because its dress or colour is supposed to resemble their sound.

Farinelli appears with the singer Teresa Castellini, the poet Metastasio and Amigoni himself. The group is a constructed image of friendship, not evidence that all four posed together in Madrid. Farinelli and Scarlatti served the same Spanish royal household, and Farinelli’s later collection is part of the history of the sonata manuscripts. The NGV’s painting makes the court’s connections among singing, poetry, collecting and painting visible without inventing a meeting between Scarlatti and an unrelated Spanish artist.

Domenico Scarlatti

To the reader of the Essercizi — opening sentence

1738–39
ItalianNon aspettarti, o dilettante o professor che tu sia, in questi componimenti il profondo intendimento, ma bensì lo scherzo ingegnoso dell’arte, per addestrarti alla franchezza sul gravicembalo.
Our translationWhether you are an amateur or a professional, do not expect learned exposition in these pieces, but rather art’s inventive play, to train you in fluency at the harpsichord.

The preface addresses both kinds of player and names a practical purpose: freedom and assurance at the instrument. It does not instruct the performer to treat the sonatas as emotionally empty exercises. The phrase belongs to Scarlatti’s own publication, unlike the later cat story attached to K. 30. The text is quoted in the Treccani account; spelling and capitalisation are normalised here.

VIListen

Recordings worth seeking out.

  • Complete keyboard sonatasScott Ross, harpsichordErato · 1984–85The 555-sonata cycle places the familiar technical pieces beside slow, contrapuntal and less often selected works. It is a survey rather than an argument that every sonata should be played in catalogue order.
  • Selected sonatasPierre Hantaï, harpsichordMirareA series of selective programmes. Listen for the distinctions among repeated attacks, sustained notes and the releases that expose a change of harmony.
  • The Celebrated Scarlatti RecordingsVladimir Horowitz, pianoSony ClassicalA modern-piano alternative with dynamic and sustaining resources unavailable to a harpsichord. Compare the balance of registers and the shaping of cantabile phrases with Ross or Hantaï.

Passed toAntonio SolerClementi and the keyboard study traditionVladimir HorowitzScott Ross

Antonio Soler’s sonatas belong to the same Iberian court environment, though claims about the exact extent of his lessons with Scarlatti require caution. Later pianists made selected pieces into recital studies in articulation and leaps; harpsichordists restored the effects of plucked attack and instrument-specific resonance. Neither tradition changes the need to distinguish a manuscript’s directions from a later editor’s additions.

Further reading

  • Ralph Kirkpatrick, Domenico Scarlatti (Princeton, 1953) — the catalogue and a performing musician’s analysis of the sonatas; some biographical reconstructions require comparison with later research.
  • W. Dean Sutcliffe, The Keyboard Sonatas of Domenico Scarlatti and Eighteenth-Century Musical Style (Cambridge, 2003) — detailed attention to repetition, discontinuity and form.
  • Treccani, Scarlatti, Giuseppe Domenico — documentary biography; Venice and Parma manuscript index — useful for separating copying dates from composition dates.
