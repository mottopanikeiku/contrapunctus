Life

On 30 May 1705 Alessandro Scarlatti wrote to Ferdinando de’ Medici about his nineteen-year-old son Domenico: “This son is an eagle whose wings have grown; he must not idle in the nest, and I must not prevent his flight.” Domenico already held a post as organist at the royal chapel in Naples and had written operas. His father sent him north towards Venice, by way of Florence, with this recommendation. Domenico later settled much farther from home, as teacher to a Portuguese princess whose marriage took them both to Spain.

ItalyNaples, Rome and the Stabat Mater

Domenico was born in Naples on 26 October 1685, the same year as J. S. Bach and Handel, the sixth of Alessandro’s ten children. His family included singers as well as composers, and several of them worked in opera. He became a salaried royal organist at fifteen. In Rome he served Maria Casimira, the widowed Queen of Poland, who maintained a theatre in her Palazzo Zuccari. Scarlatti supplied her with seven operas between 1710 and 1714, working with the poet Carlo Sigismondo Capece and the stage designer Filippo Juvarra.

Ten voicesThe Stabat Mater calls for four sopranos, two altos, two tenors and two basses, with continuo. Its place and date of composition remain open. The Roman years are a traditional attribution, and Lisbon is another possibility.

At St Peter’s he became assistant director of the Cappella Giulia in 1713 and its head the following year. The Stabat Mater is written for ten independent voices. They sing together in the passages about the grieving mother, then divide into smaller groups. At “Inflammatus et accensus” a tenor begins a long melisma, which the other voices take up in turn. The final ‘Amen’ changes pace again and ends in a fast triple time.

Lisbon and SpainMaria Bárbara

He arrived in Lisbon on 29 November 1719. João V had been waiting impatiently for him, and Queen Maria Anna accompanied him at the keyboard while he sang. His duties included teaching the king’s brother António and his daughter Maria Bárbara. The princess became an exceptional keyboard player. The association that began with these lessons lasted for the rest of Scarlatti’s life, through her marriage, her accession to the Spanish throne and the accumulation of hundreds of sonatas.

Maria Bárbara married the future Ferdinand VI in 1729. Scarlatti joined her in Spain later that year, first at Seville, where the court remained until 1733, then in Madrid and the royal residences around it. In Portugal he had directed court music. In Spain his work centred on the princess’s private music-making. She was his pupil, owned harpsichords and early pianos, and assembled a substantial music library.

In K. 141 in D minor the harpsichord plays rapidly repeated notes, so that each string is plucked again before it has stopped vibrating. Elsewhere, chords thicken until neighbouring notes clash; bass notes are held while the harmony above them changes. These sounds have close parallels in Iberian guitar playing and dance. The D major K. 119 goes furthest in this direction: large clusters of notes interrupt the flow and give the instrument a rougher sound.

Two halvesBinary form in Scarlatti’s sonatas

A sonata in one movementMost of these pieces have two sections, each repeated. The first usually closes away from the home key, and the second returns to it. The word ‘sonata’ here does not mean the several movements of a Beethoven sonata.

Within those two halves Scarlatti allowed himself wide freedom. A phrase may be repeated many times over, then dropped for material that does not resemble what came before. The hands may begin by answering one another and end by playing in the same stretch of keyboard. In K. 27 they cross, so that one arm moves above the hand that goes on playing below it. Bass and treble lie far apart, and the crossing hand has to reach its notes in time.

In the sonatas, the closing passage of the first half often returns near the end of the second, now in the home key. Ralph Kirkpatrick called the point from which the two halves follow their corresponding closing courses the ‘crux’. It can be a more useful landmark than the return of the opening tune. Scarlatti was under no obligation to bring the opening tune back.

The slow pieces ask different things of the hands. In K. 87 in B minor, close-set voices move by small steps, one holding a note while another forms a dissonance against it. The hands start close together. K. 208 in A major is marked Adagio e cantabile. Its decorated melody is set above an accompaniment, in the manner of a solo voice. K. 466 in F minor moves through a succession of cadences, each with a different harmony.

The reader and the queenEssercizi per gravicembalo, London, 1738–⁠39

Thirty sonatas reached print in London in 1738–⁠39 as the Essercizi per gravicembalo, dedicated to João V. In his preface Scarlatti addressed amateurs and professionals together, offering “art’s ingenious play” to help them towards fluency at the harpsichord. He asked his reader to be “more human than critical” and signed off with “Vivi felice” — “Live happily”. The collection closes with K. 30, a fugue whose oddly spaced opening notes later earned it the nickname ‘Cat’s Fugue’.

The dates on the copiesKirkpatrick’s K. numbers identify the sonatas but do not date them. The Venice and Parma manuscripts preserve music copied over many years. Dates in the work list below belong to particular copies or publications.

The great manuscript collections were a more private undertaking. Volumes were copied in 1742 and 1749, followed in the 1750s by the two extensive sets now in Venice and Parma. Many sonatas stand beside a companion in the same key. Farinelli, who had joined the Spanish court in 1737, inherited music from the queen’s collection and brought the manuscripts to Italy. Scarlatti died in Madrid on 23 July 1757. Maria Bárbara survived him by little more than a year, and she and the king had secured pensions for his widow and children.

The sound

𝄞Hands

In K. 27 the hands cross. One hand keeps playing while the other passes over it to reach a distant note, so that bass and treble answer each other across a wide gap. The player has to cover the distance without disturbing the pulse.

𝄋The return home

Most sonatas have two repeated halves. Their endings often correspond: music that first established a new key returns to establish the home key. Between these arrivals Scarlatti can dwell on a phrase, cut it short or take an unusual harmonic detour. Late in the sonata, a passage already heard returns in a different key.

𝅘𝅥A repeated note

K. 141 is in D minor and is built on rapid repetitions of a single pitch. Each attack renews the harpsichord’s fading sound, and changing fingers keeps the chain of notes moving. Below the repeated notes are solid chords.

♮Crowded chords

K. 119 in D major fills the hands with thick chords and dissonant notes. The dissonances sound at the moment the strings are plucked, and a hard edge remains audible after the sound has begun to die. The sonata uses guitar-like strumming and sudden changes from a single line to a fistful of harmony.

𝆏Space for a voice

K. 208 is marked Adagio e cantabile, a slow tempo with a singing line, and its ornaments are part of the melody. K. 87 is written in several voices set close together, with held notes sounding against the notes next to them. K. 208 has a single melodic line over accompaniment, and K. 87 divides its material among several voices.

Sister arts

Ranc painted Maria Bárbara around the time of her marriage to the Spanish heir in 1729. She had already had years of lessons with Scarlatti and took her teacher into her new household. She later owned the sonata manuscripts now divided between Venice and Parma.

Farinelli appears with the singer Teresa Castellini, the poet Metastasio and Amigoni himself. Castellini was leaving Madrid for Italy, and the painting marks the friendship at that point. The dog wears Farinelli’s initials on its collar. He and Scarlatti served the same royal household, and he later carried the queen’s sonata manuscripts back to Italy. The NGV’s account identifies the sitters.

Domenico Scarlatti

To the reader of the Essercizi — opening sentence

1738–⁠39
ItalianNon aspettarti, o dilettante o professor che tu sia, in questi componimenti il profondo intendimento, ma bensì lo scherzo ingegnoso dell’arte, per addestrarti alla franchezza sul gravicembalo.
Our translationAmateur or professional, expect in these compositions no learned exposition, but art’s ingenious play, to train you in fluency at the harpsichord.

Scarlatti addressed the preface to anyone prepared to sit down and play. The thirty pieces that follow demand considerable skill, and the preface ends ‘Vivi felice’, ‘Live happily’. Scarlatti wrote it for his first readers in London in 1738–⁠39. The Italian is transcribed in the Treccani account; spelling and capitalisation are normalised here.

Lineage

Scarlatti
Passed toAntonio SolerClementi and the keyboard study traditionVladimir HorowitzScott Ross

The slow sonatas show the influence of Alessandro’s world of singers, alongside the guitar-like chords of Domenico’s Iberian years. Antonio Soler worked in the same Spanish court world; his precise apprenticeship with Scarlatti is uncertain. Clementi helped bring the sonatas into print for later keyboard players. Horowitz played selected pieces on the piano, and Scott Ross recorded all 555.
