Life

Alessandro Scarlatti had an eagle to launch. Writing to Ferdinando de’ Medici on 30 May 1705, he described his nineteen-year-old son Domenico: “This son is an eagle whose wings have grown; he must not idle in the nest, and I must not prevent his flight.” The boy already had a post as organist at the royal chapel in Naples and operas to his name. His father sent him north towards Venice, by way of Florence, with a recommendation that could hardly be improved upon. Domenico would eventually settle much farther from home, teaching a Portuguese princess whose marriage took them both to Spain.

ItalyA musician among singers

Born in Naples on 26 October 1685, the same year as J. S. Bach and Handel, Domenico was the sixth of Alessandro’s ten children. His family included singers as well as composers; opera was a family business. He became a salaried royal organist at fifteen. Rome gave him another kind of court: Maria Casimira, the widowed Queen of Poland, maintained a theatre in her Palazzo Zuccari. Scarlatti supplied her with seven operas between 1710 and 1714, working with the poet Carlo Sigismondo Capece and the stage designer Filippo Juvarra. There were words, costumes and human voices in his working life long before it became a matter of sonata numbers.

Ten voicesThe Stabat Mater calls for four sopranos, two altos, two tenors and two basses, with continuo. Its place and date of composition remain open: the Roman years are a traditional attribution; Lisbon is another possibility.

At St Peter’s he became assistant director of the Cappella Giulia in 1713 and its head the following year. The Stabat Mater reveals a composer at ease with a crowd of independent voices. They gather around the grieving mother, then separate into smaller groups. At ‘Inflammatus et accensus’ a tenor sets off on a long, impassioned vocal run which the other singers take up. The final ‘Amen’ changes pace again, spilling into a vigorous triple-time close. Scarlatti could sustain a large sacred work through the changing weight and movement of ten singing parts.

Lisbon and SpainThe pupil who stayed

He arrived in Lisbon on 29 November 1719. João V had been waiting impatiently for him, and Queen Maria Anna accompanied him at the keyboard while he sang. His duties included teaching the king’s brother António and his daughter Maria Bárbara. The princess proved an exceptional keyboard player. Their lessons began an association that lasted for the rest of Scarlatti’s life, through her marriage, her accession to the Spanish throne and the accumulation of hundreds of sonatas.

Maria Bárbara married the future Ferdinand VI in 1729. Scarlatti joined her in Spain later that year, first at Seville, where the court remained until 1733, then in Madrid and the royal residences around it. In Portugal he had directed court music; in Spain his work centred on the princess’s private music-making. She owned harpsichords and early pianos, and assembled a substantial music library. The composer and his pupil had instruments, time and an appetite for difficulty. A new sonata could give them something genuinely awkward to solve.

The repeated notes of K. 141 in D minor make the harpsichord rattle like a plucked string struck again before it has finished vibrating. Elsewhere, chords thicken until neighbouring notes grind against one another; bass notes hold on while the harmony above shifts. These sounds have close cousins in Iberian guitar playing and dance. The D major K. 119 is particularly brazen: great handfuls of notes interrupt the flow, and the instrument seems to acquire a rougher voice. The polished court keyboard has learnt to make a racket.

Two halvesGetting home by the long road

A sonata in one movementMost of these pieces have two sections, each repeated. The first usually closes away from the home key; the second finds its way back. The word ‘sonata’ here does not promise the several movements of a Beethoven sonata.

Within those two halves, Scarlatti gives himself astonishing freedom. A phrase can be repeated until it becomes funny, then abandoned for something that sounds as though it has wandered in from another piece. The hands may begin by politely answering one another and end by fighting over the same stretch of keyboard. In K. 27 they cross, placing a moving arm above the hand that must keep playing below it. Bass and treble are far apart, and the hand has to get there in time.

Yet the sonatas know where they are going. Often the closing passage of the first half returns near the end of the second, now in the home key. After the detours, familiar music comes into focus in a new place. Ralph Kirkpatrick called the point from which the two halves follow their corresponding closing courses the ‘crux’. It can be a more useful landmark than the return of the opening tune. Scarlatti has no obligation to begin again where he began.

The slow pieces ask the hands for quite different skills. In K. 87 in B minor, close-set voices move by small steps, one holding a note while another leans painfully against it. The hands start near each other; the grief is compressed. K. 208 in A major carries the marking Adagio e cantabile. A decorated melody sings above its accompaniment with the poise of a solo voice. K. 466 in F minor keeps finding new shades of harmony in a succession of cadences. None of this music needs the excuse of being a rest between fireworks.

The reader and the queen‘Live happily’

Thirty sonatas reached print in London in 1738–39 as the Essercizi per gravicembalo, dedicated to João V. Scarlatti addressed amateurs and professionals together in his preface, offering “art’s ingenious play” to help them towards fluency at the harpsichord. He asked his reader to be “more human than critical” and signed off with “Vivi felice” — “Live happily”. His own contribution to the public discussion of his music is disarmingly hospitable. The collection closes with K. 30, a fugue whose oddly spaced opening notes later earned it the nickname ‘Cat’s Fugue’.

The dates on the copiesKirkpatrick’s K. numbers identify the sonatas; they do not supply a dated diary. The Venice and Parma manuscripts preserve music copied over many years. Dates in the work list below belong to particular copies or publications.

The great manuscript collections were a more private undertaking. Volumes were copied in 1742 and 1749, followed in the 1750s by the two extensive sets now in Venice and Parma. Many sonatas stand beside a companion in the same key. Farinelli, who had joined the Spanish court in 1737, inherited music from the queen’s collection and brought the manuscripts to Italy. Scarlatti died in Madrid on 23 July 1757; Maria Bárbara survived him by little more than a year, and she and the king had secured pensions for his widow and children. Her lessons had ended. The books passed into other hands, carrying the music that had kept hers busy.

The Sound

𝄞Flying hands

In K. 27, crossing hands turns the keyboard into an obstacle course. One hand must continue its work while the other passes over it to reach a distant note. The ear hears bass and treble answering across a gap; the player has to negotiate the distance without disturbing the pulse. The gesture is part of the entertainment.

𝄋The way home

Most sonatas have two repeated halves. Their endings often correspond: music which first secured a new key comes back to secure the home key. Between these arrivals Scarlatti can linger over a phrase, cut it short or take an extravagant harmonic detour. The recognition comes late, when a passage already heard returns with its feet on different ground.

𝅘𝅥A repeated note

K. 141 drives its D minor course with rapid repetitions of a single pitch. Each attack renews the harpsichord’s fading sound, while changing fingers keeps the chain moving. Against this dry rattle, the chords below can sound almost brutally solid. A very small piece of material fills the instrument with noise; speed alone will not give it that bite.

♮Crowded chords

K. 119 in D major packs the hands with thick chords and dissonant notes. The collision happens at the moment the strings are plucked: a hard edge remains audible even after the sound has begun to die. Guitar-like strumming and sudden changes from a single line to a fistful of harmony make the harpsichord a remarkably unruly instrument.

𝆏Room for a voice

K. 208 asks for Adagio e cantabile: a slow pace and a singing line. Its ornaments belong to the melody’s breath. K. 87 works differently, drawing several voices close together and letting held notes rub against their neighbours. Both pieces are intimate, but one gives us a solo singer while the other makes a small company share the sorrow.

Sister Arts

Ranc painted Maria Bárbara around the time of her marriage to the Spanish heir in 1729. She already had years of lessons with Scarlatti behind her and took her teacher into her new household. The richly dressed princess in this portrait would become the owner of the sonata manuscripts now divided between Venice and Parma.

Farinelli appears with the singer Teresa Castellini, the poet Metastasio and Amigoni himself. Castellini was leaving Madrid for Italy; the painting celebrates a friendship about to become a long-distance one. Even the dog wears Farinelli’s initials on its collar. He and Scarlatti served the same royal household, and he later carried the queen’s sonata manuscripts back to Italy. The NGV’s account identifies this affectionate company.

Domenico Scarlatti

To the reader of the Essercizi — opening sentence

1738–39
ItalianNon aspettarti, o dilettante o professor che tu sia, in questi componimenti il profondo intendimento, ma bensì lo scherzo ingegnoso dell’arte, per addestrarti alla franchezza sul gravicembalo.
Our translationAmateur or professional, expect in these compositions no learned exposition, but art’s ingenious play, to train you in fluency at the harpsichord.

Scarlatti welcomes anyone prepared to sit down and play. The thirty pieces that follow demand considerable skill, but his tone is cheerful: the preface ends ‘Vivi felice’, ‘Live happily’. This is the composer addressing his first readers in London in 1738–39. The Italian is transcribed in the Treccani account; spelling and capitalisation are normalised here.

Recordings to own

  • Complete keyboard sonatasScott Ross, harpsichordErato · 1984–85The indispensable map: 555 sonatas, played with rhythmic spring and an appetite for each new piece. Ross gives the unfamiliar works the same care as the recital favourites; the scale of the undertaking never becomes its subject.
  • Selected sonatasPierre Hantaï, harpsichordMirareFor a more combustible Scarlatti. Hantaï relishes the music’s brashness, and the crisp, open sound of his first Mirare volume gives repeated notes a fierce edge without clogging the quick passages.
  • The Celebrated Scarlatti RecordingsVladimir Horowitz, pianoSony ClassicalThe seductive piano route: eighteen sonatas ranging from the F minor song of K. 466 to the brilliance of K. 39 in A major. Horowitz’s touch makes the change from delicacy to danger part of the pleasure.

Lineage

Scarlatti
Passed toAntonio SolerClementi and the keyboard study traditionVladimir HorowitzScott Ross

Alessandro’s world of singers remains audible in the slow sonatas, alongside the guitar-like chords of Domenico’s Iberian years. Antonio Soler worked in the same Spanish court world; his precise apprenticeship with Scarlatti is uncertain. Clementi helped bring the sonatas into print for later keyboard players. Horowitz made selected pieces into dazzling piano performances, while Scott Ross gave all 555 their turn on record.
