26.1Life

Liszt was born in Doborján, a German-speaking village in the Hungarian kingdom, where his father Adam worked on an Esterházy estate. Hungary remained his declared homeland, although German and French were his principal languages. Adam taught him piano before taking him to Vienna in 1822 for lessons with Carl Czerny, who had studied with Beethoven, and Antonio Salieri. The family moved to Paris the following year. The Conservatoire refused him admission as a foreigner; private teaching and public concerts supplied his education instead. His early career therefore joined two institutions that would remain distinct in his adult life: the disciplined lesson and the commercial concert platform.

Adam died in 1827, leaving the sixteen-year-old Liszt to support himself and his mother by teaching. Paris offered an education beyond the keyboard: Victor Hugo, the religious social thinker Félicité de Lamennais, Chopin and Berlioz belonged to his intellectual or musical circle. Hearing Paganini in 1832 suggested that a pianist might make technical difficulty carry the same theatrical force as the violinist’s leaps and harmonics. Liszt responded with prolonged practice and transcription: his piano version of Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique, published in 1834, aimed to preserve orchestral detail.

Switzerland and ItalyPictures, books and a keyboard

In 1835 he left Paris with the married Countess Marie d’Agoult. They lived in Switzerland and Italy and had three children, Blandine, Cosima and Daniel. The landscapes and artworks of these years became the material of Années de pèlerinage, revised for its later publication. The Swiss pieces include a chapel, a spring and the valley of Obermann, the last drawn from Étienne Pivert de Senancour’s novel. The Italian volume places Raphael, Michelangelo, Petrarch and Dante beside one another, allowing the pianist to move between architectural proportion, funeral rhythm, lyric declamation and a sonata-length fantasy.

Three Petrarch sonnetsThe piano pieces retain the older numbering 47, 104 and 123. In modern editions of the Canzoniere they are 61, 134 and 156. Liszt first treated them as songs; the piano versions keep the breath and ornament of a vocal line.

His international touring years, especially 1839–47, made the solo pianist a sufficient attraction for an entire evening. He performed from memory, adapted programmes to the room and used operatic paraphrases to make familiar stage music sound newly dangerous under ten fingers. The Réminiscences de Don Juan combine the Commendatore’s music, the seduction duet and the Champagne Aria into a confrontation between death and appetite. The public image of hair, gloves and extravagant applause has obscured the practical achievement: he made a piano sustain orchestral argument without another performer.

WeimarA theme changes character

Liszt stopped regular paid touring in 1847 and settled in Weimar with Princess Carolyne zu Sayn-Wittgenstein. As court conductor he promoted new music, including the first performance of Wagner’s Lohengrin in 1850 while its composer was in exile. Rehearsing an orchestra gave him repeated opportunities to revise instrumental balances. The Weimar years produced most of the symphonic poems, the Faust and Dante symphonies, and the B minor Piano Sonata. Assistants helped prepare scores; his revisions show continuing involvement in the sound and construction of the works.

The symphonic poem was a single orchestral movement associated with a poem, picture or historical subject. A theme could lose its march rhythm, change mode, broaden its note values or acquire a different accompaniment, while retaining enough intervals to be recognised. In Les Préludes, material related to the opening becomes pastoral lyricism and military display. The published connection with Lamartine followed earlier work on music associated with Joseph Autran’s poems: a literary programme could emerge during composition.

The Sonata in B minor, completed in 1853 and dedicated to Schumann, compresses a multi-movement design into one continuous span. A slow introduction, sonata argument, central slow section and fugato can be heard as separate functions within that span, but their themes keep crossing the boundaries. One of the hard-edged early ideas becomes the singing secondary material. The pianist must distinguish these changes of identity without making every new tempo sound like a new piece. Hans von Bülow gave its first public performance in Berlin in 1857; its structural economy took longer to become as famous as its difficulty.

Urban tunes and Romani bandsThe Hungarian Rhapsodies draw heavily on urban popular tunes and the performance style of Romani bands, including the slow lassú and quick friss. These sources differ from the rural repertories later collected by Bartók and Kodály.

Liszt’s support for other composers extended beyond conducting. His transcriptions circulated Beethoven’s nine symphonies, Schubert songs and Wagner scenes in homes without an orchestra. He helped raise money for the Beethoven monument unveiled in Bonn in 1845 and taught large numbers of pianists without charging for his mature masterclasses. His advocacy of programme music, and the polemics surrounding the “New German School”, helped turn differences of method into a public contest with the circle around Brahms.

Rome, Weimar, BudapestThree homes and fewer notes

In 1861 Liszt moved to Rome, where his planned marriage to Carolyne was prevented by unresolved difficulties over the annulment of her earlier marriage. He received minor orders in 1865, short of priestly ordination, and became known as Abbé Liszt. Religious composition had preceded this change of dress. The oratorio Christus, completed in the 1860s, uses plainchant, pastoral writing and large choral movements. In the final decades he divided his year between Rome, teaching in Weimar and work in Budapest, where the new music academy gave his Hungarian allegiance an institutional form.

The later piano music can be startling after the rhapsodies. Nuages gris of 1881 is spare, low-voiced and harmonically unsettled; augmented triads and chromatic bass movement weaken the distinction between departure and arrival. La lugubre gondola, connected with his stay in Venice before Wagner’s death, makes rocking motion carry a funeral association without a regular march. The title Bagatelle sans tonalité identifies an experiment in withholding a stable tonal centre. The decorative surface has been removed, leaving the unstable intervals exposed.

He continued to teach, attend performances and revise music despite failing eyesight and health. In July 1886 he went to Bayreuth, where Cosima, now Wagner’s widow, directed the festival. He died there of pneumonia at seventy-four. His pupils carried forward sharply different Liszts: the public virtuoso, the interpreter of Beethoven and the composer whose late pages required almost no display. The distance between a Hungarian Rhapsody and Nuages gris marks a change in the use of the instrument.

26.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄋Thematic transformation

Keep a melodic outline; alter its metre, mode, tempo or accompaniment until its dramatic function changes. In the B minor Sonata, material introduced with force returns as lyric melody and later as a fugato subject. Unlike a conventional variation set, the transformations occur inside an unfolding form rather than as a row of self-contained versions.

𝄞Piano as orchestra

Octave doublings assign weight to a line; tremolos supply sustained energy; arpeggios prolong a harmony while the hand travels through several registers. The player often has to distinguish a bass, an inner tune and upper decoration with only two hands. The Beethoven transcriptions require each instrumental function to remain distinct.

♯Harmony

Chromatic sequences and enharmonic respelling let a chord become an entrance to another key. An augmented triad divides the octave into three equal major thirds, so no note has an uncontested claim to be its root. The Faust Symphony exploits that symmetry; late pieces such as Nuages gris withhold the cadence that would normally settle the ambiguity.

𝄾Declamation and silence

The Petrarch pieces preserve a singer’s long appoggiaturas, breath points and cadenzas. Liszt often writes a melody alone before supplying its harmony, as if the pianist were speaking before the orchestra answers. Rests in the Sonata’s introduction and the exposed lines of the late pieces mark structural separations.

𝄢Borrowing and form

A borrowed tune can become the skeleton of a long movement: the Dies irae in Totentanz, Meyerbeer’s chorale from Le Prophète in the fantasy and fugue Ad nos, ad salutarem undam, or a song in an operatic paraphrase. Liszt changes the scale of its treatment through register, counterpoint, texture and the distribution of climaxes.

𝆏Colour without a tune

Les jeux d’eaux à la Villa d’Este distributes repeated notes, trills and broken chords across the upper keyboard. The ear follows changing densities and harmonic light as much as a continuous melody. Ravel’s Jeux d’eau inherits the problem of making water audible through the mechanics of the piano.

26.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1851
    Hungarian Rhapsody No. 2 in C♯ minorS. 244/2Piano
    Published in 1851. A slow lassú gives way to the accelerating dance-world of the friss; accent, register and flexible tempo imitate the delivery of a band.
  2. 1852
    Transcendental ÉtudesS. 139Piano
    The final form of twelve studies whose first version dates from 1826. Mazeppa makes a repeated-note figure drive a large narrative; Chasse-neige builds its texture from tremolo.
  3. 1853
    Piano Sonata in B minorS. 178Piano
    One continuous movement in which a few motifs become march, song, slow meditation and fugato. The quiet B major ending replaces an earlier loud conclusion preserved in the manuscript.
  4. 1854
    Les PréludesS. 97Orchestra
    Premiered in Weimar in 1854. Earlier choral-overture material became a symphonic poem carrying a programme after Lamartine; hear how the same thematic family serves lyrical and martial episodes.
  5. 1855
    Piano Concerto No. 1 in E♭ majorS. 124Piano and orchestra
    Premiered in Weimar with Liszt as soloist and Berlioz conducting. Its connected sections recall earlier themes in altered functions; the scherzo gives the triangle an unusually exposed role.
  6. 1855
    Années de pèlerinage: Première année, SuisseS. 160Piano
    Published in 1855, reworking music from the Swiss years. Vallée d’Obermann turns a questioning descending phrase into an extended argument; Au lac de Wallenstadt suspends a song above rippling accompaniment.
  7. 1857
    A Faust SymphonyS. 108Orchestra; tenor and male chorus in the revised ending
    Three character studies: Faust, Gretchen and Mephistopheles. Begun in 1854; the choral conclusion was added for 1857. Mephistopheles deforms Faust’s themes rather than receiving a wholly independent thematic vocabulary.
  8. 1858
    Années de pèlerinage: Deuxième année, ItalieS. 161Piano
    Published in 1858. Raphael and Michelangelo open the book, three Petrarch sonnets occupy its centre, and Après une lecture du Dante closes it with tritones, tremolos and transformed thematic returns.
  9. 1877
    Les jeux d’eaux à la Villa d’EsteS. 163/4Piano
    Part of the third book of Années de pèlerinage, published in 1883. Its fountains have a scriptural as well as a visual meaning: Liszt inserts a Latin reference to the water of eternal life from John’s Gospel.
  10. 1881
    Nuages gris (Grey Clouds)S. 199Piano
    A short late experiment in chromatic bass movement, augmented harmony and an ending that refuses a conventional tonic cadence. Much of the argument is carried by bare intervals.

26.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

The direct source for Sposalizio, the first piece of the Italian Années. Raphael’s central temple and radiating pavement give the crowded ceremony a strict perspective. Liszt’s small opening idea expands through repetition and harmonic enlargement until it can sustain a ceremonial climax. The connection is named in the score.

The helmeted, seated figure from the tomb of Lorenzo, Duke of Urbino, supplied the title of Liszt’s Il penseroso. Its low register, dotted tread and chromatic harmonies make a deliberate contrast with the preceding Sposalizio. Liszt also printed Michelangelo’s four-line answer to a poem about the adjacent figure of Night: he connected the chapel’s sculpture with sleep, stone and the wish not to be awakened.

Francesco Petrarca

Pace non trovo — opening quatrain

Fourteenth century; Liszt’s Sonetto 104
ItalianPace non trovo, et non ò da far guerra; e temo, et spero; et ardo, et son un ghiaccio; e volo sopra ’l cielo, et giaccio in terra; et nulla stringo, et tutto ’l mondo abbraccio.
Our translationI find no peace, and have no war to wage; I fear and hope; I burn and am ice; I fly above the sky and lie upon the earth; I grasp nothing, and embrace the whole world.

The opening of Sonnet 134 in the modern numbering, called 104 in Liszt’s edition. The contradictory verbs give a reason for the music’s changes between declamation, sustained song and sudden agitation. Liszt’s piano setting is descended from his vocal one: the decorated right-hand line becomes an instrumental singer.

26.8Lineage

Liszt
Passed toRichard WagnerRichard StraussClaude DebussyMaurice RavelBéla BartókFerruccio Busoni

Liszt’s symphonic poems supplied Strauss with a model for organising a large orchestral movement around a subject and recurring themes. His transcriptions taught Busoni to regard transcription as interpretation. Ravel inherited the water-piece’s keyboard techniques; Bartók admired the late harmonic experiments while rejecting Liszt’s account of Hungarian musical sources.

Questions to take away

  1. Can you follow a melodic line through the changing hands and registers that carry it?

    The rippling surface of this miniature hides an intricate exchange between the hands: accompaniment and melodic notes cross registers without breaking the flow. Eugen d’Albert, who studied with Liszt, records it on an early Odeon disc.

    Années de pèlerinage I: Suisse, S. 160 · 4. Au bord d’une source

  2. What makes the fast dance feel like a release from the slow opening?

    A measured, declamatory Lassan gives way to a dance-like Friska, then delays its conclusion before the final rush. Martha Goldstein’s performance lets the contrasts of pulse, register and density carry a piece often reduced to its most familiar tune.

    Hungarian Rhapsody No. 2, S. 244/2
