FrédéricChopin

Born 1 March 1810, Żelazowa Wola, Duchy of WarsawDied 17 October 1849, Paris

He made the piano sing through ornament, contrapuntal voice-leading and the unstable accents of Polish dance.

ILife

Chopin was born at Żelazowa Wola in 1810, to a French-born father, Nicolas, and a Polish mother, Justyna Krzyżanowska. The family soon moved to Warsaw, where Nicolas taught French. The name Fryderyk belongs to his Polish life; Frédéric became usual in France. His family observed his birthday on 1 March, although the baptismal record gives 22 February. Piano lessons with Wojciech Żywny introduced Bach and Mozart alongside the fashionable repertory of the day. As a child he composed polonaises and played in aristocratic houses, but his later musical education extended beyond the cultivation of a prodigy.

Józef Elsner taught him harmony and composition at Warsaw’s Main School of Music from 1826 to 1829. Chopin heard Italian opera, encountered Polish vernacular music during visits outside the capital and learned the working forms of the virtuoso concerto. The F minor Concerto was composed before the E minor, despite their eventual numbering as Nos. 2 and 1. Both preserve an extended orchestral opening, but once the piano enters, its vocal line and ornament determine the movement’s scale. The slow movements belong as much to operatic singing as to the display repertory of a concert pianist.

1830–31Leaving Warsaw

He left Poland in November 1830, shortly before the uprising against Russian rule. After an uncomfortable period in Vienna he reached Paris in 1831 and never returned to his homeland. Polish émigré society remained part of his daily life; poets, aristocratic patrons and musicians circulated between its private gatherings. Yet the mazurkas and polonaises are not transcripts of political events. Their altered accents, modal inflections, drones and intricate phrase structures convert dance materials into composed piano music. Exile helps explain their persistence without supplying a reliable programme for each piece.

The dance familyA mazurka can draw on the mazur, kujawiak and oberek: related Polish triple-metre dances with different speeds and accents. Chopin’s title names a field of possibilities, not one rigid rhythmic formula.

Paris provided publishers, piano makers, pupils and colleagues including Liszt, Bellini and the painter Eugène Delacroix. Chopin’s first Paris concert took place in 1832, but public performance remained a small part of his livelihood. Lessons for wealthy pupils paid more consistently and suited an approach to sound that depended on close listening. He favoured Pleyel instruments, whose responsive action and tone rewarded distinctions of touch. His teaching, preserved in pupils’ accounts and annotated scores, concerned phrasing, fingering, balance and a hand’s natural differences rather than the production of equal force from every finger.

The études turn specific physical demands into complete musical structures. Op. 10 No. 1 requires broad arpeggios while preserving a bass line and harmonic direction; No. 2 asks the weaker outer fingers of the right hand to carry a chromatic scale while other fingers play accompanying notes. The later Op. 25 places similarly exact problems inside sustained phrases, contrary motion and registral changes. Their virtuosity cannot be separated from voicing: playing all the written notes evenly may conceal the melody or inner part for which the difficulty exists.

The speaking pianoOrnament as composition

The nocturnes inherited a genre cultivated by John Field, but Chopin made its melody and accompaniment less predictable. A returning phrase can carry new ornaments, a changed bass or an inner voice that competes with the upper line. An appoggiatura — a leaning dissonance that delays a chord note — can take enough time to change the apparent weight of a beat. Ornament is consequently not a removable fringe. The performer must distinguish a structural arrival from the notes approaching it, just as a singer distinguishes a vowel’s destination from the consonants and grace notes around it.

The ballades and scherzos expanded this principle to large single movements. Chopin’s four ballades suggest narrative through thematic return and altered context, but no surviving statement authorises a detailed story for each bar. The G minor First Ballade changes the balance of its themes at their return; the F minor Fourth combines variation, contrapuntal intensification and a coda preceded by quiet chords. The scherzos preserve triple metre while abandoning the modest scale of a symphonic inner movement. Their abrupt registers and silences are written structural contrasts, not invitations to arbitrary speeding up.

Rubato has more than one levelPupils describe a singing line that may borrow time against a steadier accompaniment, but Chopin also wrote tempo changes affecting the whole texture. ‘Keep the left hand strict’ is not a rule that explains every phrase.

1838–46Mallorca and Nohant

His relationship with the novelist George Sand began in 1838. That winter they travelled to Mallorca with her children, Maurice and Solange, eventually staying in the former monastery at Valldemossa. Illness, bad weather and practical difficulties qualify the later tourist image of a productive retreat. Chopin completed the twenty-four Preludes, Op. 28, there, but had begun the collection earlier. Their arrangement moves through major keys and their relative minors around the circle of fifths. Some are concentrated studies in one texture; others make an interrupted gesture or unresolved pressure sufficient for an independent piece.

Sand’s house at Nohant gave him more stable working conditions during subsequent summers. Delacroix visited, drew and listened; Sand’s accounts describe repeated revision rather than effortless improvisation transferred directly to paper. The B♭ minor Sonata, completed in 1839 around an earlier funeral march, ends with a rapid unison movement that removes the usual distinction between tune and accompaniment. The B minor Sonata of 1844 uses a more expansive four-movement design, including a long Largo. These two sonatas cannot be adequately explained by opposing a ‘poetic’ Chopin to an allegedly conventional sonata form.

The late works increasingly expose independent voices. In the F♯ major Barcarolle, paired melodic lines move above a rocking bass, and ornamental returns alter the density of the duet. The Polonaise-Fantaisie introduces fragments and harmonic uncertainty before a dance rhythm can consolidate. The two Nocturnes, Op. 62, let trills and inner counterpoint prolong the melody without simply increasing its speed. His Cello Sonata, written with the practical knowledge of Auguste Franchomme, transfers these problems to an actual sustaining instrument. The cello and piano negotiate register and continuity rather than dividing permanently into singer and accompaniment.

1847–49Last performances

The relationship with Sand ended in 1847 amid family conflict. Chopin’s health continued to decline, and the revolution of 1848 disrupted Parisian teaching and concert life. With support from his pupil Jane Stirling he travelled to Britain, performing and visiting patrons in England and Scotland. His last public appearance was at a Polish charity event at London’s Guildhall in November 1848. Back in Paris, he died the following October, aged thirty-nine. His body was buried at Père-Lachaise; his sister Ludwika took his heart to Warsaw. The familiar diagnosis of tuberculosis belongs to a documented history of chronic pulmonary illness, not to a romantic explanation of his style.

Publication left another practical legacy. Works appeared through French, German and English publishers, sometimes with differences between first editions, corrected proofs and pupils’ copies. A modern pianist therefore encounters variants, not a single mechanically transmitted text. Decisions about accidentals, ornaments, fingering and pedalling can require comparison of sources. Chopin’s pupils and later editors preserved indispensable evidence, but their preferred solutions do not all carry the authority of his autograph. The music’s apparent improvisatory freedom rests on unusually specific choices of notes, timing and voice-leading.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝅘𝅥Bel canto

The nocturne’s upper line behaves like a singer: appoggiaturas delay chord notes, ornaments expand repeated phrases and cadences require breathing. The piano cannot sustain a note by adding breath, so accompaniment, pedalling and the timing of subsequent notes must preserve the impression of a continuous voice.

♯Chromatic voice-leading

A chord may change because one inner voice moves a semitone while another note is retained. In the E minor Prelude, Op. 28 No. 4, repeated melodic notes allow the accompaniment’s descending motion to carry much of the drama. Hear lines moving inside chords, not merely a succession of chord labels.

𝄾Dance accent

Mazurkas displace emphasis towards the second or third beat, lengthen a note across an expected accent, or preserve a drone beneath altered scale degrees. Polonaises use a different rhythmic profile and public bearing. Neither genre should be flattened into a generic waltz in three.

𝄞The pianist’s hand

Études isolate extensions, thirds, sixths, octaves and chromatic fingering, but their difficulty includes maintaining separate voices. Chopin’s fingering can move a thumb onto a black key or let one finger pass over another when that serves a legato line.

𝄋Form & return

A returning theme seldom means a return to an unchanged state. The Fourth Ballade enriches its principal material through imitation and decoration; the Polonaise-Fantaisie delays the stability promised by its dance title. Recollection and recomposition operate together.

𝄢Counterpoint

Bach remained a practical model. In the late nocturnes and Barcarolle, the accompanimental texture can contain a second melodic argument. A pianist who gives every phrase to the highest note will miss entrances and suspensions placed between the hands.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1827
    Variations on “Là ci darem la mano”Op. 2Piano and orchestra
    Mozart’s duet becomes a sequence of contrasting piano textures; the work prompted Schumann’s early public advocacy.
  2. 1829–30
    Piano Concerto No. 2 in F minorOp. 21Piano and orchestra
    Composed before No. 1. Its Larghetto makes operatic ornament the centre of the solo part.
  3. 1830
    Piano Concerto No. 1 in E minorOp. 11Piano and orchestra
    A broad first movement, an E major Romance and a finale whose syncopations draw on the krakowiak.
  4. 1829–32
    Twelve ÉtudesOp. 10Piano
    Each technical problem produces a distinctive texture; No. 2 combines a right-hand chromatic scale with accompanying notes in the same hand.
  5. 1831–35
    Ballade No. 1 in G minorOp. 23Piano
    A large single movement whose transformed thematic returns prepare a fast coda rather than a conventional rondo refrain.
  6. 1832–37
    Twelve ÉtudesOp. 25Piano
    Includes studies in thirds, sixths, octaves and contrary motion; the technical task always has a harmonic and melodic function.
  7. 1836
    Two NocturnesOp. 27Piano
    C♯ minor and D♭ major share a tonic pitch under different spellings; the second sustains a decorated duet above its rocking accompaniment.
  8. 1836–39
    Twenty-four PreludesOp. 28Piano
    Major and relative-minor pairs move around the circle of fifths, allowing extreme contrasts of scale, register and duration.
  9. 1837–39
    Piano Sonata No. 2 in B♭ minorOp. 35Piano
    The funeral march predates the complete sonata; the finale’s unison writing removes the usual distinction between melody and harmony.
  10. 1842
    Ballade No. 4 in F minorOp. 52Piano
    Variation and counterpoint gradually intensify the main theme; quiet chords make a break before the coda.
  11. 1842
    Polonaise in A♭ majorOp. 53Piano
    A recurrent polonaise rhythm frames a central passage driven by repeated left-hand octaves.
  12. 1844
    Piano Sonata No. 3 in B minorOp. 58Piano
    A four-movement design with a B major Largo and a finale whose recurring theme gathers increasing momentum.
  13. 1845–46
    Barcarolle in F♯ majorOp. 60Piano
    Compound metre and a rocking bass support two intertwined singing parts; returns are recomposed through ornament and register.
  14. 1845–46
    Polonaise-Fantaisie in A♭ majorOp. 61Piano
    An introduction of isolated gestures and resonant arpeggios precedes the gradual consolidation of dance and large-scale form.
  15. 1845–46
    Cello Sonata in G minorOp. 65Cello and piano
    Written for Auguste Franchomme; its dense first movement and compact Largo reveal two different kinds of instrumental partnership.

Wiosna · opening three stanzas

Set by Chopin in 1838
PolishBłyszczą, krople rosy, Mruczy zdrój po błoni, Ukryta we wrzosy Gdzieś jałowka dzwoni. Piękną, miłą błonią Leci wzrok wesoło; W koło kwiaty wonią, Kwitną gaje w koło. Paś się, błąkaj, trzódko, Ja pod skałą siędę, Piosnkę lubą, słodką Śpiewać sobie będę.
Our translationDrops of dew gleam; a spring murmurs across the meadow. Hidden somewhere in the heather, a heifer’s bell rings. Across the lovely meadow my gaze flies gladly; flowers give their scent all round, and all around the groves are blooming. Graze and wander, little herd; I shall sit beneath the rock and sing myself a dear, sweet song.

Chopin set this poem as Wiosna, later published as Op. 74 No. 2. These are the first three stanzas; the poem subsequently turns towards an unexplained sorrow and a lark’s ascending song. His small body of Polish songs establishes an actual relation to émigré poetry without inventing literary programmes for the instrumental ballades. Polish sung text.

VIILineage

Chopin
Passed toFranz LisztGabriel FauréClaude DebussyAlexander ScriabinSergei Rachmaninoff

Debussy’s études retain the idea of a technical constraint producing an independent piece; Scriabin began with Chopinesque genres before changing their harmonic basis. Rachmaninoff inherited the problem of sustaining a vocal line through an intensely active keyboard texture. None of these connections requires describing Chopin’s piano as an imitation orchestra.
