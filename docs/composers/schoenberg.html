48.1Life

Arnold Schoenberg was born in Vienna’s Leopoldstadt, the son of a Jewish shopkeeper, and learned the violin as a child. When his father died in 1889, the family’s finances made extended formal study impossible. He worked in a bank from 1891 to 1895, played chamber music with friends and learned composition by examining scores. Alexander Zemlinsky, whom he met through the amateur orchestra Polyhymnia, gave him counterpoint lessons. Schoenberg’s later teaching grew from this close reading: he explained, with unusual insistence, what each note in Bach, Mozart, Beethoven and Brahms was doing.

In the 1890s the apparent opposition between Brahms and Wagner organised Viennese musical argument. Schoenberg took techniques from both. From Brahms came the continuous alteration of a small motif, later described as developing variation; from Wagner came chromatic harmony whose resolution could be postponed across an entire scene. Verklärte Nacht, written in 1899 for string sextet, applies these procedures to Richard Dehmel’s poem about a woman confessing to her lover that she carries another man’s child. The opening D minor eventually transforms into D major, supplying the work’s large-scale harmonic argument.

Vienna and BerlinA teacher before a school

Schoenberg married Zemlinsky’s sister Mathilde in 1901 and moved to Berlin, where he worked at Ernst von Wolzogen’s cabaret, the Überbrettl. The surviving Brettl-Lieder belong to that theatrical world. Richard Strauss helped him obtain work and support. Back in Vienna in 1903, he taught privately and composed the symphonic poem Pelleas und Melisande, drawing on Maeterlinck’s play independently of Debussy’s opera. His First String Quartet, completed in 1905, compresses the functions of a four-movement cycle into a continuous movement in D minor.

Alban Berg and Anton Webern began studying with him in 1904. Their lessons included harmony and counterpoint. Mahler supported Schoenberg materially and publicly, although he did not find all the new music easy to understand. The Chamber Symphony, Op. 9, completed in 1906, reduces the late-Romantic orchestra to fifteen solo instruments. Its opening horn writing builds chains of fourths, while whole-tone formations interrupt the pull of ordinary major and minor triads. Its nominal E major still provides a destination, but the route to it is increasingly difficult to predict.

Two distinct practicesSchoenberg abandoned a governing key around 1908, writing in free atonality. He developed his ordered twelve-note method more than a decade later.

The Second String Quartet, composed in 1907–08, introduces a soprano in its last two movements, setting Stefan George. The finale begins with “Ich fühle Luft von anderem Planeten” — “I feel air from another planet.” Here the key signature disappears and the tonal centre becomes uncertain, though the movement eventually closes on F♯ major. During its composition Mathilde had an affair with the painter Richard Gerstl, who killed himself after she returned to her family. Earlier scores had already taken technical steps towards atonality.

1909–12Music without a home key

In 1909 Schoenberg wrote the Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11, the Five Orchestral Pieces, Op. 16, and the monodrama Erwartung. Without tonic and dominant to regulate expectation, phrase length, register, instrumental colour and recurring intervals take on more structural responsibility. Erwartung follows one woman through a forest at night as she discovers her lover’s body. Marie Pappenheim’s text gives the soprano abrupt changes of perception; the orchestra responds without supplying the recurrent, named leitmotifs of Wagnerian drama. The score was not staged until 1924, fifteen years after its composition.

The third of the Five Orchestral Pieces, later entitled Farben, begins with a five-note chord whose instrumental distribution changes almost imperceptibly. Tiny contrapuntal movements alter its internal pitches too. Schoenberg’s Harmonielehre, published in 1911 and dedicated to Mahler’s memory, speculates about melodies of tone-colour. The book itself remains a practical investigation of tonal harmony, including the behaviour of dissonance. Its author was still teaching students how chords resolve while composing pieces in which resolution was no longer obligatory.

Painting occupied him intensely during these same years. His portraits and staring heads were exhibited in Vienna in 1910. After hearing Schoenberg’s music in Munich in January 1911, Wassily Kandinsky began a correspondence with him about the independence of artistic means from inherited representation. Schoenberg exhibited with the first Blaue Reiter exhibition later that year. His paintings remained figurative even when Kandinsky was moving away from recognisable objects.

Speaking on notesIn Pierrot lunaire, crossed note-stems distinguish Sprechstimme from ordinary singing. Schoenberg asks the performer to observe the rhythm, touch the indicated pitch and leave it by rising or falling.

For the actress Albertine Zehme he composed Pierrot lunaire in 1912: twenty-one poems by Albert Giraud in Otto Erich Hartleben’s German versions, arranged in three groups of seven. Five instrumentalists play flute and piccolo, clarinet and bass clarinet, violin and viola, cello and piano. The speaking voice is only one innovation. Nacht uses a passacaglia; Der Mondfleck employs canonic and backward-moving counterpoint. Old procedures organise new sounds, and the instrumentation changes from poem to poem. Schoenberg conducted the Berlin premiere and the subsequent tour.

After the warThe row and the dance

The huge Gurre-Lieder, begun in 1900 and finally orchestrated in 1911, received a successful premiere under Franz Schreker in Vienna in 1913. Its late-Romantic idiom already belonged to Schoenberg’s past. Military service interrupted his work during the First World War, and the oratorio Die Jakobsleiter remained unfinished. In 1918 he founded the Society for Private Musical Performances. Membership, extensive rehearsal, repeated performances and the exclusion of newspaper critics were intended to replace the hostile public concert with sustained listening. The programmes included Debussy, Ravel, Stravinsky and Mahler, often in chamber reductions.

By the early 1920s he had formulated composition with twelve tones related to one another. A work could derive its pitches from an ordered series containing all twelve pitch classes, used forward, backward, inverted or in transposition. Rhythm, register, dynamics and instrumentation remained the composer’s choices; successive row notes might pass between voices or form chords. The practical ambition was continuity over longer spans without returning to a home key.

The Suite for Piano, Op. 25, composed in 1921–23, applies one row throughout a sequence of prelude, dances and intermezzo. Its final four row notes, B–C–A–B♭, spell B–A–C–H when reversed in German note names. The homage to Bach is embedded in the material. Mathilde died in 1923; Schoenberg married Gertrud Kolisch in 1924. In 1926 he began teaching a composition masterclass at the Prussian Academy of Arts in Berlin. The Variations for Orchestra, Op. 31, brought twelve-tone technique into a large public orchestral work, with theme, nine variations and finale.

ExileBerlin, Paris, Los Angeles

His opera Moses und Aron, composed principally in 1930–32, makes the difficulty of communicating an idea its dramatic subject. Moses speaks; Aron sings, translating an unrepresentable God into images and promises that the crowd can understand. The opposition is carried by vocal technique as well as text. Schoenberg completed the music of two acts and left the third unset. The unfinished act posed a long-standing dramatic and theological problem, which Schoenberg continued to consider after emigrating.

The Nazi seizure of power ended his Berlin appointment in 1933. Schoenberg, who had converted to Protestantism in 1898, formally returned to Judaism in Paris, with Marc Chagall among the witnesses. He emigrated to the United States that autumn, first teaching in Boston and then settling in Los Angeles in 1934. At UCLA his pupils encountered rigorous exercises in traditional musical craft; John Cage also studied with him in California. The American spelling “Schoenberg” replaced “Schönberg”. Alongside serial works he continued to compose tonal music, including the Suite in G for string orchestra, written in 1934.

A constructed testimonyA Survivor from Warsaw uses Schoenberg’s own dramatic text. Its English narration, German commands and Hebrew prayer have distinct musical functions.

The Piano Concerto of 1942 folds four contrasting sections into one continuous span. The String Trio of 1946 subjects related material to abrupt breaks and compressed returns; its composition followed a severe illness. In A Survivor from Warsaw, written in 1947, a narrator describes a roundup under Nazi occupation before the men’s chorus enters with the Shema Yisrael. Schoenberg died in Los Angeles in 1951. The post-war composers who adopted his method often preferred Webern’s smaller, more symmetrical structures, but Schoenberg’s own scores retain the long-breathed phrases, contrapuntal density and dramatic reversals of the nineteenth-century music he taught.

48.2The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄋Developing variation

A motif changes as it returns: an interval widens, an upbeat moves, a rhythm is compressed. Schoenberg heard this procedure in Brahms and made it the means of connecting phrases that no longer shared a key. Listen for related shapes through these transformations.

♮Harmony

The early works intensify chromatic tonality; the music of 1909 stops requiring dissonance to resolve into consonance. A semitone pressed together, a widely spaced major seventh and a fourth in the horn create different tensions. In Farben, the spacing and instrumental weight of five pitches matter as much as their names.

𝄆Twelve-tone technique

The ordered row controls pitch relationships. Its inversions reverse the direction of intervals; its retrogrades reverse note order. Schoenberg distributes these forms between melody and accompaniment, repeats notes and groups segments into chords. The dance rhythms of Op. 25 and the rhetoric of the Piano Concerto are further compositional choices.

𝅘𝅥Voice

Pierrot lunaire separates speech-like pitch from exact rhythmic placement. Erwartung uses a fully singing soprano, often at the extremes of register. Moses und Aron places spoken and sung delivery in direct conflict. These are distinct solutions to the relation between a verbal sentence and a musical phrase.

𝄞Orchestration

The Chamber Symphony makes fifteen solo parts compete without a string section to absorb their edges. Op. 16 moves between sharply contrasted blocks and the almost continuous redistribution of colour in Farben. The later scores mark principal and subsidiary voices — Hauptstimme and Nebenstimme — so performers can make a hierarchy audible inside dense counterpoint.

𝄐Form

Schoenberg kept sonata procedures, variation form, canon and the Baroque suite after abandoning tonal key schemes. Their functions change: returning row forms and motivic shapes can mark a recapitulation without a tonic. Erwartung represents the opposite extreme, binding its continuous movement to the woman’s rapidly changing perceptions.

48.3Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1899
    Verklärte Nacht (Transfigured Night)Op. 4Two violins, two violas and two cellos; later arranged for string orchestra
    Dehmel’s poem supplies five broad sections. D minor becomes D major as the man accepts the woman and her unborn child.
  2. 1900–11
    Gurre-LiederSoloists, speaker, choruses and very large orchestra
    Most of the composition dates from 1900–01; the orchestration was completed in 1911. Jens Peter Jacobsen’s Danish poems reach Schoenberg through Robert Franz Arnold’s German translation.
  3. 1906
    Chamber Symphony No. 1 in E majorOp. 9Fifteen solo instruments
    A continuous movement with scherzo and slow sections, opened by fourth-built and whole-tone harmonies. Five solo strings stand against ten wind instruments.
  4. 1907–08
    String Quartet No. 2Op. 10String quartet; soprano in movements III and IV
    The F♯ minor opening belongs to a work whose George settings move beyond a governing key. The finale nevertheless ends on an F♯ major chord.
  5. 1909
    Five Orchestral PiecesOp. 16Large orchestra; revised scoring 1949
    The third piece, Farben, makes changes of instrumental colour a structural event. The titles were added after composition.
  6. 1909
    Erwartung (Expectation)Op. 17Soprano and orchestra; monodrama to a text by Marie Pappenheim
    A single woman’s nocturnal search, in four scenes without a break. First staged in Prague in 1924 under Alexander Zemlinsky.
  7. 1912
    Pierrot lunaireOp. 21Speaking voice and five instrumentalists
    Twenty-one melodramas in three groups of seven. The instrumental changes, strict counterpoint and speech-song deliver different readings of Hartleben’s grotesque moon poems.
  8. 1921–23
    Suite for PianoOp. 25Piano
    One twelve-note row serves every movement, including gavotte, musette, minuet and gigue. The last four notes conceal the B–A–C–H signature in reverse.
  9. 1926–28
    Variations for OrchestraOp. 31Orchestra
    Introduction, theme, nine variations and finale. Twelve-tone counterpoint is projected across the orchestra; a B–A–C–H figure is explicitly heard in the work.
  10. 1930–32
    Moses und AronOpera for soloists, chorus and orchestra; two acts completed
    Moses’s speech and Aron’s tenor singing embody a dispute over thought and representation. The third act survives as text alone.
  11. 1942
    Piano ConcertoOp. 42Piano and orchestra
    Four linked sections replace separate movements. The opening waltz-like metre and the closing rondo make the music’s phrase shapes audible before its serial construction is analysed.
  12. 1947
    A Survivor from WarsawOp. 46Narrator, men’s chorus and orchestra
    A brief dramatic account in English and German ends with the Hebrew Shema Yisrael. Schoenberg assembled the text himself.

48.5Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Schoenberg painted this small frontal self-portrait during the period of Op. 11 and Erwartung. Colour alters the face without eliminating it: the blue surface is still organised around eyes, nose and mouth. His first exhibition took place in Vienna in 1910, and four paintings appeared in the first Blaue Reiter exhibition in 1911. Commons identifies this reproduction as public domain; Schoenberg’s works entered the public domain in life-plus-seventy-year jurisdictions in 2022.

Gerstl painted Arnold, Mathilde and their children during his association with the family. Broad brushmarks break up the conventional portrait group; the figures remain present without precise facial description. His affair with Mathilde and his suicide in November 1908 coincide with the Second String Quartet’s completion and first performance. The connection is biographical.

Kandinsky began corresponding with Schoenberg after a Munich concert in January 1911. This large canvas was exhibited with the first Blaue Reiter exhibition, where Schoenberg’s paintings also appeared. Its residual Last Judgement imagery is broken into lines and colour masses. The parallel lies in the pair’s debate about inherited artistic rules.

Albert Giraud, German version by Otto Erich Hartleben

Mondestrunken

French original 1884; German version 1892
GermanDen Wein, den man mit Augen trinkt, Gießt Nachts der Mond in Wogen nieder, Und eine Springflut überschwemmt Den stillen Horizont. Gelüste schauerlich und süß, Durchschwimmen ohne Zahl die Fluten! Den Wein, den man mit Augen trinkt, Gießt Nachts der Mond in Wogen nieder.
Our translationThe wine that one drinks with the eyes The moon pours down in waves at night, And a spring tide floods The silent horizon. Desires, dreadful and sweet, Swim without number through the waters! The wine that one drinks with the eyes The moon pours down in waves at night.

The first two stanzas of the opening poem in Pierrot lunaire. Hartleben’s German freely reworks Giraud. The repeated opening lines belong to the poem’s rondel construction; Schoenberg varies their musical setting. Verbal recurrence and musical variation are deliberately separated.

Schoenberg
Passed toAlban BergAnton WebernPierre BoulezJohn CageHanns Eisler

Berg and Webern developed incompatible possibilities within the same teaching: Berg retained dramatic quotation and tonal implication, while Webern compressed rows into symmetrical cells. Cage’s study with Schoenberg concerned musical construction. After 1945, Boulez adopted serial organisation while criticising the older forms in which Schoenberg had placed it.
