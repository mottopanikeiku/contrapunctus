38.1Life

Elgar learned the orchestra from inside it. His father William kept a music shop in Worcester, tuned pianos and played the organ at St George’s Catholic Church. Edward read the scores in the shop, played violin in local ensembles, taught several instruments and eventually succeeded his father at the organ. He was born in Lower Broadheath in 1857, a Catholic in a predominantly Anglican musical establishment and a tradesman’s son in a profession increasingly organised around London colleges. He remained sensitive to both distinctions throughout his later success. His practical education taught him how a phrase feels under a bow.

PowickFrom 1879 to 1884 Elgar directed the staff band at Worcester County Asylum. Its surviving dance arrangements show him adapting to the players and instruments actually available.

At Powick he wrote quadrilles and polkas for an unusual ensemble, learning to give each instrument useful work. The Three Choirs Festival brought larger repertory within reach: Handel, Mendelssohn and contemporary oratorio, alongside orchestral music from the Continent. Visits to London widened that education. Wagner mattered particularly: the chromatic connection between chords, the orchestral recollection of earlier events and the long postponement of a cadence all entered Elgar’s language. His mature scores acquired part of their English character through their subsequent public use.

MalvernA composer among friends

In 1889 he married Caroline Alice Roberts, a writer and former pupil nine years his senior. Her family’s objections were social as well as religious. An attempt to establish themselves near London failed financially; they returned to Worcestershire in 1891 and settled in Malvern Link. Alice kept diaries, copied music and organised the conditions for composition. Their daughter Carice was born in 1890. The Serenade for Strings of 1892 preserves the chamber musician’s instinct for pliable inner parts, while The Black Knight, King Olaf and Caractacus established him with the provincial choral festivals. None yet secured a dependable national career.

The Enigma Variations changed that in 1899, when Hans Richter conducted the premiere at St James’s Hall. Fourteen variations turn an opening theme into incidents and conversations associated with Alice, fellow musicians and other friends. The viola’s string-crossing exercise in “Ysobel” and the clarinet’s quotation from Mendelssohn in the thirteenth variation are as specific as entries in a diary. “Nimrod” remembers August Jaeger, Elgar’s adviser at Novello, through their discussion of Beethoven. Its ceremonial afterlife can obscure the original situation: one musician encouraging another to keep composing.

The unsolved enigmaElgar said that a larger, unplayed theme ran through the set. No proposed solution has won general acceptance; the identities of most pictured friends are a separate, largely documented matter.

1900–11Chorus, symphony, concerto

The Dream of Gerontius followed in 1900. John Henry Newman’s poem describes a dying man, his soul’s encounter with judgment and the Angel’s lowering of that soul into purgatory. Elgar dispensed with a narrator: tenor, mezzo-soprano and bass embody the participants, while the chorus changes from praying friends to demons and angels. The orchestra binds the scenes through recurring motives. Inadequate preparation damaged the Birmingham premiere under Richter; Julius Buths’s Düsseldorf performances in 1901 and 1902 helped establish the work abroad. Its specifically Catholic text also complicated reception in English cathedrals.

Public recognition now accumulated: a knighthood in 1904, international conducting engagements and a professorship at Birmingham. The first Pomp and Circumstance march, written in 1901, supplied the tune for “Land of Hope and Glory”; the song’s words came afterwards. In the First Symphony, premiered by Richter in Manchester in 1908, the A♭ major motto repeatedly encounters music far from its tonal security. The scherzo becomes the slow movement without a break: related material changes speed and character so completely that continuity must be heard beneath the surface.

The Violin Concerto of 1910, written for Fritz Kreisler, makes a similarly public form accommodate private recollection. Its finale suspends forward motion for an accompanied cadenza: the soloist returns to earlier themes while orchestral strings pluck a tremulous background. The Second Symphony, completed in 1911 and dedicated to the memory of Edward VII, begins in E♭ with an impetuous upward impulse; its finale subsides. The slow movement’s procession and the third movement’s violent repeated notes deepen the work’s unease.

BrinkwellsReduced forces

During the First World War Elgar wrote patriotic pieces, including The Spirit of England on Laurence Binyon’s poems. At Brinkwells, a cottage in Sussex, he then concentrated on three chamber works in 1918: the Violin Sonata, String Quartet and Piano Quintet. Rests, exposed lines and abrupt changes of texture give them their distinctive economy. The Cello Concerto of 1919 continues it. Its opening solo recitative returns near the end, interrupting the finale’s momentum; the slow movement is recalled before the terse final bars. Felix Salmond played the premiere with Elgar conducting, after insufficient rehearsal time had again compromised a first performance.

Elgar on recordHis electrical recordings preserve quicker basic tempos and more local flexibility than many later performances. Broad melodies unfold within a moving pulse.

Alice died in 1920. Elgar’s production diminished sharply, though he continued to conduct, record and return to unfinished projects. With the young Yehudi Menuhin he recorded the Violin Concerto in 1932, leaving an unusually direct document of composer and interpreter working together. A BBC commission prompted substantial sketches for a Third Symphony, but cancer prevented its completion. Elgar died in Worcester in 1934. Alongside the two symphonies he completed stands Anthony Payne’s much later performing elaboration of those sketches.

38.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝅘𝅥Melody & accent

Elgar often begins a melody after the downbeat. The opening of Enigma leaves a crotchet rest before its first two quavers; the metre remains firm while the phrase appears to hesitate. Sequences carry short cells across long spans, and an expressive leap is frequently answered by a falling step.

♯Harmony

Chromatic inner parts connect otherwise clear triads. A bass note can remain fixed while the chords above it change their meaning; a cadence can be delayed by a sequence or redirected into another key. The First Symphony makes the distance between its A♭ motto and the main Allegro’s D minor a structural problem.

𝄞Orchestration

The strings are written by a violinist: divided desks, flexible bowing and moving middle parts produce weight without requiring everyone to double the tune. Clarinet, bassoon and solo viola often speak individually inside the large orchestra. The Violin Concerto’s accompanied cadenza separates the solo line from the orchestral strings’ trembling pizzicato.

𝄋Recollection

A returning theme acquires a new role. The finale of the First Symphony must recover its opening motto; the Cello Concerto’s finale is interrupted by memories of the Adagio and the opening recitative. Earlier material alters the direction of the present movement.

𝆑Choral drama

In Gerontius orchestral motives do work that a narrator might otherwise perform. The demons’ quick, abrasive writing, the angels’ layered praise and the final restrained prayer require sharply different kinds of choral attack. The large climax at the soul’s glimpse of God is brief: its force depends on the preparation and immediate withdrawal.

38.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1892
    Serenade for Strings in E minorOp. 20String orchestra
    Three movements; the central Larghetto gives the lower parts melodic independence.
  2. 1898–99
    Variations on an Original Theme “Enigma”Op. 36Orchestra; optional organ in the revised finale
    Theme and fourteen variations, dedicated “to my friends pictured within”. The finale recalls Alice and Jaeger as well as the opening theme.
  3. 1899
    Sea PicturesOp. 37Contralto and orchestra
    Five songs, including a setting of Alice Elgar’s “In Haven”. “The Swimmer” requires an unusually wide vocal range and an orchestral conclusion of symphonic weight.
  4. 1900
    The Dream of GerontiusOp. 38Tenor, mezzo-soprano, bass, chorus and orchestra
    Newman’s Catholic poem becomes a continuous drama in two parts, with recurring motives connecting earthly death to judgment and purgatory.
  5. 1904–05
    Introduction and AllegroOp. 47Solo string quartet and string orchestra
    A modern concerto grosso: the quartet separates from and rejoins the larger body; a fugue converts string virtuosity into contrapuntal argument.
  6. 1907–08
    Symphony No. 1 in A♭ majorOp. 55Orchestra
    The opening motto frames a four-movement design. The scherzo and Adagio are connected without a pause and transform closely related melodic material.
  7. 1909–10
    Violin Concerto in B minorOp. 61Violin and orchestra
    Dedicated to Fritz Kreisler. The finale’s accompanied cadenza revisits earlier themes over a delicate orchestral pizzicato.
  8. 1909–11
    Symphony No. 2 in E♭ majorOp. 63Orchestra
    After the energetic opening, C minor funeral procession and hammering scherzo, the finale withdraws into quiet.
  9. 1918
    Piano Quintet in A minorOp. 84Piano and string quartet
    An unsettled introduction returns in a large three-movement design; the piano often shares the argument with the strings.
  10. 1918–19
    Cello Concerto in E minorOp. 85Cello and orchestra
    Four compact movements. Solo recitative, a fleet scherzo and a short Adagio leave the finale vulnerable to interruption by its own memories.

38.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

The Malvern ridge belongs to Elgar’s working geography: he lived at Malvern Link during the years leading to Enigma, walked in the hills and later cycled through Worcestershire. Barker’s painting shares that terrain, with no documented connection to a particular composition. Its cultivated foreground recalls the inhabited countryside on which Elgar’s musical life depended: nearby households, churches, schools and amateur players.

John Henry Newman

The Dream of Gerontius — the Angel’s farewell

1865
EnglishSoftly and gently, dearly-ransomed soul, In my most loving arms I now enfold thee, And, o’er the penal waters, as they roll, I poise thee, and I lower thee, and hold thee. And carefully I dip thee in the lake, And thou, without a sob or a resistance, Dost through the flood thy rapid passage take, Sinking deep, deeper, into the dim distance.

These are the opening two stanzas of the Angel’s final song: the water is purgatory, and the separation is temporary. Elgar sets the farewell in D major, with the mezzo-soprano’s line supported by a restrained orchestral texture. The poem’s repeated actions — holding, lowering, releasing — give the final scene its measured pace. Newman’s text supplies the theological detail that concert extracts often lose.

38.8Lineage

Drew fromLudwig van BeethovenFelix MendelssohnRobert SchumannRichard WagnerWorcester and the Three Choirs Festival
Elgar
Passed toWilliam WaltonBenjamin BrittenMichael Tippett

Elgar absorbed Continental symphonic technique through performance and study. Walton inherited the mixture of ceremonial brass and unsettled lyricism; Britten admired Elgar’s orchestral craft while developing his own treatment of English words. The composer’s recordings remain an additional line of transmission, preserving tempo relationships that printed markings alone cannot settle.
