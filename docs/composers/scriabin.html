Born 6 January 1872, MoscowDied 27 April 1915, Moscow

ILife

Scriabin was born in Moscow into a family whose musical and military occupations coexisted. His mother, Lyubov, a trained pianist, died while he was an infant; his father’s diplomatic career left much of his upbringing to female relatives. He attended a cadet school while pursuing music, then entered the Moscow Conservatory in 1888. Rachmaninoff was a fellow student. Scriabin studied piano with Vasily Safonov and theoretical subjects with Sergei Taneyev and Anton Arensky, graduating as a pianist in 1892 rather than completing the full composition course. His earliest published music identifies the piano as both instrument and compositional laboratory.

Chopin supplied recognisable genres: prelude, étude, mazurka, nocturne and impromptu. Scriabin’s Twenty-four Preludes, Op. 11, traverse all major and minor keys in the order familiar from Chopin’s Op. 28. The influence extends to melody floating over an independently patterned accompaniment and to chromatic notes that delay a cadence. An injury to his right hand during his student years made the distribution of work between the hands a practical concern. The Prelude and Nocturne for the left hand, Op. 9, create melody, bass and harmonic filling within the reach of one hand rather than sounding like deliberately incomplete piano textures.

The pianist in publicBelyayev, teaching and marriage

The publisher and patron Mitrofan Belyayev supported his career and helped bring his music into print and performance. Scriabin’s own playing, preserved on piano rolls, became an important part of his reputation, though rolls cannot preserve every aspect of touch or balance. He married the pianist Vera Isakovich in 1897 and became a piano professor at the Moscow Conservatory in 1898. Teaching provided income but competed with composition and touring. The F♯ minor Piano Concerto, written in 1896, still treats the orchestra and soloist within a recognisable nineteenth-century concerto design; its central movement is a set of variations.

The first three symphonies expanded his ambitions beyond that instrumental model. The First ends with soloists and chorus in praise of art; the Third, The Divine Poem, joins its movements into a sustained progression through conflict, pleasure and assertion. At the piano, the Fourth Sonata of 1903 compresses a comparable trajectory into two linked movements. The slow opening establishes a distant goal in F♯ major; the fast movement repeatedly gathers motion until the earlier material returns at much greater intensity. Register, texture and speed make the relationship audible even without reading the accompanying poetic programme.

AbroadFrom dominant chords to harmonic fields

Scriabin left regular teaching in 1903 and moved abroad in 1904, living in Switzerland and subsequently elsewhere in Europe. His relationship with Tatiana Schloezer led to separation from Vera; he and Tatiana formed a household, although his first marriage was not dissolved. His reading widened to philosophy and Theosophy, and his conversations increasingly attached cosmological meanings to musical structures. Those interests should not replace analysis of the structures themselves. A chord with a raised fourth, added sixth and minor seventh has audible interval relationships whether or not the listener accepts a theory of spiritual transformation.

The “mystic chord”The familiar form is C–F♯–B♭–E–A–D. The intervals are not six identical fourths: the stack includes augmented and diminished fourths. Scriabin also changes the register and order of these notes.

In the middle-period music, dominant-like chords can remain active for long stretches while their expected tonic resolution is postponed. In later works, related sonorities become relatively stable fields from which both melody and accompaniment are drawn. This is not a single overnight replacement of tonality by one chord. The Fifth Sonata of 1907 retains a key signature while sharply contrasting fragmentary, suspended passages with eruptive motion. The later sonatas abandon key signatures, yet continue to distinguish arrival, preparation and return through register, recurrent motives and the density of figuration.

The Poem of Ecstasy, composed in 1905–08 and first performed in New York under Modest Altschuler in 1908, retains an especially conspicuous role for a solo trumpet. Short ideas accumulate, overlap and return at increased pressure until the final C major assertion. The harmonic language is highly chromatic, but the ending still makes a conventional triad an event. In Prometheus: The Poem of Fire, completed in 1910, the relationship between a recurring six-note collection and its transpositions becomes more systematic. Piano, orchestra, organ and an optional chorus participate in the work; the score also includes a separate part for light.

PrometheusA score for seeing

Colour is not a diagnosisScriabin’s colour correspondences are documented, but they do not establish involuntary neurological synaesthesia. Sabaneev described a system partly constructed through analogy with the circle of fifths.

The clavier à lumières does not produce sound. Its notated pitches specify projected colours: C is associated with red, D with yellow, and F♯ with blue-violet in the surviving accounts. Two lines move at different rates, one following local harmonic changes and the other much longer divisions. The Moscow premiere in 1911, with Scriabin at the piano and Koussevitzky conducting, omitted the lights because the apparatus was inadequate. Scriabin later annotated a score with further instructions for colour and intensity. A modern light show following its own preferences is therefore not necessarily a performance of his light part.

Jean Delville’s design for the published score places Prometheus within a specific Symbolist and Theosophical visual culture. Scriabin’s larger projected Mysterium would have combined music, language, movement, light and other sensory experiences in a collective rite. He developed an introductory project, the Prefatory Action, leaving a text and musical sketches rather than a completed performing score. These plans were not standard opera librettos waiting to be orchestrated. They proposed a changed relation between participants and artwork, with philosophical claims considerably larger than anything the surviving concert works can demonstrate.

The late pianoTrills, compression and interruption

Back in Russia, he continued composing and performing. The Ninth and Tenth Sonatas, completed in 1913, concentrate on short figures that change their register, intervallic setting and rate of recurrence. Trills can become a structural texture rather than an ornament placed on a finished tune. Vers la flamme, written in 1914, begins with widely spaced, relatively static sonorities and progressively replaces their separation with tremulous activity. An infected lesion on his lip led to fatal septicaemia in April 1915. He left no completed Mysterium; the late sonatas and short piano pieces remain the most compact evidence of how precisely he could organise acceleration without relying on ordinary tonal cadences.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♯Harmony

Early dominant tensions gradually become stable collections. The six-note “mystic” sonority can furnish both a vertical chord and the notes of a horizontal phrase. It is especially important in Prometheus, but does not explain every late work: octatonic collections and other pitch relations also matter.

𝅘𝅥Rhythm

Different subdivisions can occupy the same beat in the two hands, loosening the connection between melody and accompaniment without losing an underlying pulse. Recurrent figures then accelerate or accumulate. A climax may result from the increasing frequency of an event rather than from a new theme or a traditional dominant pedal.

𝄞Piano texture

Widely spaced bass notes, a middle-register harmonic layer and a high melodic fragment can behave as separate planes. Pedal connects them acoustically while the notation preserves their different rhythms. In the late sonatas, trills and tremolos increasingly replace sustained lines, making resonance and attack part of the formal argument.

𝄋Form

The early sonatas use several movements; the Fifth through Tenth are single-movement designs. Sonata procedures remain recognisable through contrast, development and return, but a recapitulation need not mean a conventional restoration of the tonic. Recurring texture, register and harmonic collection can mark the return instead.

𝄐Orchestra & light

The Poem of Ecstasy sets a projecting trumpet line against a large orchestra. Prometheus adds a piano and a notated visual part whose local changes can clarify transpositions that are difficult to hear. The light part is therefore a separate organised layer, not a request for an unspecified wash of colour.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1888–96
    Twenty-four PreludesOp. 11Piano
    All twenty-four major and minor keys in a Chopin-derived ordering; short pieces already make rhythm and voice-leading disturb their familiar genres.
  2. 1892–97
    Piano Sonata No. 2 in G♯ minor “Sonata-Fantasy”Op. 19Piano
    Two movements: a sustained Andante and a fast, continuously active Presto. The contrast depends on figuration and register as much as on tempo.
  3. 1896
    Piano Concerto in F♯ minorOp. 20Piano and orchestra
    Three movements, with a central variation set. It provides a useful point of comparison with the later orchestral works in which the piano is no longer a conventional concerto protagonist.
  4. 1903
    Piano Sonata No. 4 in F♯ majorOp. 30Piano
    An Andante followed without a break by Prestissimo volando; earlier material returns transformed by speed and dynamic force.
  5. 1905–08
    The Poem of EcstasyOp. 54Large orchestra with organ
    A single movement with a prominent trumpet idea and a final C major culmination; premiered in New York in 1908.
  6. 1907
    Piano Sonata No. 5Op. 53Piano
    One movement, sharply alternating suspension and propulsion. Its printed poetic epigraph connects it with the imaginative world of The Poem of Ecstasy.
  7. 1909–10
    Prometheus: The Poem of FireOp. 60Piano, large orchestra, organ, optional chorus and colour keyboard
    Premiered in Moscow in 1911 without the intended lights. The luce staff contains a locally changing line and a much slower layer.
  8. 1912–13
    Piano Sonata No. 9Op. 68Piano
    The nickname “Black Mass” was not supplied by the composer. Short motives and a gathering march-like motion transform the initial material without a key signature.
  9. 1912–13
    Piano Sonata No. 10Op. 70Piano
    Trills multiply until ornament becomes an organising texture. The single movement repeatedly changes the scale and density of its figuration.
  10. 1914
    Vers la flamme (Towards the Flame)Op. 72Piano
    A compact study in increasing activity: separated sonorities become repeated attacks and tremulous motion, with no need for a succession of contrasting tunes.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Delville’s Prometheus belongs to the Belgian painter’s Symbolist and Theosophical interpretation of the myth: fire becomes an emblem of intellectual and spiritual illumination. Scriabin knew Delville, who designed the published score of Prometheus. The connection is therefore a shared intellectual circle and a documented collaboration, not a claim that the composition illustrates every detail of this earlier painting.

The printed design places the score inside the same esoteric visual culture as Delville’s painting. It should not be confused with a chart of the clavier à lumières: the colour correspondences are separate documentary evidence. Scriabin’s system orders hues partly through the circle of fifths, while the score’s two light lines distinguish local harmonic change from larger formal spans. Gawboy and Townsend’s study compares Sabaneev’s 1911 table with Scriabin’s own 1913 annotations.

Scriabin
Passed toNikolai RoslavetsSamuil FeinbergOlivier MessiaenSergei Prokofiev

Scriabin’s late harmony offered Russian modernists a route beyond functional tonality different from Schoenberg’s. Roslavets developed his own synthetic-chord procedures rather than simply repeating the six-note “mystic” collection. Messiaen’s interest in colour and special pitch collections invites comparison, but his modes and theological framework are not continuations of Scriabin’s system.
