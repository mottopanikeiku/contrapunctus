Life

At the Moscow première of Prometheus in 1911, Scriabin sat at the piano and Serge Koussevitzky conducted. The audience heard the large orchestra, the solo piano and the sustained harmonies that precede the first horn melody. One part of the score was not performed: the keyboard intended to project coloured light. Alexander Mozer had built a small apparatus for Scriabin, but because of technical difficulties Scriabin withdrew it from the concert, and the première went ahead without the lights.

1872–92Moscow Conservatory and the Op. 11 Preludes

He was born in Moscow in 1872. His mother, Lyubov, was a trained pianist; she died when he was a baby. His father was pursuing a diplomatic career abroad, and his aunt, also Lyubov, helped bring him up. He entered the cadet corps in 1882, took lessons from Nikolai Zverev, and joined Vasily Safonov’s piano class at the Moscow Conservatory in 1888. Rachmaninoff studied there too. Scriabin graduated as a pianist in 1892, a year after Rachmaninoff, but left without completing the composition course with Sergei Taneyev and Anton Arensky.

He had small hands and practised hard. While he was still a student, an injury to his right hand threatened his career. The First Sonata ends with a funeral march, which he later connected with the despair of that time. The Prelude and Nocturne for the left hand, Op. 9, were a practical response to the injury. In them the left hand plays the bass, the accompaniment and the melody.

Twenty-four keysThe Op. 11 Preludes follow Chopin’s order, each major key followed by its relative minor, then onwards around the circle of fifths. Scriabin divided his set into four groups of six.

The young man’s music drew heavily on Chopin, in the titles of his mazurkas and nocturnes, in melodies set above busy accompaniments and in harmonies that delay the last chord. The Twenty-four Preludes, Op. 11, carry dates and place-names from his travels. One was written in Amsterdam, another in Dresden, several beside Lake Lucerne. The left hand is often as active as the right; in No. 19 it starts each new accompanying figure just before the beat.

1894–1903Belyayev and the Moscow Conservatory

The wealthy publisher Mitrofan Belyayev took him up in the 1890s, paying for new compositions and helping arrange concerts. Scriabin married the pianist Vera Isakovich in 1897. The following year he began teaching at the Moscow Conservatory. He needed the income, though lessons and touring took hours he wanted for composing. His Piano Concerto, written in 1896–97, has as its central movement a set of variations on a quiet theme for muted strings, which the piano decorates.

His ambitions were growing. The First Symphony ends with soloists and chorus praising art. The Third, The Divine Poem, has three connected movements that pass through struggle, pleasure and release. Among the piano works, the Fourth Sonata of 1903 sets a slow Andante beside a movement marked Prestissimo volando (flying, as fast as possible). Scriabin imagined a star drawing closer until it could be possessed. Near the end the opening music returns at full strength across the whole keyboard.

1904–08The Fifth Sonata and The Poem of Ecstasy

In 1904 he moved to Switzerland. His relationship with Tatiana Schloezer broke up his household with Vera, who refused a divorce. He and Tatiana lived together abroad, and for a time settled in Brussels. Philosophy and Theosophy entered his conversation and notebooks. “I am, and there is nothing outside me,” he wrote in 1904, and a few lines later, “I am god”.

The Fifth Sonata of 1907 is in a single movement. A sudden rush out of the bass breaks off into quiet, widely spaced notes, and slow, suspended phrases and fast dance passages interrupt one another. Its epigraph comes from the poem Scriabin wrote alongside The Poem of Ecstasy. That orchestral work was first heard in New York in 1908, conducted by Modest Altschuler. A solo trumpet plays repeatedly above the orchestra. Eight horns, five trumpets, bells and organ play in the final C major chord.

1909–13Prometheus and the light part

Six notesThe chord usually called “mystic” can be written C–F♯–B♭–E–A–D. It is built from unequal fourths, and Scriabin spaced its six notes either widely across the piano or close together.

In Prometheus, completed in 1910, the six-note chord and its transpositions supply much of the music’s harmony. A melody can draw on the same notes as its accompaniment. The score begins quietly, with tremolo strings and the six-note chord; it ends on a loud chord of F♯ major. In between, the piano is one solo voice among the horns, trumpet, flute and violin. A wordless chorus can join in the closing section.

The light part has two lines. One follows the changing harmony; the other moves slowly through the larger spans of the piece. C is red, D yellow, F♯ blue-violet. In 1913 Scriabin added instructions for flames, lightning and sparks to a copy of the score. Explaining his plan to the British psychologist Charles Myers in 1914, he said: “The colour underlines the tonality”; it “makes the tonality more evident”.

The Belgian painter Jean Delville designed the cover of the published score. Both Delville and Scriabin took occult ideas seriously. Scriabin’s projected Mysterium was to be a collective rite in India, combining music, poetry, dance, light and perfume, in which everyone present would participate and which was intended to transform the world. He worked on an introduction, the Prefatory Action, and left words and musical sketches for it.

1911–15Late sonatas and Vers la flamme

Scriabin’s piano rollsScriabin recorded piano rolls, including a session for Welte in 1910. They preserve his quick changes of tempo and the freedom with which he treated the rhythms of his own music.

Back in Russia, he continued giving concerts and wrote his last five sonatas. These have no key signatures, but their ideas still return, develop and collide within recognisable sonata forms. The Ninth acquired the nickname Black Mass, which he accepted. A quiet phrase becomes a march with increasingly violent accents. According to Leonid Sabaneev’s memoir, published in 1925, Scriabin called the Tenth “a whole sonata of insects”. Its trills spread across the keyboard.

Vers la flamme, written in 1914, opens with widely spaced chords. Repeated notes and tremolos gradually fill the texture, and the piece builds in intensity over a few minutes. Scriabin was still making plans when an infection on his lip developed into septicaemia. He died in Moscow on 27 April 1915, aged forty-three. Mysterium had never been rehearsed.

The Sound

♯Six-note harmony

In Prometheus, C–F♯–B♭–E–A–D and transpositions of that collection can supply both a chord and a melody. The collection returns repeatedly, over changing bass notes. Elsewhere Scriabin draws on octatonic scales. In a late sonata the harmony can stay unresolved for pages, and the strongest arrivals are made by a trill, an accent or a sudden leap.

𝅘𝅥Unequal divisions

Three notes in one hand can run against five in the other, or a melody can pause while its accompaniment continues. Scriabin notated these cross-rhythms precisely. The early Op. 11 Preludes already vary the bar: No. 16 alternates 5/8 and 4/8, and in No. 19 the accompanying figures start before the beat.

𝄞Trills & tremolos

In the Tenth Sonata the trills accumulate and spread into different registers of the keyboard. In Vers la flamme, widely separated chords grow increasingly busy until the texture is filled with tremolos.

𝄋Sonata shapes

The Fourth joins a slow movement to a fast one; the Fifth through Tenth each occupy one movement. Within them, contrasting ideas are introduced, developed and brought back. Scriabin’s eruptions are carefully placed. The Fifth’s opening rush interrupts the music again later, while the Ninth takes its gentle material into a brutal march.

𝄐Orchestra & light

The trumpet in The Poem of Ecstasy plays over a very large orchestra. Prometheus has a solo piano part and adds two lines for light on a single stave. One line changes colour quickly with the chords, and a slower sequence follows the larger course of the work. At the end, chorus, organ and orchestra arrive together on F♯ major, and the light was meant to be dazzling.

Sister Arts

Delville’s fire-bearer offers light to humanity, the gift Scriabin made the subject of his orchestral Prometheus. The painter and composer knew each other in their Theosophical circle, and Delville later designed the cover of the score. Both treated Prometheus as the awakener of human consciousness.

Delville drew this cover for the published score in 1911. It shows an elaborate face, rays and geometric shapes. Inside the score, Scriabin wrote two lines for light, and in 1913 he added handwritten colour annotations.

Lineage

Scriabin
Passed toNikolai RoslavetsSamuil FeinbergOlivier MessiaenSergei Prokofiev

Scriabin took the singing style of piano writing and many of his favourite forms from Chopin. Later composers took up his harmonies: Roslavets built his own method around synthetic chords, and Feinberg extended the sonata. Prokofiev dedicated Dreams to him in 1910. Messiaen also used colour and selected scales, with a very different religious imagination.
