58.1Life

Olivier Messiaen was born in Avignon in 1908 into a literary household. His mother, Cécile Sauvage, wrote the poems of L’Âme en bourgeon while expecting him; his father, Pierre Messiaen, taught English and translated Shakespeare. During the First World War, while Pierre served in the army, Cécile took Olivier and his younger brother Alain to Grenoble. The Dauphiné mountains became a permanent geographical reference for his music. He taught himself to play the piano and read opera scores by Gluck, Mozart and Wagner. In Nantes, his teacher Jean de Gibon gave him Debussy’s Pelléas et Mélisande; its suspended movement and changing harmony offered an alternative to continuous tonal argument.

Paris, 1919–39The organ loft and the first system

He entered the Paris Conservatoire in 1919. Paul Dukas taught him composition, Maurice Emmanuel music history, and Marcel Dupré organ and improvisation. In 1931 he became titular organist at the church of La Trinité, a post he retained until his death. Its Cavaillé-Coll instrument gave him combinations of reeds, foundations and mixtures that could be tested during liturgical improvisation. His Catholic faith supplied specific subjects: the Incarnation in La Nativité du Seigneur (1935), the risen body in Les Corps glorieux (1939), and the Ascension in a four-movement work first written for orchestra, then adapted for organ with a new third movement.

In 1932 he married Claire Delbos, a violinist and composer with whom he gave recitals. The violin Thème et variations was written for her; the nine songs of Poèmes pour Mi (1936) address marriage through Messiaen’s own texts, using his nickname for Claire. Their son Pascal was born in 1937. With André Jolivet, Daniel-Lesur and Yves Baudrier, Messiaen formed La Jeune France in 1936. The group argued for a human and expressive music. Messiaen pursued that aim through exact procedures: particular pitch collections, unequal durations and recurring themes that could retain their identity across sharply contrasted movements.

Limited transpositionA symmetrical pitch collection repeats before all twelve transpositions have been exhausted. Mode 2 has three distinct forms; mode 3 has four. These totals include the starting form.

The two-volume Technique de mon langage musical, published in 1944, codified devices already present in the pre-war scores. His second mode alternates semitones and tones; the third repeats a tone followed by two semitones. Both allow harmony to move independently of the dominant–tonic resolutions of a major or minor key. Rhythm could be organised through added short values, unequal augmentation or a pattern identical forwards and backwards. Greek metres and a table of Indian deçi-tâlas supplied models for compositional transformation rather than reconstruction of a living performance tradition. These procedures explain how a passage can remain precisely measured while withholding a predictable sequence of strong beats.

1940–45Captivity and occupied Paris

The premiere’s instrumentsÉtienne Pasquier explicitly disputed Messiaen’s later three-string-cello story: he played on four strings. Nigel Simeone’s account reproduces his testimony; Rebecca Rischin compares the surviving witnesses.

Mobilised during the Second World War, Messiaen was captured in 1940 and taken to Stalag VIII-A near Görlitz, then in Germany, on a site now in Poland. There he worked with clarinettist Henri Akoka, violinist Jean Le Boulaire and cellist Étienne Pasquier. These musicians, with Messiaen at the piano, gave the first performance of Quatuor pour la fin du temps on 15 January 1941 in the camp. The work incorporates earlier music: the cello’s slow Louange reworks Fête des belles eaux (1937), while the final violin movement draws on the organ Diptyque (1930). Captivity brought existing material into a new eight-movement design.

The title refers to the angel of Revelation 10. Messiaen interpreted the angel’s announcement as an abolition of time and represented eternity through several different temporal experiences. In the opening Liturgie de cristal, repeating piano patterns of different lengths circulate beneath violin and clarinet birdsong. The unaccompanied clarinet in Abîme des oiseaux alternates sustained notes with rapid calls. In Danse de la fureur, pour les sept trompettes, all four instruments play together in unison or octaves, so unequal durations become the principal event. The two Louanges instead stretch a string melody over repeated piano chords.

Released in 1941, Messiaen returned to occupied Paris and began teaching harmony at the Conservatoire. Yvonne Loriod, then seventeen, joined his class and became the pianist whose technique changed his instrumental expectations. They premiered Visions de l’Amen for two pianos in 1943; he assigned her the more elaborate keyboard writing and kept the principal thematic statements for himself. He composed Vingt Regards sur l’Enfant-Jésus in 1944, and Loriod gave its complete premiere in March 1945. Its twenty contemplations connect through recurring themes, including the slow chords of the ‘Theme of God’. The religious texts and sensuous musical language provoked a sustained dispute in the French press.

After the LiberationLove, teaching and experiment

Claire Delbos’s progressive illness made family life increasingly difficult; she eventually required institutional care and died in 1959. During these years Messiaen wrote three works associated with the love and death of Tristan and Isolde: Harawi (1945), the Turangalîla-Symphonie (1946–48), and Cinq Rechants (1948). Turangalîla, commissioned by Serge Koussevitzky, expands to ten movements for orchestra, solo piano and ondes Martenot. Its recurring ‘statue’, ‘flower’ and love themes pass between blocks of percussion, piano cadenzas and sustained string writing. Leonard Bernstein conducted the Boston premiere on 2 December 1949, with Loriod at the piano and Ginette Martenot playing the electronic instrument.

A mode, not a rowIn Mode de valeurs et d’intensités, a particular pitch in a particular register carries fixed attributes. An independently ordered series for each parameter would produce a different system.

In 1949 Messiaen composed Mode de valeurs et d’intensités, subsequently included in the Quatre Études de rythme. It associates pitches with durations, dynamics and attacks, extending systematic organisation beyond pitch alone. Boulez and Karlheinz Stockhausen took such possibilities towards serial procedures that Messiaen did not adopt as a permanent method. His own Livre d’orgue (1951) continued rigorous rhythmic experiments beside birdsong. His classroom became a meeting point for post-war modernism, while his own music embraced both fixed associations and Turangalîla’s F♯ major love music.

Messiaen’s teaching centred on close analysis of scores, including Beethoven, Debussy and Stravinsky. Successive generations included Boulez, Stockhausen, Iannis Xenakis, Gérard Grisey, Tristan Murail and George Benjamin. He became professor of composition in 1966 and retired from the Conservatoire in 1978. In 1961 he married Loriod, whose work extended beyond performance to checking scores, assisting on birdsong expeditions and preparing his writings for publication. Their partnership joins the large piano cycles to the later orchestral works: Loriod’s ability to differentiate registers, attacks and simultaneous rhythms made the keyboard an especially detailed medium for his transcriptions.

Birds and colourNotebooks, recordings and stained glass

Birdsong became a dominant source during the 1950s. The ornithologist Jacques Delamain helped Messiaen identify species by ear, while field notebooks and commercial recordings supplied material from different regions. Réveil des oiseaux (1953) follows a woodland night and morning; Oiseaux exotiques (1955–56) brings together species that could not share one habitat. The thirteen pieces of Catalogue d’oiseaux (1956–58) place their principal birds within French landscapes, with neighbouring species and changes of light. Messiaen brings high frequencies and rapid calls within instrumental range, adding harmonies to represent aspects of timbre beyond the reach of equal-tempered single notes.

Messiaen described private associations of colour with particular harmonies, experienced inwardly when he heard or read them. Stained glass provided a public counterpart. The Sainte-Chapelle, visited in childhood, also housed the first performance of Et exspecto resurrectionem mortuorum in May 1965. André Malraux had commissioned this memorial to the dead of the two world wars. Its winds, brass and metallic percussion dispense with strings and voices. La Transfiguration de Notre Seigneur Jésus-Christ (1965–69) restores both, adding a large chorus and seven instrumental soloists to set Latin biblical, liturgical and theological texts.

1971–92Canyons, Francis and the beyond

A commission from Alice Tully for the American bicentenary led to Des canyons aux étoiles… (1971–74) and a research visit to Utah in 1972. Bryce Canyon, Cedar Breaks and Zion supplied named landscapes; birds from outside Utah also enter the score. Piano, horn, xylorimba and glockenspiel have solo roles within an unusually proportioned ensemble. The unaccompanied horn movement, Appel interstellaire, uses calls, echoes and interrupted phrases without orchestral support. Elsewhere percussion includes a wind machine and a geophone, whose moving grains produce a granular noise. Landscape remains inseparable from theology: the final movement connects Zion Park with the heavenly city.

His only opera, Saint François d’Assise, occupied him from 1975 to 1983. Messiaen wrote the libretto himself and organised eight tableaux in three acts around the saint’s spiritual development. The healing of the leper, the angel’s music, the sermon to the birds and the stigmata require different musical timescales. Three ondes Martenot join the large orchestra, and individual birds have elaborately differentiated instrumental colours. Seiji Ozawa conducted the Paris Opéra premiere in November 1983, with José van Dam as Francis and Christiane Eda-Pierre as the Angel. Messiaen had turned the materials of his organ loft and notebooks into staged action.

He continued composing after the opera: Livre du Saint Sacrement (1984) added eighteen movements to his organ repertory, and Un vitrail et des oiseaux (1986) explicitly reunited glass and birds. The New York Philharmonic commissioned Éclairs sur l’Au-Delà… for its 150th anniversary. Completed in 1991, its eleven orchestral movements include the Australian superb lyrebird, massed birdsong and extended writing for strings. Messiaen died in Clichy on 27 April 1992, before Zubin Mehta conducted the premiere that November. Loriod subsequently edited the seven volumes of his Traité de rythme, de couleur, et d’ornithologie, preserving detailed analyses and the working distinctions behind the published scores.

58.2The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Modes & harmony

The octatonic second mode contains eight pitch classes; mode 3 contains nine. Their internal symmetries limit how many distinct transpositions they possess while allowing a varied chord vocabulary. Messiaen can extract triads, superimpose modal forms or move to specially constructed resonant chords. A recognisable major chord can stand free of dominant–tonic motion.

Listen for the difference between harmonic colour and harmonic direction: successive chords can change their internal spacing and register without announcing a modulation.

𝅘𝅥Rhythm

An added value extends a pattern by a short note, rest or dot. Non-retrogradable rhythms are palindromes of duration, such as 3–2–1–2–3: reversal produces no new ordering. Augmentation can enlarge some values while leaving others intact. Greek metres and Indian rhythmic tables offered patterns independent of the regular bar.

In the Quartet’s sixth movement, instrumental unisons expose the changing lengths directly. The notation is exact even when a listener cannot maintain a repeated two-, three- or four-beat accent pattern.

𝄞Birdsong

A bird transcription must accommodate a different frequency range, speed and timbre from an orchestral instrument. Messiaen slows, transposes and harmonises material while identifying species and often the place and hour. In Catalogue d’oiseaux, a title bird is only one participant: other birds, rocks, water and light receive distinct musical materials.

The solo piano can juxtapose a high repeated call, a low landscape chord and an intervening silence.

𝆑Instrumental colour

The organ supplies sustained colour through registration; the piano supplies sharply differentiated attacks and resonance after the attack. Turangalîla combines piano with celesta, keyboard glockenspiel, vibraphone and percussion, while the ondes Martenot can sustain a line and slide continuously between pitches. Et exspecto uses winds, brass and metal percussion without string cushioning.

Exact choices of register, doubling, attack and decay make these colours audible.

𝄋Form & recurrence

Movements often alternate recognisable blocks instead of developing a theme continuously. Recurring themes bind the twenty Regards and the ten movements of Turangalîla; a return may preserve identity while changing duration or orchestration. In the Quartet’s opening, ostinatos of unequal lengths postpone the recurrence of the whole combination.

At larger scales, cycles of contemplations, successive habitats and operatic tableaux replace the expectation that one tonal conflict must organise the entire work.

𝄐Faith & time

Scriptural subjects affect musical construction. The Quartet places extremely slow string cantilenas beside compressed rhythmic dances; La Transfiguration alternates narrative and meditation. These are different ways of measuring, suspending or accumulating events.

Messiaen’s titles specify the Incarnation, Resurrection, Trinity or heavenly Jerusalem. Plainchant, birds and modern harmonic complexes can inhabit the same work because his Catholic interpretation treats nature and revelation as related sources.

58.3Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1932–34
    L’Ascension (The Ascension)Orchestra, 1932–33; organ version, 1933–34
    Four meditations. The organ version replaces the orchestral third movement with Transports de joie, a newly composed toccata; the slow final prayer survives in both versions.
  2. 1935
    La Nativité du Seigneur (The Nativity of the Lord)Organ
    Nine meditations on the Nativity. Modal harmony, added rhythmic values and sharply contrasted registrations lead to the closing Dieu parmi nous, whose toccata depends on an active pedal part.
  3. 1940–41
    Quatuor pour la fin du temps (Quartet for the End of Time)Clarinet, violin, cello and piano
    Eight movements inspired by Revelation 10, premiered at Stalag VIII-A on 15 January 1941. The changing combinations include solo clarinet, a trio without piano, two string-and-piano duos and full-ensemble unisons.
  4. 1944
    Vingt Regards sur l’Enfant-Jésus (Twenty Contemplations of the Infant Jesus)Piano
    Written for Yvonne Loriod. Recurring themes connect twenty movements whose techniques include fugue, rhythmic canons, slow chordal meditation and rapidly alternating registers.
  5. 1946–48
    Turangalîla-SymphonieSolo piano, ondes Martenot and large orchestra
    Ten movements organised around recurring themes and contrasting rhythmic strata. The sixth, Jardin du sommeil d’amour, places piano birdsong over sustained love music; the fifth is the violent Joie du sang des étoiles.
  6. 1955–56
    Oiseaux exotiques (Exotic Birds)Piano, winds, brass and percussion
    Birds from Asia and the Americas meet in an ensemble without strings. Piano cadenzas alternate with ensemble passages; percussion carries Greek and Indian rhythmic patterns independently of the birdsong.
  7. 1956–58
    Catalogue d’oiseaux (Catalogue of Birds)Piano
    Thirteen pieces in seven books, dedicated to Loriod. Each principal bird belongs to a described French habitat; the central La Rousserolle effarvatte expands the reed warbler’s surroundings into a long sequence of changing light and activity.
  8. 1964
    Et exspecto resurrectionem mortuorum (And I await the resurrection of the dead)Woodwind, brass and metallic percussion
    Five movements commissioned by André Malraux to commemorate the dead of both world wars. Biblical inscriptions govern the sequence; deep wind sonorities, bells, gongs and tam-tams require space for their resonance.
  9. 1965–69
    La Transfiguration de Notre Seigneur Jésus-ChristMixed chorus, seven instrumental soloists and large orchestra
    Fourteen movements in two groups of seven. Latin Gospel narrative alternates with commentary; the solo group comprises piano, cello, flute, clarinet, xylorimba, vibraphone and marimba.
  10. 1971–74
    Des canyons aux étoiles… (From the Canyons to the Stars…)Solo piano, horn, xylorimba and glockenspiel with orchestra
    Twelve movements written for Alice Tully’s American bicentenary commission. Named Utah landscapes coexist with birds from other regions, two unaccompanied piano movements and the solo horn’s Appel interstellaire.
  11. 1975–83
    Saint François d’AssiseOpera in three acts and eight tableaux; solo voices, chorus and large orchestra with three ondes Martenot
    Messiaen’s own libretto follows Francis’s spiritual transformation. The Angel’s music and the Sermon to the Birds occupy extended tableaux; the final scene sets the saint’s death and entry into light.
  12. 1988–91
    Éclairs sur l’Au-Delà… (Flashes of the Beyond…)Large orchestra
    Eleven movements commissioned for the New York Philharmonic’s 150th anniversary. The final completed orchestral cycle includes Australian birds and slow string writing; Zubin Mehta conducted its posthumous premiere on 5 November 1992.

58.5Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

The wood thrush, Hylocichla mustelina, is Messiaen’s grive des bois. It supplies the title and musical material of movement ten of Des canyons aux étoiles…. The work’s commentary describes its clear major arpeggio and lower rustling aftermath. Havell’s plate after Audubon identifies the same bird visually, beside flowering dogwood. The pairing rests on the shared species. The nineteenth-century artwork is public domain; the National Gallery’s scan is released under CC0.

Movement nine of Des canyons aux étoiles…, Le Moqueur polyglotte, gives the northern mockingbird an unaccompanied piano movement. Short calls, trills and repeated-note figures make one instrument carry a changing repertory of gestures. Audubon’s birds confront a snake at their nest. The connection is the identified species and the act of detailed observation. Like the wood-thrush plate, this public-domain engraving is available as a CC0 National Gallery scan.

John of Patmos; Latin Vulgate tradition

Revelation 10:1, 5–6 — the angel’s oath

First century CE; Clementine Vulgate edition, 1598
LatinEt vidi alium angelum fortem descendentem de cælo amictum nube, et iris in capite ejus, et facies ejus erat ut sol, et pedes ejus tamquam columnæ ignis: Et angelus, quem vidi stantem super mare et super terram, levavit manum suam ad cælum: et juravit per viventem in sæcula sæculorum, qui creavit cælum, et ea quæ in eo sunt: et terram, et ea quæ in ea sunt: et mare, et ea quæ in eo sunt: Quia tempus non erit amplius:
Our translationAnd I saw another mighty angel descending from heaven, clothed in a cloud, with a rainbow upon his head, his face like the sun, and his feet like pillars of fire. And the angel whom I saw standing upon the sea and upon the earth raised his hand to heaven and swore by the one who lives for ever and ever, who created heaven and everything in it, and earth and everything in it, and the sea and everything in it: that there would be no more time.

The angel, rainbow and oath in this biblical source supply the Quartet’s programme. Verses 2–4 are omitted, with the break shown above; the Latin follows the public-domain Clementine Vulgate. ‘No more time’ translates the wording literally and follows Messiaen’s musical reading of it; the passage is also understood as announcing no further delay before fulfilment. Slow Louanges and rhythmic palindromes make that interpretation audible without a sung text.

Messiaen visited the Sainte-Chapelle as a child and repeatedly connected its coloured light with his musical thinking. Et exspecto resurrectionem mortuorum received its first performance here on 7 May 1965 under Serge Baudo. This photograph shows the Judith and Job bay within the chapel’s medieval biblical glazing. The image is by Yair Haklai, licensed CC BY-SA 4.0. Medieval glass and later restoration coexist in the present window.

58.7Lineage

Drew fromClaude DebussyRichard WagnerIgor StravinskyPaul DukasMarcel DupréPlainchant, Greek metres and Indian rhythmic theory
Messiaen
Passed toPierre BoulezKarlheinz StockhausenIannis XenakisGérard GriseyTristan MurailGeorge Benjamin

Debussy offered harmonic colour and discontinuous pacing; Dukas taught composition, Dupré organ and improvisation, and Stravinsky provided scores for rhythmic analysis. Messiaen’s pupils pursued diverse paths: Boulez developed serial procedures, Xenakis mathematical constructions, and Grisey and Murail spectral approaches to sound. They shared a teacher who treated rhythm, register and sonority as subjects for exact analysis.
