11.1Life

Vivaldi was born in Venice, the son of a barber who became a professional violinist at St Mark’s. Giovanni Battista Vivaldi taught his son the violin and remained involved in his musical work. Antonio trained for the priesthood and was ordained in 1703, acquiring the nickname il prete rosso, the red priest, from his hair. He soon ceased regularly celebrating Mass, later explaining that a condition of tightness in the chest prevented him. He travelled extensively, taught and managed operatic productions.

The PietàAn institution with an orchestra

In 1703 the Ospedale della Pietà appointed him violin teacher. The Pietà was a charitable institution that cared for abandoned children; its celebrated performing body consisted of girls and women whose training could continue into adult professional responsibility within the institution. Concerts and services attracted Venetian patrons and foreign visitors. Vivaldi’s employment was periodically renewed and sometimes interrupted, but the connection endured for decades. The players supplied a practical laboratory: a concerto could be written for a particular instrumental skill, tried in performance, and followed by another that changed the problem.

RitornelloVivaldi can detach a returning passage’s opening gesture, cadence or middle segment, using a recognisable fragment to mark a new key.

The concertos extend far beyond solo violin. Vivaldi wrote for cello, oboe, bassoon, flute, recorder, mandolin and combinations of instruments, as well as strings without an individual soloist. A low bassoon’s long line, a pair of mandolins and a violin’s rapid string crossings call for different kinds of solo writing. The fast movements often share a method: the orchestra states a ritornello, the soloist leaves it for more mobile material, and parts of the ritornello return in different keys. Repetition establishes points of reference while sequences carry the soloist away from them.

L’estro armonico, the twelve concertos published in Amsterdam in 1711, carried this practice across Europe. The collection alternates different solo groupings. Bach transcribed several concertos for organ or harpsichord; the concerto for four violins in B minor, RV 580, became his concerto for four harpsichords, BWV 1065. Such arrangements show what travelled: formal proportions, harmonic direction and the opposition of ensemble and solo writing. Amsterdam’s music trade made the printed parts available well beyond the audiences who could hear Vivaldi in Venice.

Voice and theatreThe violinist writes for singers

His first surviving opera, Ottone in villa, was performed in Vicenza in 1713. He subsequently worked as composer and impresario, especially at Venice’s Teatro Sant’Angelo, negotiating the practical demands of casts, librettos, schedules and receipts. The da capo aria gave a singer a framed opportunity: an opening section, a contrasting middle, and a return inviting ornament. Vivaldi could make the instrumental ritornello participate in the character’s agitation. The contralto Anna Girò became an important interpreter of his operas.

The sacred music uses the same command of dramatic contrast for different purposes. The Gloria, RV 589, divides the liturgical text among chorus, soloists and changing instrumental combinations. In Et in terra pax, a minor-key choral texture displaces the opening movement’s trumpet-supported D major. Juditha triumphans, written for the Pietà in 1716, is a Latin oratorio on Judith and Holofernes, with unusually varied instrumental colours. Its political context was Venice’s war against the Ottoman Empire; its biblical women were sung by the institution’s female performers. The smaller Stabat Mater concentrates instead on a low solo voice, slow tempos and recurring minor-mode material.

1725Words inside the concerto

Who wrote the sonnets?The poems accompanied the 1725 publication anonymously. Their attribution to Vivaldi remains conjectural; text and score are closely coordinated.

The Four Seasons appeared as the first four concertos of Op. 8, Il cimento dell’armonia e dell’inventione, in 1725. Sonnets printed with them identify birds, storms, sleep, hunting and ice; letters connect passages in the poems with locations in the music. In Spring’s slow movement, the solo violin is the sleeping goatherd’s repose, the accompanying violins supply rustling foliage, and the viola repeats the dog’s bark. The parts inhabit different rhythmic layers. Bass, cadence and returning material support the scene’s musical structure.

Vivaldi worked in Mantua, Rome and other centres as well as Venice. In 1723 the Pietà arranged to receive two concertos a month from him, allowing the institution to buy music even when he travelled. He left Venice in 1740 and died in Vienna in 1741; the circumstances of his intended career there remain incompletely documented. His printed concertos continued to circulate, but much of the vocal and manuscript repertory disappeared from ordinary performance. The acquisition of large manuscript collections for Turin in the twentieth century made that repertory available for systematic study. The present catalogue therefore includes a theatre and church composer whom the popularity of four violin concertos can still conceal.

11.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄋Ritornello form

The orchestra gives the listener recognisable material before the soloist begins to move through other keys. Later returns can be shortened or redistributed. Listen for the bass and cadence as well as the opening tune: a ritornello’s arrival can establish a new tonal station without reproducing the whole opening paragraph.

♮Sequence & harmony

A short figure repeats at another pitch while the bass moves through a chain of harmonies. This makes rapid movement intelligible: the surface may be full of semiquavers, but the ear hears a repeating harmonic operation. A sudden cessation of the sequence, a pedal note or a delayed cadence can therefore become a strong formal event.

𝄞Violin technique

Arpeggios, repeated notes, rapid changes of string, high-register passagework and bariolage exploit the instrument’s physical layout. An open string can remain constant while stopped notes move around it. The solo part is often contrasted with an orchestral texture that is deliberately simpler and more rhythmically unanimous.

𝆏Slow movements

Many slow movements isolate a cantabile line above a spare, repeated accompaniment. The harmonic rhythm can slow enough for ornaments and suspensions to carry the local argument. This is also a singer’s design: breath, stress and the approach to a cadence matter more than the number of notes.

𝆑Drama & colour

The operas and sacred works change instrumental combinations to distinguish situations and texts. In Juditha triumphans, mandolin, chalumeau and other unusual colours have specific dramatic assignments. In the concertos, the title may describe an event, but the effect is made from particular bow strokes, registers, rests and repetitions.

11.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1711
    L’estro armonicoOp. 3Twelve concertos for one, two or four violins, with strings and continuo
    The Amsterdam publication that spread Vivaldi’s concerto procedures across Europe. Six concertos became the basis of Bach transcriptions.
  2. 1712
    Stabat MaterRV 621Alto solo, strings and continuo
    A compact F minor setting of the opening portion of the sequence. Music from the first movements returns later in the text, making repetition part of the lament.
  3. 1713
    Ottone in villaRV 729Opera in three acts
    First performed in Vicenza. The relatively small cast and orchestral forces expose the mechanics of the da capo aria.
  4. c. 1715
    Gloria in D majorRV 589Soloists, chorus, trumpet, oboe, strings and continuo
    Twelve movements distribute the text between public choral declaration and more intimate solo writing. The opening D major is interrupted by the B minor Et in terra pax.
  5. 1716
    Juditha triumphansRV 644Five vocal soloists, female chorus and varied instrumental ensemble
    A Latin oratorio for the Pietà, connecting the biblical Judith with Venice’s wartime circumstances. Its instrumentation extends well beyond the normal string-and-continuo group.
  6. 1725
    The Four SeasonsOp. 8 Nos. 1–4; RV 269, 315, 293, 297Solo violin, strings and continuo
    Spring in E major, Summer in G minor, Autumn in F major and Winter in F minor. Printed sonnets and lettered references make the programme unusually explicit.
  7. 1727
    Orlando furiosoRV 728Opera in three acts
    Ariosto’s characters become vehicles for jealousy, deception and madness. Sol da te, mio dolce amore joins the voice to an extended obbligato flute part.
  8. c. 1728–29
    Six Flute ConcertosOp. 10Flute, strings and continuo
    Includes La tempesta di mare, La notte and Il gardellino. Several works rework earlier versions for different instrumental combinations.
  9. 1735
    GriseldaRV 718Opera in three acts
    Carlo Goldoni adapted an earlier libretto by Apostolo Zeno. Agitata da due venti translates the image of competing winds into breath-testing vocal passagework.

11.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

The basin and its boats place Venice’s ceremonial buildings within a working system of transport and trade. Canaletto painted such views for an international market that included travellers who also heard the city’s institutional concerts. The Pietà stood on the waterfront east of the Piazza San Marco. This is Vivaldi’s urban and commercial setting; the building now called the Pietà’s church was erected after his death.

La Primavera

Published with Op. 8, 1725
ItalianGiunt’è la Primavera e festosetti La salutan gl’augei con lieto canto, E i fonti allo spirar de’ zeffiretti Con dolce mormorio scorrono intanto. Vengon’ coprendo l’aer di nero amanto E lampi, e tuoni ad annuntiarla eletti; Indi tacendo questi, gl’augelletti Tornan di nuovo al lor canoro incanto. E quindi sul fiorito, ameno prato Al caro mormorio di fronde e piante Dorme ’l caprar col fido can’ a lato. Di pastoral zampogna al suon festante Danzan ninfe e pastor nel tetto amato Di primavera all’apparir brillante.
Our translationSpring has arrived, and the little birds joyfully greet it with their cheerful song; and springs, at the breath of gentle breezes, flow meanwhile with a soft murmur. Covering the air with a black cloak, lightning and thunder come, chosen to announce it; then, when these fall silent, the little birds return once more to their enchanting song. And then upon the pleasant, flowering meadow, to the dear murmuring of leaves and plants, the goatherd sleeps, his faithful dog beside him. To the festive sound of a shepherd’s bagpipe nymphs and shepherds dance beneath their beloved roof at spring’s shining appearance.

The sonnet’s first eight lines belong to the opening Allegro, the next three to the Largo, and the final three to the pastoral dance. In the original publication, lettered references connect these events to the parts. The poem documents the programme. The Philharmonie de Paris’s account of Spring follows these correspondences.

11.8Lineage

Vivaldi
Passed toJohann Sebastian BachJohann Georg PisendelGiuseppe Tartini

Bach’s arrangements provide direct evidence of study: Vivaldi’s printed concertos became keyboard scores with changed instrumental means but recognisable formal procedures. The Dresden violinist Johann Georg Pisendel studied with Vivaldi in Venice and preserved his music in manuscript. The transmission depended on players, copyists and publishers as well as on a later history of compositional influence.
