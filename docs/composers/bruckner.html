AntonBruckner

Born 4 September 1824, Ansfelden, Upper AustriaDied 11 October 1896, Vienna

A church organist who built symphonic movements from long harmonic spans, separate instrumental blocks and the silences between them.

30.1Life

Bruckner was born in Ansfelden, near Linz, where his father was a schoolmaster and church musician. Teaching, organ playing and the parish liturgy belonged to the same local profession. After his father’s death in 1837, he became a choirboy at the Augustinian abbey of St Florian. The abbey gave him daily contact with sacred music and a large organ, but his first adult qualification was as a schoolteacher. He worked in small Upper Austrian communities before returning to St Florian in 1845. The later symphonist emerged from this practical church-and-school environment.

He became the abbey’s organist and moved to Linz Cathedral in 1855. By then he was thirty-one, yet he submitted himself to six further years of harmony and counterpoint study with Simon Sechter in Vienna, much of it conducted through written exercises and correspondence. Sechter’s discipline discouraged free composition while the technical work continued. Bruckner sought formal certification even after he had become an accomplished organ improviser. This extended apprenticeship matters when hearing a symphonic climax: its apparent mass rests on carefully managed lines, prepared dissonances and a bass that controls the rate of harmonic change.

LinzLearning an orchestra

From 1861 to 1863 he studied form and orchestration with Otto Kitzler, conductor at the Linz theatre. Kitzler’s teaching included contemporary orchestral scores and introduced him to Wagner’s music. The F minor Study Symphony, completed in 1863, belongs to that period of deliberate exercise. The D minor Mass of 1864, followed by the E minor and F minor masses, shows the technical schooling becoming an independent language. Choral imitation, blocks of wind harmony and abrupt changes in texture are already present. The First Symphony was completed in 1866 and first performed in Linz in 1868.

Instrumental choirsBruckner’s separate instrumental choirs can suggest changes of organ registration. String tremolo, brass attacks and breathing wind phrases each have their own behaviour, distinct from sustained organ pipes, and he composes for those differences.

His development was interrupted by a serious psychological crisis in 1867, for which he received treatment at Bad Kreuzen. Accounts of compulsive counting describe his distress; later caricatures of rustic simplicity have obscured it. In 1868 he succeeded Sechter as a teacher at the Vienna Conservatory. Vienna exposed him to larger institutions and stronger criticism, but his credentials as an organist were already international: performances in France in 1869 and London in 1871 won admiration for improvisations of which little survives in notation.

ViennaSymphonies without a party platform

Bruckner’s admiration for Wagner made him a convenient target in the disputes surrounding Brahms and the critic Eduard Hanslick. He dedicated the Third Symphony to Wagner in 1873, and its first version contains recognisable Wagner quotations. The symphonies organise their returns through tonal areas, thematic groups, rhythmic patterns and the relation of a finale to preceding movements. Catholic devotion and theatrical harmony coexist within these instrumental designs.

The Third’s Vienna premiere in 1877 was a failure. Bruckner conducted after the death of its intended conductor Johann Herbeck; much of the audience left, and the performance became part of the story of an incomprehensible provincial in the capital. The young Mahler was among his supporters and helped prepare a piano-duet arrangement. Bruckner continued to revise the work, producing versions with different proportions and details. Similar processes reshaped the Fourth and Eighth. Some revisions answered practical criticism; others reveal changes in his own ideas about pacing and orchestration.

The Fifth, completed in its first form in 1876, displays this care for construction especially clearly. Its finale recalls earlier material, introduces a fugue and eventually combines contrapuntal argument with a brass chorale. The listener needs to retain several kinds of theme across a long span before the concluding combination can register. Bruckner never heard the symphony performed by an orchestra: the 1894 premiere in Graz, conducted by Franz Schalk, used extensive cuts and alterations, and illness prevented him from attending. The score’s original orchestral argument reached listeners only after his death.

RecognitionThe Seventh and the Eighth

The Seventh Symphony brought a decisive success in Leipzig in 1884 under Arthur Nikisch. Its opening cello melody extends over a quiet string tremolo. The Adagio includes four Wagner tubas, instruments whose blend connects horn and low-brass sonorities; Bruckner completed its mourning music around the time of Wagner’s death in 1883. The symphony’s broad melody made an entry point into a language whose large forms had previously seemed forbidding. Recognition arrived when he was sixty, after decades as a teacher, organist and composer.

He remained vulnerable to criticism. Hermann Levi, an important supporter, found the first version of the Eighth difficult to accept in 1887. Bruckner revised it extensively, completing the new version in 1890. The changes include the first movement’s ending: the earlier loud conclusion becomes a quiet one. The revised symphony was successfully premiered in Vienna under Hans Richter in 1892. Its finale brings material associated with different movements into a common C major field, making the entire cycle participate in the ending.

Version and editionThe Eighth exists in 1887 and 1890 authorial versions. Robert Haas’s twentieth-century edition combines the later text with passages from the earlier one; Leopold Nowak published the versions separately. An edition credit can therefore identify a substantive musical choice.

The Ninth occupied Bruckner from 1887 until his death. He completed three movements by 1894, including an Adagio that passes through violent dissonance before ending in E major. He then continued to work on a finale, leaving extensive manuscript material. Modern performing completions have to make editorial decisions about missing or incomplete stretches. His reported suggestion of using the C major Te Deum if necessary offers a separate possibility for concluding the D minor symphony.

He died in Vienna in 1896 and was buried in the crypt at St Florian beneath the organ. The estate and successive editions made his posthumous reputation unusually dependent on editorial mediation: cuts, retouchings and competing versions altered what audiences thought “Bruckner” sounded like. A performance can now specify its text and let the listener follow its actual proportions. The decisive events are often small within the large span: a bass changes after a long pedal, a brass chord arrives where strings had been alone, or a silence makes the next entry sound newly separated from what came before.

30.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Harmonic pacing

A bass pedal or repeated harmony can last while the upper voices grow more active. When the bass finally moves, its change carries structural weight. Bruckner also places distant key areas beside one another with little connective tissue; the juxtaposition can be as important as a smoothly prepared modulation.

𝄞Instrumental blocks

Strings, woodwind and brass frequently act as separate choirs. A string tremolo creates a low-definition background; a horn call supplies a contour; a brass chorale makes the harmony explicit. The Seventh’s Wagner tubas add their particular blend to these colours.

𝅘𝅥Two and three

The characteristic “Bruckner rhythm” joins a group of two equal notes to a group of three within the same span. This alternating duplet–triplet motion gives a theme an uneven internal gait without changing the underlying metre. Repetition can make it a movement’s frame of reference, against which lyrical phrases move more freely.

𝄋Sonata groups

Many first movements organise three substantial thematic groups. A lyrical second group often contains simultaneous contrasting rhythms; a third may restore a firmer tread. Repeated blocks and large crescendos clarify the returns, but their meaning depends on the changed key, bass or orchestration.

𝄢Counterpoint and culmination

The Fifth’s finale makes fugue and chorale cooperate. In the Eighth’s coda, material from different movements is combined. Such endings require motifs to have been designed for compatibility: harmony and counterpoint govern the final accumulation.

𝄾Silence and sacred text

Pauses separate instrumental blocks and let a cadence occupy the room before the next entry. The motets show the same attention to delivery on a smaller scale: in Locus iste, homophonic phrases establish the text before chromatic movement unsettles its middle section. Clear consonants and moving inner parts sustain this motion even at slow tempos in a resonant church.

30.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1866
    Mass No. 2 in E minorWAB 27Eight-part mixed chorus and fifteen wind instruments
    Composed for celebrations associated with Linz’s new cathedral and first performed in 1869; later revised. Choral counterpoint is supported by winds without strings, making the independence of the vocal parts particularly audible.
  2. 1868
    Mass No. 3 in F minorWAB 28Four soloists, chorus, orchestra and organ
    First performed in Vienna in 1872. Large-scale fugues and returning material connect the liturgical sections; the work stands beside the early symphonies in its own right.
  3. 1873
    Symphony No. 3 in D minorWAB 103Orchestra
    Dedicated to Wagner. The 1873, 1877 and 1889 versions differ substantially; the earliest contains Wagner quotations that were largely removed in revision. The exposed opening trumpet idea gives the movement a long-range point of reference.
  4. 1874
    Symphony No. 4 in E♭ major “Romantic”WAB 104Orchestra
    Substantially rewritten in 1878–80 and revised again later. The familiar horn call and “hunting” scherzo belong to the later conception; the first version has a different scherzo and finale.
  5. 1876
    Symphony No. 5 in B♭ majorWAB 105Orchestra
    Revised through 1878. A slow introduction establishes widely separated gestures; the finale recalls earlier movements and combines fugue subjects with a brass chorale in its concluding span.
  6. 1881
    Symphony No. 6 in A majorWAB 106Orchestra
    The first movement pits an insistent string rhythm against a broad low-string theme. The Adagio’s oboe line and funeral-march material offer contrasting kinds of sustained motion.
  7. 1883
    Symphony No. 7 in E majorWAB 107Orchestra including four Wagner tubas
    Premiered in Leipzig in 1884. The long opening melody grows over tremolo; the C♯ minor Adagio places the Wagner tubas within a carefully paced sequence of returns and climaxes.
  8. 1884
    Te Deum in C majorWAB 45Four soloists, chorus, orchestra and organ
    A concentrated large choral work. Octaves and open fifths give the opening a public, declarative profile; the final fugue sets “In te, Domine, speravi”.
  9. 1887; revised 1890
    Symphony No. 8 in C minorWAB 108Large orchestra including Wagner tubas and harps
    The revised version was premiered in Vienna in 1892. The slow movement’s harp colour is exceptional within Bruckner’s symphonies; the finale gathers material from all four movements into its closing C major.
  10. 1887–96
    Symphony No. 9 in D minor (unfinished)WAB 109Orchestra including Wagner tubas
    Three movements completed by 1894, with extensive material for a fourth. The violent scherzo and the Adagio’s dissonant climax belong to a work conceived with a further finale.

30.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

The abbey where Bruckner became a choirboy in 1837, later worked as teacher and organist, and was buried in 1896. Vischer’s engraving records the institution and its setting before the Baroque rebuilding. Behind the familiar “cathedral in sound” metaphor stand the actual buildings and daily musical work that formed him.

Böhler shows Bruckner accepting snuff from Wagner. The caricature records the public image of the deferential admirer, an image encouraged by the Third Symphony’s dedication. Bruckner’s contrapuntal finales and three-group sonata expositions belong to his independent symphonic practice; the print documents his reception.

Roman Catholic liturgy

Locus iste

Traditional Latin gradual; Bruckner’s setting, 1869
LatinLocus iste a Deo factus est, inaestimabile sacramentum, irreprehensibilis est.
Our translationThis place was made by God, a mystery beyond price; it is without fault.

The text belongs to the dedication of a church. Bruckner composed his four-part unaccompanied setting, WAB 23, in connection with the dedication celebrations for the votive chapel of Linz’s new cathedral in 1869. Its C major opening is predominantly chordal; the middle phrase introduces chromatic movement before the opening returns. A few minutes of vocal writing expose the same management of stability, departure and return that the symphonies enlarge.

30.8Lineage

Bruckner
Passed toGustav MahlerHans RottFranz Schmidt

Mahler attended Bruckner’s university lectures and supported his music, but populated the inherited symphonic span with songs, marches and sharply differentiated social voices. Franz Schmidt continued the Austrian combination of symphonic form, contrapuntal craft and organ thinking. The sacred works offer a separate inheritance from the large symphonies: compact settings in which modal or chromatic detail remains intelligible through vocal part-writing.

Questions to take away

  1. How does a funeral music grow from one chorale-like theme?

    Four Wagner tubas, heard here for the first time in a symphony, sound the C♯-minor theme that Bruckner said came to him as a premonition of Wagner’s death. Follow it through three statements, with an F♯ major tune between, to a great climax and the funeral music that ends the movement. All four movements of this set come from one live concert.

    Symphony No. 7 in E major, WAB 107 · II. Adagio — Sehr feierlich und sehr langsam

  2. How does one long melody carry a whole symphonic movement?

    Cellos and a solo horn rise out of tremolo violins with a theme that Bruckner said came to him in a dream. Follow it through three theme groups, a development that breaks into an inverted C-minor eruption, and a coda where the timpani speak for the first time. All four movements of this set come from one live concert.

    Symphony No. 7 in E major, WAB 107 · I. Allegro moderato
