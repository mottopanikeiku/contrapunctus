14.1Life

Handel grew up in Halle, the son of a barber-surgeon, and studied with Friedrich Wilhelm Zachow, organist of the town’s principal church. Zachow’s teaching included counterpoint, keyboard playing and the copying of other composers’ music. Handel briefly enrolled at the university and held an organist’s post before moving to Hamburg in 1703. Its public opera house offered a different education: he played in the orchestra and encountered the practical demands of singers, stage machinery and mixed theatrical repertory. His first opera, Almira, was performed there in 1705, with a German text incorporating Italian arias.

ItalyThe voice as a dramatic instrument

From 1706 to 1710 Handel worked in Italy, writing cantatas, sacred music and opera for patrons in Florence, Rome, Naples and Venice. Rome gave him access to virtuoso singers and to instrumentalists associated with Corelli. The Latin Dixit Dominus of 1707 demands rapid, independent choral lines and sustained control of dissonance; the chamber cantatas make emotional turns within much smaller forces. Agrippina, performed in Venice at the end of 1709, assembles much previously written material into a calculated theatrical comedy. Reuse was already part of his method: a successful musical gesture could acquire a different function when assigned to a new character and situation.

He accepted the Hanoverian court’s Kapellmeister post in 1710 but soon obtained leave to visit London. Rinaldo opened there in 1711, combining Italian virtuoso singing with spectacular scenery and a plot drawn from Tasso. Handel settled in London in 1712. When the Elector of Hanover became George I in 1714, his old employer became the British king. Water Music was performed on the Thames in July 1717. Horns, trumpets, woodwinds and strings were organised to project across an outdoor royal entertainment.

The London theatreCompanies, singers and subscribers

The Royal Academy of Music, established in 1719, was an opera-producing enterprise. Handel recruited singers and supplied operas for its seasons. In a da capo aria the character states an emotional position, passes through a contrasting middle section, then returns to the opening music; the singer’s ornament can give that return renewed insistence. Handel also uses accompanied recitative, changes of instrumental colour and interruptions of expected form. In Giulio Cesare, Cleopatra’s different arias require the same singer to move between calculated seduction, fear and triumph.

The 1720s produced Giulio Cesare, Tamerlano and Rodelinda amid continuing financial pressure. Imported stars were expensive, subscriptions uncertain, and rival companies competed for patrons and audiences. The Opera of the Nobility, founded in 1733, drew singers away from Handel’s enterprise and brought Farinelli to London. Handel continued to write Italian operas, including Ariodante and Alcina in 1735, while exploring other kinds of public performance. His eventual turn towards English oratorio was gradual.

Oratorio was theatre without stagingThe English works were commonly performed in theatres, without scenery or acted movement. Alongside sacred subjects, works such as Hercules draw on classical stories.

A public revival of Esther in 1732 helped establish the new market. English words could be understood without an Italian libretto in hand, and the chorus could represent a people, a community of observers or a collective moral judgement. Saul and Israel in Egypt, both first performed in 1739, assign very different proportions of their drama to soloists and chorus. The instrumental forces also change: trombones and a carillon contribute to Saul’s particular ceremonial world. Organ concertos played between sections displayed Handel himself, linking the composer-performer to the evening’s larger work.

MessiahA scriptural compilation and its occasions

Charles Jennens compiled the text of Messiah from the Bible and the Prayer Book Psalter. Handel composed it in 1741, and its first performance took place in Dublin on 13 April 1742 as a charitable benefit. Unlike the dramatic oratorios, it has no continuously embodied protagonist and very little direct narrative action. Prophecy, the Passion and resurrection are organised through changes of speaker and musical form. He was despised suspends the movement in an extended alto aria; All we like sheep lets separate vocal lines wander before the concluding change of tempo gathers them into a different harmonic and rhetorical weight.

Two benefits, two datesHandel’s first Foundling Hospital benefit in 1749 included the new Foundling Hospital Anthem. The first Messiah performance there followed on 1 May 1750. The Dublin premiere of 1742 belonged to other charities.

The Foundling Hospital gave Messiah a lasting philanthropic setting in London. Handel’s support joined William Hogarth’s work for the institution, which had made painting part of its public appeal. The annual performances also required adaptation: Handel revised vocal movements for the singers available, leaving more than one legitimate performing version. In the same late period he wrote the outdoor Music for the Royal Fireworks, the inwardly concentrated Theodora and the final oratorio Jephtha, each with distinct forces and dramatic purposes.

His eyesight deteriorated while he was composing Jephtha in 1751, and he eventually became blind. Assistants helped maintain performances, and he continued to appear in public musical life. He died in London in April 1759 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. The large commemorative performances that followed in later decades changed the scale of his music, especially the oratorios: expanded choirs and orchestras could obscure the agility written into individual lines. Modern performances on smaller forces recover that agility, but the scores themselves preserve both possibilities of emphasis — the sharply characterised solo speaker and the collective weight of a chorus.

14.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞Vocal character

An aria’s rhythm, register and instrumental partner define a particular situation. Long notes can expose a singer’s control of breath and dissonance; rapid divisions can depict rage, flight or command. Handel wrote for identifiable performers with particular capabilities, so a change of cast could bring a rewritten movement.

𝄋Da capo form

The return of the opening section is a dramatic repetition. The contrasting middle may change key, texture or thought; an ornamented return can make familiar words more insistent or more vulnerable. A complete performance therefore depends on the singer’s treatment of the return as well as on Handel’s written notes.

𝆑The chorus

Homophonic blocks make a declaration intelligible; imitation lets a crowd gather, scatter or pursue an argument. Handel can move between these textures within one movement. In Israel in Egypt, the chorus carries much of the narrative, while other oratorios place it beside an action principally conducted by solo characters.

♮Harmony & pacing

A pedal point can hold the scene in place while the upper parts become dissonant. Sequences build expectation; a delayed cadence prolongs a word or a stage situation. Timing and register often give a harmonic change its force.

𝄢Instrumental rhetoric

French overture rhythms, Italian concerto exchanges and German contrapuntal training coexist in the same career. Horns and trumpets project ceremonial signals; oboes, recorders, bassoons or a solo violin can specify an aria’s character. The organ concertos add an improvising keyboard player’s presence to the public performance of an oratorio.

14.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1707
    Dixit DominusHWV 232Soloists, five-part chorus, strings and continuo
    A Latin psalm setting from the Roman years. The vocal writing demands both rapid articulation and the ability to sustain dissonant notes against moving parts.
  2. 1709
    AgrippinaHWV 6Opera in three acts
    A Venetian political comedy whose characters manipulate one another through changing claims and disguises. Handel adapts substantial amounts of earlier music.
  3. 1711
    RinaldoHWV 7Opera in three acts
    Handel’s first London opera, drawing on Tasso’s crusading epic. The spectacle and virtuoso arias include music reworked from earlier Italian compositions.
  4. 1717
    Water MusicHWV 348–350Orchestra with horns, trumpets and woodwinds
    Associated with George I’s Thames excursion of July 1717. The surviving suites combine French overture writing, dances and concerto-like exchanges.
  5. 1724
    Giulio Cesare in EgittoHWV 17Opera in three acts
    Political and erotic manoeuvres generate sharply differentiated arias. In Va tacito e nascosto, a horn participates in Caesar’s image of the stealthy hunter.
  6. 1736
    Alexander’s FeastHWV 75Soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Dryden’s ode becomes a demonstration of music’s power over Alexander. The changing emotions are carried by a sequence of instrumental, solo and choral characters.
  7. 1739
    Twelve Concerti grossiOp. 6; HWV 319–330Concertino of two violins and cello, string orchestra and continuo
    Corelli’s model is expanded through overtures, dances, fugues and contrasting solo episodes.
  8. 1741; first performed 1742
    MessiahHWV 56Soloists, chorus, orchestra and continuo
    Jennens’s scriptural compilation moves from prophecy through Passion to resurrection. Handel’s revisions for successive casts make the choice of version a real performance decision.
  9. 1749
    Music for the Royal FireworksHWV 351Large wind band with percussion; later version includes strings
    Written for the celebration of the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle. Handel adapted the initial outdoor forces for later concert performance.
  10. 1751; first performed 1752
    JephthaHWV 70Soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Handel’s final oratorio, interrupted during composition by failing sight. The father’s vow and its consequences give the soloists and chorus conflicting ethical positions.

14.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Coram founded the Foundling Hospital in 1739. Hogarth donated this portrait and encouraged other artists to support the institution, helping make it a public destination for painting. Handel’s benefit performances added music to the same philanthropic project. His 1749 benefit introduced the Foundling Hospital Anthem; Messiah followed there in 1750. The Foundling Museum’s collection preserves the material connection.

The procession displays the river as a route for public ceremony, with decorated barges, spectators and the city’s new bridge. Painted thirty years after the documented Water Music excursion, it shows the continuing civic practice that makes a floating royal concert intelligible: the Thames could function as an auditorium as well as a commercial waterway.

John Dryden

Alexander’s Feast — closing stanza

1697
EnglishLet old Timotheus yield the prize, Or both divide the crown: He raised a mortal to the skies; She drew an angel down.

Timotheus has used music to manipulate Alexander’s emotions; Saint Cecilia’s sacred music supplies the final comparison. Handel set Dryden’s ode in 1736, with adaptations by Newburgh Hamilton. The closing antithesis becomes a choral judgement after an evening in which musical affects have been demonstrated one by one. The 1697 text supplies the work’s literary source.

14.8Lineage

Handel
Passed toJoseph HaydnWolfgang Amadeus MozartLudwig van BeethovenFelix MendelssohnEnglish choral societies

Mozart arranged several Handel works for Baron van Swieten, including Messiah, adding instruments and adjusting the sound to another performance culture. The London Handel tradition contributed to the context in which Haydn conceived his late oratorios. Later choral festivals expanded the forces; modern performances often reverse that expansion to recover the independence and speed of the written parts.
