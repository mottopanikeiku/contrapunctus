Life

In May 1914 a fifteen-year-old pianist went to work for Jerome H. Remick and Company in New York, playing songs to sell them. George Gershwin earned fifteen dollars a week. He could sight-read, transpose and keep a tune attractive through another repetition. Four years earlier, his family had bought a piano for his elder brother Ira; George astonished them by sitting down and playing. He had been experimenting at a friend’s house. Born in Brooklyn on 26 September 1898 to Russian-Jewish immigrants, he learned his first professional lesson at a publisher’s keyboard: a song had to make its case while someone was standing beside the piano.

Charles Hambitzer was the teacher who made that keyboard open on to a larger world. Gershwin later called him “the first great musical influence in my life”. He attended concerts by pianists including Leopold Godowsky and Josef Lhévinne, and studied theory with Edward Kilenyi. Even after leaving school, he kept finding classrooms: in the summer of 1921 he took courses in Romantic music and elementary orchestration at Columbia University. The composer who sold popular songs was also the student trying to understand how an orchestra worked.

1919–24A brother at the other end of the song

In 1919 he and Irving Caesar wrote Swanee. Its first appearance in the Capitol Revue attracted little attention; Al Jolson heard it at a party, put it into Sinbad, and made it a hit. Gershwin had already moved from song plugging to theatre rehearsals and a job as a staff composer at T. B. Harms. In December 1924, Lady, Be Good! brought George and Ira together for their first complete Broadway show. Fred and Adele Astaire supplied the dancing feet.

Two kinds of authorshipIra supplied the lyrics to most of the famous later songs. Irving Caesar wrote the words of Swanee; DuBose Heyward wrote the libretto of Porgy and Bess and shared the lyric-writing with Ira.

George usually supplied Ira with music first. For I Got Rhythm, written for Girl Crazy in 1930, Ira spent more than two weeks trying rhymes before deciding that the refrain worked better with very few. George’s four-note figure made ordinary possessions sound like a declaration. Ethel Merman introduced it at the Alvin Theatre, with Ginger Rogers elsewhere in the cast. In the pit were young musicians including Benny Goodman and Gene Krupa. The song could become a vocal show-stopper, a jazz performance, or a set of chords on which someone else could write a new tune.

1924The clarinet goes up

On 4 January 1924 the New York Tribune announced that Gershwin was writing a jazz concerto for Paul Whiteman. The concert was on 12 February; the piece still had to be written. Gershwin worked in a two-piano score, handing pages to Whiteman’s arranger Ferde Grofé, who knew the players and made the band parts. At Aeolian Hall, Gershwin sat at the piano while Whiteman conducted Rhapsody in Blue. Broadway and the concert platform now had a composer in common.

The opening belonged partly to clarinettist Ross Gorman. Gershwin had written a rising run; Gorman turned it into the sliding ascent that reaches a high concert B♭. The sound makes a joke of the distance between the low trill and the top note. Piano solos alternate with band paragraphs, and the broad singing tune can make the whole piece seem to stop and look around. On 10 June Gershwin and Whiteman recorded it for Victor. About a third of the score had to disappear to fit the two record sides.

Whose orchestration?Grofé scored the Rhapsody for Whiteman’s band and later made larger versions. Gershwin orchestrated the Concerto in F himself, as he did his subsequent concert works and opera.

1925–32Trumpet at night, horns in the street

Walter Damrosch commissioned the Concerto in F for the New York Symphony Society and conducted its première at Carnegie Hall on 3 December 1925, with Gershwin as soloist. “The first movement employs the Charleston rhythm,” Gershwin explained in his note on the concerto. Its opening percussion and displaced accents make the dance audible inside a three-movement concert work. The slow movement begins with winds and brass; a muted trumpet hints at a tune before the piano arrives. Late in that movement, piano and flute recall the opening introduction together.

In Paris in 1928 Gershwin shopped for taxi horns. He had begun thinking about An American in Paris on an earlier visit, and now the city’s traffic could enter the orchestra as itself. In Musical America that August he called the score “the most modern music I have yet attempted”. He also described its homesick American drinking in a café, succumbing to the blues, then stepping back into the street. Damrosch conducted the première in December. There was no starring solo pianist: the orchestra did the walking.

A holiday in Havana in February 1932 supplied another set of instruments. For the Cuban Overture, first performed that August under the title Rumba, Gershwin put claves, maracas, güiro and bongos at the front, immediately before the conductor. That year he also began studying with Joseph Schillinger, whose teaching involved mathematical patterns and graph paper. Meanwhile Of Thee I Sing, the Gershwins’ political musical with George S. Kaufman and Morrie Ryskind, won the Pulitzer Prize for drama. The award named the book writers and Ira; the composer was left out.

1935Catfish Row on stage

Gershwin read DuBose Heyward’s Porgy in 1926. Serious work on the opera began in 1933; in the summer of 1934 he stayed on Folly Beach, near Charleston, where the Heywards also had a cottage. DuBose prepared the libretto and worked with Ira on lyrics, while Dorothy Heyward’s stage adaptation stood behind the new work. The score brought lullaby, street cries, prayer and dance into the lives of Catfish Row. Summertime belongs to a woman singing to a child; My Man’s Gone Now belongs to a widow.

Porgy and Bess opened in Boston on 30 September 1935 and in New York on 10 October. Todd Duncan and Anne Brown played the title roles; the Eva Jessye Choir was among the named original performers. The production ran for 124 Broadway performances and lost money. Its Black cast won admiration while its portrayal of Black life attracted objections to stereotyping. Those singers also changed the conditions outside the fiction: on the 1936 tour, the cast’s protest forced Washington’s National Theatre to admit an integrated audience.

The tune after the showJazz musicians built new compositions on the chords of I Got Rhythm. Duke Ellington’s Cotton Tail (1940) is one: the borrowed harmonic route carries new melodies and a new title.

1936–37The last film songs

George, Ira and Ira’s wife Leonore moved to Los Angeles in August 1936. The brothers wrote for films including Shall We Dance, with Fred Astaire and Ginger Rogers. Its songs include They Can’t Take That Away from Me and Let’s Call the Whole Thing Off: small differences in pronunciation could still set a whole duet in motion. Gershwin was working on The Goldwyn Follies when he died of a brain tumour on 11 July 1937. He was thirty-eight. Ira lived until 1983, continuing to work with other composers and preserving his brother’s manuscripts.

The Sound

𝄽Displaced accents

In I Got Rhythm, the recurring four-note figure strikes across the underlying beat. In Fascinating Rhythm, the opening accents pull the phrase into uneven groups while the accompaniment keeps its footing. The tension is audible because the regular pulse stays put.

♭Blues and the piano

The second of the Three Preludes rocks on a repeating bass in C♯ minor. A singing line sits above it, and the slight freedom of poco rubato keeps the melody from marching in step with the left hand. The two quicker preludes frame this small nocturnal room.

𝄞An orchestra with a voice

The clarinet’s slide in Rhapsody in Blue and the muted trumpet in the Concerto in F give wind instruments a singer’s way of shaping a phrase. Grofé knew Whiteman’s individual players; Gershwin’s own concerto gives its slow movement a long wind-and-brass introduction before the piano speaks.

𝄋A song’s architecture

The chorus of I Got Rhythm has three related eight-bar sections around a contrasting bridge: AABA, with a two-bar tag at the end. Jazz performers could leave off that tag, change the melody and retain a shared harmonic route. The song became working material for whole bands.

♮Place as an instrument

The taxi horns in An American in Paris and the claves, güiro, maracas and bongos in the Cuban Overture bring particular streets and dances into the orchestra. In the latter score the Cuban percussionists stand at the front, close to the conductor.

𝄢The operatic community

In Porgy and Bess, a lullaby, a widow’s lament and the cries of street sellers belong to different people in the same neighbourhood. The chorus lets Catfish Row answer as a group; individual songs remain part of a stage full of other lives.

Sister Arts

Gershwin painted himself in the year he was working on Porgy and Bess. Ira later gave the portrait to the Smithsonian. Here the composer supplies the image as well as the name: another of the things he made away from the piano.

Demuth’s portrait of William Carlos Williams uses the poet’s image of a fire engine crossing a dark city: a repeated gold numeral, red vehicle and intersecting streets. Beside the taxi horns of An American in Paris, painted and composed in the same year, it shows another American artist making city noise into a deliberate pattern.

Lineage

Gershwin
Passed toDuke EllingtonCharlie ParkerLeonard Bernstein

Gershwin’s piano and theatre training met the jazz and blues heard in New York; the slow movement of his concerto also shows his admiration for French orchestral writing. The traffic ran both ways. Ellington’s Cotton Tail uses the chords of I Got Rhythm, and bebop players made the same harmonic route a common resource. Bernstein became one of the Rhapsody’s most prominent interpreters.

Further reading

  • Howard Pollack, George Gershwin: His Life and Work (University of California Press, 2007).biography

    How did the songs, concert works and opera grow out of Gershwin’s working life? A large documentary biography with separate chapters on the major scores and their reception.

  • Larry Starr, George Gershwin (Yale University Press, 2011).study

    How does Gershwin’s musical language work in the Broadway theatre? A compact study in Yale’s Broadway Masters series, useful beside the larger biography.

  • Ira Gershwin, Lyrics on Several Occasions (1959; Limelight reprint, 2004).memoir

    How were words fitted to George’s already-composed tunes? Ira explains his craft through lyrics, discarded ideas and the circumstances of particular songs.

  • Richard Crawford, ‘George Gershwin’s “I Got Rhythm” (1930)’, in The American Musical Landscape: The Business of Musicianship from Billings to Gershwin (University of California Press, 1993), chapter 7.chapter

    How did one show tune become a jazz standard and the harmonic basis of other compositions? Crawford follows performers and recordings as well as the Gershwins’ original song.
