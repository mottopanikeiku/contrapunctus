Life

In late 1964, Reich took a tape recorder to Union Square in San Francisco. A young Pentecostal preacher who called himself Brother Walter was telling the story of Noah and the Flood. His warning became It’s Gonna Rain (1965). “Sometimes when people speak, they almost sing,” Reich told Terry Gross on Fresh Air in 1999. Walter stood right on that border. Reich cut a few words from the sermon into loops, put them on two machines and found that a voice could become a round without anyone singing a second part.

Reich had been born in New York in 1936. His parents separated when he was one: his father, a lawyer, stayed in New York; his mother, a singer and songwriter, moved to Los Angeles. From 1939 to 1942 he crossed America by train with his governess, Virginia. At fourteen, records opened another set of doors: Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto, Stravinsky’s Rite of Spring, Charlie Parker and Miles Davis. He took up drums, with Kenny Clarke’s playing in his ears.

He graduated in philosophy from Cornell in 1957. William Austin’s music-history classes took him from chant to Bach, then jumped to Stravinsky and Duke Ellington. Composition lessons with Hall Overton followed, then Juilliard and Mills College, where he studied with Luciano Berio and Darius Milhaud, receiving his master’s degree in 1963. San Francisco brought experimental film and Terry Riley. Reich played in the first performance of Riley’s In C in 1964, among musicians repeating short phrases.

1965–67The machines and the hands

The discovery in It’s Gonna Rain came through two cheap tape recorders that would not quite agree on their speed. Reich started identical loops together. At first the voice seemed to sit in the middle of his head; then one copy edged away, until the words overlapped and answered themselves. The slow separation became the piece.

Back in New York, he made Come Out (1966) for a benefit supporting the retrial of the Harlem Six. The civil-rights activist Truman Nelson had brought him interviews to edit. Daniel Hamm described opening a bruise to show injuries inflicted by police. Reich took the fragment that supplied the title and sent it through the tape machines. Hamm’s syllables gradually crowd one another.

PhasingTwo pianists repeat the same notes. One speeds up just enough to move a note ahead, then matches the other’s speed again. The new alignment is a close canon; the passage into it is part of the music.

Late in 1966, Reich sat at a piano and tried playing against a loop of himself, then creeping ahead of it. He had thought the machines’ trick might be impossible for human hands. It worked. Piano Phase (1967) begins with twelve quick notes in one piano; the second joins in unison before they start slipping apart. Dotted lines between bars ask the pianists to make the change gradually. Their ears have to steer their fingers.

1970–76What the ensemble carried

In the summer of 1970 he went to the University of Ghana to study drumming with Gideon Alorwoyie and other Ewe musicians. He recorded lessons and wrote down the patterns. Back home, Drumming grew from a single bongo stroke: another beat replaces a rest, then another, until the full pattern has appeared. Completed in 1971, it passes from tuned bongos to marimbas and glockenspiels, with voices and piccolo picking out melodies hidden among the repeating notes.

Steve Reich and Musicians began touring Europe in 1971 with some 2,000 pounds of equipment. In Clapping Music (1972), he found a way to leave the marimbas, amplifiers and loudspeakers behind. Two people clap one pattern. One keeps it fixed while the other shifts abruptly a pulse ahead, repeating each new position before moving on. He had tried gradual phasing, but its difficulty seemed out of keeping with the bare hands that made the sound.

The cue belongs to the musicIn Music for 18 Musicians, the metallophone is a vibraphone with its motor switched off. Its little signals tell the group when to change: an audible cue takes the conductor’s place.

For Music for 18 Musicians (1974–76), Reich rented four spinet pianos and set them up in his loft. The group rehearsed over two years, learning the notes and who would signal what. Eleven chords open the piece; each then becomes the home of a longer section. Pianos and mallets keep the pulse running while singers and clarinettists repeat notes for one comfortable breath. First performed at New York’s Town Hall in April 1976, it reached a wider audience through the ECM record released in 1978.

1981–84Words to sing

During the 1970s Reich also studied Balinese gamelan and the chanting of Hebrew scripture. In Tehillim (1981), four women sing newly composed settings of psalm excerpts. The opening voice has only a drum and clapping beneath it; a clarinet joins, then another voice follows in canon. Hebrew syllables decide the length of the bars. The last movement gathers the singers around Halleluyah, arriving in D major.

William Carlos Williams supplied the words for The Desert Music, completed in December 1983 and first performed in Cologne in March 1984. Reich arranged fragments of poems into five movements for chorus and orchestra. Williams’s warning about humanity’s ability to destroy itself occupies the slow heart of the work. Reich considered using a recording of the poet; by summer 1983 he had found chords on which the chorus could sing it.

1988Virginia remembers

Four quartetsDifferent Trains puts three recorded string quartets beside the live players. Recorded voices and American and European train sounds share the tape with them.

For Different Trains, Reich returned to the railway trips of his childhood. He recorded Virginia, now in her seventies, and Lawrence Davis, a retired Pullman porter in his eighties. He also collected testimony from three Holocaust survivors, Rachella, Paul and Rachel, all about his own age. In his 1988 programme note he explained the thought that brought these voices together: “if I had been in Europe during this period, as a Jew I would have had to ride very different trains”.

He chose short phrases whose pitches he could write down and gave those speech melodies to the strings. A person speaks; an instrument takes up the same rise and fall. The three movements move from America before the war to Europe during it, then to the years afterwards. Kronos Quartet gave the première at Queen Elizabeth Hall in London on 2 November 1988.

1993 onwardsFaces on the screen

Reich and the video artist Beryl Korot, whom he married in 1976, carried recorded speech into the theatre. For The Cave (1993), they filmed Israelis, Palestinians and Americans answering questions about Abraham and his family; singers and instrumentalists respond to the interviews. Three Tales (2002) brings historical footage and interviews onto one large screen, with musicians below it. Its subjects are the Hindenburg disaster, the atomic tests at Bikini and Dolly the cloned sheep.

The instrumental Double Sextet (2007), written for eighth blackbird, won the Pulitzer Prize in 2009. Six players can perform against a recording of themselves, the two pianos and vibraphones interlocking beneath winds and strings. Another collaboration began when Gerhard Richter showed Reich his Patterns book at a New York gallery in 2016. In Reich/Richter (2019), written for Richter and Corinna Belz’s film, narrow coloured stripes begin beside a two-note figure. As the images grow, the notes lengthen; when the stripes return, the semiquavers return with them.

The Sound

𝄋Phase

Two players can make a new tune by slipping apart. In Piano Phase, one pianist holds the speed while the other inches ahead, turning a single line into a close canon. The notes briefly blur before settling into their next alignment. Clapping Music makes the same move in a jump: the hands stay on the pulse, and the second player starts the pattern one quaver earlier.

𝅘𝅥Hidden melodies

Reich often adds a player to bring out notes already sounding between the other parts. In Drumming, women’s voices imitate the marimbas and sing melodies picked from their interlocking patterns. As a singer fades away, the melody remains in the wood. The higher glockenspiels call for whistling and piccolo; each new voice fits the instrument it is helping us to hear.

𝄾Pulse and breath

A player’s lungs can determine the length of a phrase. In Music for 18 Musicians, voices and clarinets pulse on a note for one comfortable breath while pianos and mallets maintain their steady beat. The strings follow the bass clarinet’s breathing. Each chord in the opening cycle lasts two breaths, so the players’ bodies help decide how slowly the harmony turns.

♮Harmony

Eleven chords carry the whole of Music for 18 Musicians. Each is heard briefly at the opening, then held for a section of its own; the third chord supports two sections. Later works make sharper changes of key. Double Sextet moves through areas centred on D, F, A♭ and B, or their relative minors, with sudden turns that clearly mark each new stretch.

𝄞Speaking and singing

The shape of a sentence can supply the notes. For Different Trains, Reich chose recorded phrases with pitches clear enough to transcribe, then had strings imitate them alongside the speaking voice. In Tehillim, the Hebrew words supply the rhythms for melodies he composed himself. Long sung sentences take the music through changing metres, with a drum and handclaps tracing their steps.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1965
    It’s Gonna RainTwo-channel tape
    A sermon about Noah, recorded in San Francisco in 1964, becomes two loops that drift apart. The preacher is Brother Walter.
  2. 1967
    Piano PhaseTwo pianos or two marimbas
    One pianist begins a twelve-note pattern; the other joins in unison, then gradually pulls ahead until the pattern has passed through every alignment and returned to unison.
  3. 1970–71
    DrummingPercussion ensemble, voices and piccolo
    A single stroke grows into the pattern that runs through all four parts. The same notes pass between bongos, marimbas and glockenspiels before the instruments combine.
  4. 1972
    Clapping MusicTwo performers clapping
    Written after European tours with some 2,000 pounds of equipment. One pair of hands keeps eight claps in twelve pulses steady while the other rotates the pattern.
  5. 1974–76
    Music for 18 MusiciansVoices, clarinets, violin, cello, pianos and tuned percussion
    Eleven pulsing chords frame the piece and supply its individual sections. The metallophone signals changes while singers and clarinets measure phrases by their breath.
  6. 1981
    TehillimFour women’s voices and ensemble; also orchestral version
    Settings of passages from Psalms 19, 34, 18 and 150, with melodies composed by Reich. A solo voice, drum and handclaps open the work; the final Halleluyah brings it to D major.
  7. 1982–83; première 1984
    The Desert MusicChorus and orchestra
    Fragments of William Carlos Williams arranged into a five-movement arch. The central movement contains the poet’s warning about the destructive power of human wishes.
  8. 1987
    Electric CounterpointElectric guitar with prerecorded guitars and basses
    Written for Pat Metheny, who helped make the writing fit the guitar. A soloist plays against up to ten recorded guitar parts and two electric basses.
  9. 1988
    Different TrainsString quartet and prerecorded sound
    The quartet plays melodies taken from the speech of Reich’s governess, a Pullman porter and Holocaust survivors. Kronos Quartet introduced it in London on 2 November 1988.
  10. 2007
    Double SextetTwo sextets, or one sextet with prerecorded counterpart
    Pairs of pianos and vibraphones drive two matching groups of six, with flute, clarinet, violin and cello in each. Written for eighth blackbird; winner of the 2009 Pulitzer Prize.

Sister Arts

Jack Delano photographed this train leaving Chicago’s Union Station in January 1943, just after the years of Reich’s childhood crossings. The locomotives and the great post-office building put those journeys back into a working city. In Different Trains, Virginia’s memories and Lawrence Davis’s account of life as a Pullman porter bring voices from that railway world into the quartet.

Psalm 19, verses 2–3 in Hebrew numbering

The heavens tell

Ancient Hebrew text
Hebrewהשמים מספרים כבוד אל ומעשה ידיו מגיד הרקיע יום ליום יביע אמר ולילה ללילה יחוה דעת
Our translationThe heavens tell the glory of God; the vault declares the work of his hands. Day pours out speech to day, and night makes knowledge known to night.

Tehillim begins with these verses and continues through verse 5 of Psalm 19. A woman sings with drum and handclaps; the clarinet doubles her, then another voice follows in canon. Reich wrote the melody himself, letting the Hebrew words set the lengths of the bars. Day answers day in the poem, and one singer answers another.

Reich
Passed toJohn AdamsJulia WolfeDavid LangMichael GordonElectronic dance music

Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto and Stravinsky’s Rite of Spring were records Reich knew as a teenager. Riley’s In C gave him hands-on experience of repeated phrases; Ghanaian drummers showed him how independent patterns could fit together. Gradual phasing was Reich’s own contribution to minimalism. John Adams carried the steady beat and repeating cells into Shaker Loops (1978), written for seven strings.
