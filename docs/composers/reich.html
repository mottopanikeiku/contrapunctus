66.1Life

Reich was born in New York in 1936. His parents separated when he was one, and his childhood included repeated train trips between his father in New York and his mother in Los Angeles. Those trips later supplied the starting point of Different Trains. He studied philosophy at Cornell, graduating in 1957, then composition with Hall Overton and at Juilliard. At Mills College in California he worked with Luciano Berio and Darius Milhaud, completing his master’s degree in 1963.

Jazz and Stravinsky offered models for new concert music built around a regular beat or tonal centre. In San Francisco, Reich also encountered experimental film, tape composition and Terry Riley’s repeating patterns. He took part in the first performance of Riley’s In C in 1964. His own next step depended on a mechanical fact: two tape machines playing the same loop at nominally the same speed would gradually separate because their speeds differed slightly.

Tape, 1965–66One voice becomes many

It’s Gonna Rain (1965) uses the recorded preaching of Brother Walter in San Francisco’s Union Square. Repetition exposes pitch and rhythm already present in the speech; gradual displacement turns one voice into overlapping versions of itself. Come Out (1966) takes a phrase from Daniel Hamm, one of the Harlem Six, describing how he made an injury visible after police violence. The recorded voice brings its history into the musical process.

PhasingTwo identical patterns begin together. One moves slightly faster until it is a note ahead, then resumes the same speed. Between stable alignments, attacks blur and regroup; the transition itself belongs to the piece.

After returning to New York, Reich transferred the tape process to live performers. In Piano Phase (1967), two pianists repeat a short pattern while one gradually advances relative to the other. The players must hear the displacement as it happens. The stable positions reveal new composite patterns within their unchanged material. The listener’s attention can change which of those patterns seems to be in the foreground.

PercussionGhana and the ensemble

Reich studied Ewe drumming with Gideon Alorwoyie at the University of Ghana in 1970. The experience confirmed the possibilities of interlocking rhythmic parts and percussion as the basis of a substantial musical structure. Drumming, completed in 1971, passes related rhythmic material through tuned bongos, marimbas and glockenspiels. Voices and piccolo draw attention to patterns emerging from the instruments’ combined attacks.

The ensemble Steve Reich and Musicians developed these pieces through rehearsal and touring. Its performers needed shared habits of pulse, listening and cueing that went beyond standard conservatory training. Clapping Music (1972) removed almost all the equipment: two people clap the same twelve-pulse pattern. One remains fixed; the other jumps one pulse ahead after a set of repetitions. Unlike Piano Phase, the displacement is abrupt. Reich abandoned gradual phasing here because its difficulty was at odds with the piece’s direct means of sound production.

Music for 18 Musicians (1974–76) expanded the harmonic and instrumental resources. Eleven chords are introduced as a cycle, and sections then elaborate them individually. Pianos and mallet instruments maintain a continuous pulse, while clarinets and voices produce lengths governed partly by breathing. A metallophone gives cues for transitions. The structure therefore depends on several kinds of time: the small regular beat, the span of a breath and the much slower harmonic succession. The ECM recording released in 1978 carried the ensemble’s work beyond its touring audience.

Language returnsPsalms and poetry

Reich’s study of Hebrew and cantillation led to Tehillim (1981), settings of four psalm excerpts for women’s voices and ensemble. He composed new melodies. The Hebrew words generate changing phrase lengths and metres, replacing the unvarying short loops of the earlier pieces. Canons and rhythmic interlocking remain, but now serve extended sung sentences. The concluding movement establishes D major after stretches of greater harmonic ambiguity; tonal arrival has become a large-scale event.

The Desert Music (1984), for chorus and orchestra, sets William Carlos Williams. Its five movements join a fast outer frame to slower central writing, while the vocal text brings questions of technological violence into a large concert work. Changes of harmony, sustained melody and sectional contrast increasingly require decisions beyond the workings of a single automatic process.

Speech melodyIn Different Trains, short recorded phrases supply approximate pitches and rhythms which the strings imitate. The recorded person remains audible alongside the instrumental line.

Different Trains, 1988Two histories on tape

For Different Trains, Reich recorded his former governess Virginia and the retired Pullman porter Lawrence Davis, and used recorded testimony from Holocaust survivors. His American train trips of 1939–42 were set against the trains he might have been forced onto as a Jewish child in Europe. Three prerecorded string quartets, voices and train sounds accompany the live quartet. Short speech contours generate instrumental material, while the words establish places, dates and consequences.

The Kronos Quartet gave the premiere in London in 1988. Reich subsequently developed documentary music theatre with the video artist Beryl Korot, whom he had married in 1976. The Cave (1993) combines recorded interviews with instrumental and vocal responses; Three Tales (2002) examines technological events through synchronised video, speech and ensemble. Their multiple screens introduce another kind of counterpoint, between images and speakers as well as between instrumental lines.

Later instrumental works include Double Sextet (2007), awarded the Pulitzer Prize in 2009, and Reich/Richter (2019), written for a film made by Gerhard Richter and Corinna Belz. Repeated units remain audible, but they now support harmonic pacing and formal contrasts unlike those of the first tape pieces. Reich turned ninety on 3 October 2026. His scores are performed by ensembles formed long after Steve Reich and Musicians established the practical skills of hearing, maintaining and deliberately disturbing a shared pulse.

66.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄋Phase

Identical repeating patterns change their relative position. Piano Phase moves gradually between alignments; Clapping Music jumps directly to the next one. The difference matters: gradual phasing includes the unstable interval between two clear positions, whereas the clapping piece places one fixed rhythmic relationship beside another.

𝅘𝅥Resulting patterns

Interlocking parts produce lines that emerge only in combination. A listener can group attacks into a new melody or accent pattern; another performer may then emphasise that pattern explicitly. The density in Drumming changes which relationships among existing notes become audible.

𝄾Pulse & breath

A steady small pulse can coexist with unequal larger spans. In Music for 18 Musicians, the repeated instrumental attacks continue beneath phrases shaped by singers’ and wind players’ breath. Tehillim lets Hebrew syllables and stresses determine changing metres.

♮Harmony

The early phase pieces hold their pitch collections relatively still so that displacement can be heard. Music for 18 Musicians expands an eleven-chord cycle; Tehillim makes a final tonal centre consequential. Later sectional works use harmonic change to define large spans.

𝄞Recorded speech

A spoken phrase has an approximate pitch contour as well as rhythm. In Different Trains, strings imitate those contours while the recording preserves the speaker’s accent and timbre. The documentary voice is both musical material and historical evidence. In the works with Beryl Korot, filmed speech adds visible bodies and places to that relationship.

66.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1965
    It’s Gonna RainTwo-channel tape
    Brother Walter’s recorded preaching is looped and displaced; the speech’s own rhythm and pitch become increasingly audible as musical material.
  2. 1967
    Piano PhaseTwo pianos or two marimbas
    Live performers move through successive alignments of repeating patterns, preserving the gradual transitions between them.
  3. 1970–71
    DrummingPercussion ensemble, voices and piccolo
    Four parts move through tuned bongos, marimbas and glockenspiels before combining them; rhythmic construction joins phase shifting.
  4. 1972
    Clapping MusicTwo performers clapping
    Eight attacks within twelve equal pulses. One player repeats unchanged; the other shifts abruptly through all twelve positions.
  5. 1974–76
    Music for 18 MusiciansVoices, clarinets, violin, cello, pianos and tuned percussion
    An eleven-chord cycle supports sections articulated by pulse, breath and audible instrumental cues.
  6. 1981
    TehillimFour women’s voices and ensemble; also orchestral version
    Hebrew psalm excerpts set to newly composed melodies. Text rhythm replaces the fixed short metric patterns of the early works.
  7. 1984
    The Desert MusicChorus and orchestra
    Five movements on texts by William Carlos Williams; the vocal and harmonic writing extends well beyond phase-process form.
  8. 1987
    Electric CounterpointElectric guitar with prerecorded guitars and basses
    Written for Pat Metheny. A live player adds a line to a multilayered recording of their own instrument.
  9. 1988
    Different TrainsString quartet and prerecorded sound
    American childhood train journeys and Holocaust testimony are linked through speech-derived melodies; written for the Kronos Quartet.
  10. 2007
    Double SextetTwo sextets, or one sextet with prerecorded counterpart
    Each group comprises flute, clarinet, violin, cello, vibraphone and piano. Three movements alternate fast, slow and fast music.

66.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Delano’s government photograph records the American railway system at almost the same moment as Reich’s childhood journeys of 1939–42. Different Trains returns to that passenger world through a governess’s memories, a Pullman porter’s testimony and recorded train sounds, then places it against deportation trains in Europe. The photograph supplies historical context.

Psalm 19, verses 2–3 in Hebrew numbering

The heavens tell

Ancient Hebrew text
Hebrewהשמים מספרים כבוד אל ומעשה ידיו מגיד הרקיע יום ליום יביע אמר ולילה ללילה יחוה דעת
Our translationThe heavens tell the glory of God; the vault declares the work of his hands. Day pours out speech to day, and night makes knowledge known to night.

The opening words of Tehillim; the first movement continues through verse 5. Reich composed his own melodies. The Hebrew syllables generate flexible rhythmic groupings, and the voices enter in canons. These verses pair one day with another and one night with another before the music makes its own overlapping statements.

minimalism groups together composers with distinct techniques. Reich’s gradual displacement differs from Riley’s independently repeated cells and from Pärt’s rule-governed scale-and-triad pairings. John Adams inherited the regular pulse and repeating instrumental figures while turning them towards different harmonic and dramatic forms.
