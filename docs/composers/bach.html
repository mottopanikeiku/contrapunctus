Johann SebastianBach

Born 21 March 1685 (Old Style), Eisenach, ThuringiaDied 28 July 1750, Leipzig

An organist, teacher and church musician who made a bass line, a dance rhythm and a fugue subject carry the same argument.

Trained

ILife

Bach was born into a family of professional musicians in Eisenach, where his father Johann Ambrosius served as a town musician. The surname already meant a trade: relatives supplied organists, cantors and instrumentalists across Thuringia. His mother died in 1694 and his father in 1695; at ten he went to live with his elder brother Johann Christoph, an organist in Ohrdruf who had studied with Pachelbel. Keyboard instruction belonged to that household, alongside school and choir. In 1700 Bach travelled to Lüneburg, where a place in the choir of St Michael’s school gave a boy without substantial means access to an education and a musical library.

The early career was practical and local. After a brief appointment at Weimar, he became organist of the New Church in Arnstadt in 1703, with a recently built instrument to test and play. His duties included working with students, and the town’s records preserve disputes over both his behaviour and his music. In 1705 he obtained leave to hear Dieterich Buxtehude in Lübeck and stayed away much longer than authorised. Buxtehude’s organ works offered a model in which free passagework, recurring basses and strict counterpoint could occupy the same piece. Bach’s own organ preludes would retain that alternation between improvisatory display and composed argument.

1707–17The organ loft and the Italian concerto

In 1707 he moved to St Blasius in Mühlhausen and married Maria Barbara Bach, a second cousin. The surviving early cantatas already treat the words as a formal problem rather than material for a succession of songs. In Aus der Tiefen rufe ich, Herr, zu dir, BWV 131, psalm verses are set beside a chorale: one voice sings an individual plea while another holds a communal melody in longer notes. The technique makes two texts audible at once. Mühlhausen also involved him in plans to improve the organ, an early instance of the technical knowledge that later brought commissions to inspect instruments in other towns.

Returning to Weimar in 1708, Bach served as court organist and chamber musician; promotion to Konzertmeister in 1714 added the regular production of cantatas. Italian concertos circulating at court gave him another working vocabulary. He arranged music by Vivaldi and others for organ or harpsichord, transferring the contrast between full ensemble and soloist to two hands, two manuals and pedals. He absorbed the structural use of a returning ritornello, sequences that move a phrase through successive harmonies, and a bass that directs the movement’s key changes. In a Bach concerto, these devices are usually occupied by more independent inner voices than the Italian model required.

The Weimar years produced much of the Orgelbüchlein, a collection of short chorale preludes organised around the church year. A familiar hymn tune is surrounded by a small, continuously recurring figure: falling lines, repeated notes, suspensions or an insistent pedal pattern. The player must make the tune, accompaniment and bass speak separately, often with only a few bars in which to establish the design. That economy is as characteristic as the scale of the later Passions. Bach’s preface presents the book as instruction in developing a chorale and in pedal playing; devotion and the training of hands and feet share the same page.

1717–23A court without weekly cantatas

In 1717 Bach accepted a post with Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Köthen before securing his release from Weimar. Duke Wilhelm Ernst had him confined for almost a month before dismissing him. Köthen’s court was Calvinist, so its elaborate professional music-making was chiefly secular rather than a programme of Lutheran church cantatas. Leopold played instruments and maintained a skilled ensemble. Bach could concentrate on suites, sonatas and concertos, although the sources seldom allow a neat equation between a surviving work and one particular court occasion. The six concertos dedicated to the Margrave of Brandenburg in 1721 assembled music of differing origins rather than constituting a newly composed, uniform set.

The Brandenburg Concertos repeatedly change the identity of the solo group. The Second places a high trumpet beside recorder, oboe and violin; the Sixth omits violins, setting violas against the lower strings. In the Fifth, flute and violin share the foreground with a harpsichord that eventually leaves its accompanying position for an extended first-movement solo. The six sonatas and partitas for unaccompanied violin, collected in a manuscript dated 1720, solve the opposite problem: how one instrument can imply bass, harmony and several melodic voices. In the D minor Chaconne, recurring harmonic functions support variations whose texture expands from broken chords to sustained multiple stopping.

Not equal temperamentThe title Das wohltemperierte Clavier proposes a keyboard usable in every major and minor key. It does not identify modern equal temperament as Bach’s preferred tuning. Unequal circulating temperaments could also make all twenty-four keys available.

Maria Barbara died in 1720 while Bach was away with the court. In 1721 he married Anna Magdalena Wilcke, a professional singer; the music books associated with her preserve household repertory by several composers, not exclusively her husband. Teaching also generated the two-part Inventions and three-part Sinfonias, whose 1723 title page recommends a singing manner of playing as well as clean part-writing. The first book of The Well-Tempered Clavier, dated 1722, places a prelude and fugue in each major and minor key. These are not twenty-four applications of one formula: subjects, textures, metres and the scale of the preludes change from pair to pair.

LeipzigMusic for a particular Sunday

Bach became Thomaskantor in Leipzig in 1723, after Telemann and Christoph Graupner had proved unavailable. He was responsible for music at the principal churches and for the musical training of boys at the Thomasschule. The job required negotiation with school authorities, clergy and the town council, not merely composition. His 1730 memorandum on a properly constituted church ensemble distinguishes able singers from pupils who could scarcely contribute and specifies the instrumental resources he considered necessary. Those documents should prevent the surviving scores from being mistaken for descriptions of an effortlessly available orchestra. Parts had to be copied, players assembled and difficult music rehearsed for an appointed service.

During his first Leipzig years he produced cantatas at an exceptional rate, reusing and revising earlier works as well as composing new ones. The prescribed readings gave each Sunday a subject; recitative, aria, chorus and chorale placed biblical words, contemporary poetry and inherited hymnody in different relationships. In the chorale-cantata cycle begun in 1724, the opening stanza frequently remains intact while inner stanzas are paraphrased for arias and recitatives. The opening chorus can turn the hymn into a large concerted movement: long notes in one part are supported by active imitation in the others and by an orchestral ritornello with material of its own.

Who sang?The number of singers Bach used on individual parts remains contested. Surviving performing materials support different reconstructions; a modern chamber choir and an ensemble of solo singers represent different answers, not a settled choice between historical truth and error.

The St John Passion was first performed in 1724; the first version of the St Matthew Passion followed in 1727. Both place the Gospel narrative in a tenor Evangelist’s recitative, but interrupt it with reflective arias, chorales and choral interventions. In St Matthew, two vocal and instrumental ensembles can answer one another, while the opening chorus adds a separate soprano chorale above them. Jesus’s words are normally accompanied by sustained strings; their absence at his cry of abandonment has a precise dramatic effect. The crowd choruses, by contrast, compress argument into repeated figures, short entries and sharply directed harmonic movement.

From 1729 Bach also directed Leipzig’s Collegium Musicum, whose concerts were associated with Gottfried Zimmermann’s coffeehouse. This civic setting provided a place for instrumental music and secular cantatas outside the Sunday service. The surviving harpsichord concertos belong to a culture in which Bach and his sons were performers, and in which an existing violin or oboe concerto could be recast for keyboard. Such adaptation was not confined to instrumental pieces. Much of the Christmas Oratorio, performed across six feast days in 1734–35, reworks earlier secular cantata movements. New words and changed scoring could redirect an existing musical structure without concealing its origin.

The printed collectionsTeaching, demonstration, experiment

The Goldberg storyThe account of Count Keyserlingk commissioning music for sleepless nights appears in Johann Nikolaus Forkel’s biography of 1802. The 1741 edition names neither the count nor the young keyboard player Johann Gottlieb Goldberg.

Bach issued comparatively little of his music in print. The four parts of Clavier-Übung nevertheless form a deliberately varied public statement: six partitas; the Italian Concerto and French Overture; a collection of organ music centred on chorales; and the variations now called Goldberg. The last work builds thirty variations not simply on the aria’s tune but on its bass and harmonic plan. Every third variation through No. 27 is a canon, with the interval between the imitating voices rising step by step. Dance, hand-crossing display and strict counterpoint are therefore ordered within the same large design, ending with a quodlibet that combines popular tunes before the aria returns.

In 1747 Bach visited Frederick II in Potsdam, where Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach served at court. The king supplied a theme for improvisation; Bach subsequently developed it into The Musical Offering, with ricercars, canons and a trio sonata for flute, violin and continuo. The flute acknowledges the king’s instrument, while the six-part ricercar puts the theme through a density of counterpoint far beyond a polite court divertissement. The Art of Fugue, worked on during the 1740s, pursues a different experiment: related subjects in D minor are inverted, augmented and combined. Its open-score notation separates the voices visually; it does not establish that the music was intended to remain unplayed.

In 1748–49 Bach assembled the complete B minor Mass from music written over several decades, including the Kyrie and Gloria sent to Dresden in 1733 and a Sanctus of 1724. No complete performance in his lifetime is documented. Failing eyesight interrupted his final work, and unsuccessful operations by the travelling surgeon John Taylor preceded his death in July 1750. Anna Magdalena survived him by ten years. Music dispersed among his children, pupils and collectors; some was lost, while keyboard and organ works continued to circulate among professionals. The unfinished fugue associated with The Art of Fugue stops in the manuscript, but the exact relationship between that interruption and his final illness is not established.

Bach was not wholly forgotten before Mendelssohn conducted an abridged St Matthew Passion in Berlin in 1829. Mozart encountered his contrapuntal music through Baron van Swieten; Beethoven studied it; pupils and collectors maintained a manuscript tradition. The nineteenth-century change was in public scale: editions, choral societies and concert performances made a working church musician into a central historical figure. Later composers learned different things from the same scores — Chopin the keyboard prelude, Brahms the bass variation, Webern the articulation of individual contrapuntal lines, Shostakovich the cycle of twenty-four preludes and fugues. Each inheritance selects a technique rather than reproducing Bach’s musical institutions.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄢Counterpoint

A voice remains a line even when it supplies a chord tone. In fugues, a subject can overlap its own answer in stretto, turn upside down in inversion, or move in longer notes in augmentation. Bach uses these procedures to control pressure and register, not simply to satisfy a rule. The C major fugue of Book I crowds its short subject into overlapping entries; the C♯ minor fugue builds a much longer argument from contrasting subjects.

♮Bass & harmony

The bass tells the ear where a phrase is going, while suspensions delay agreement above it. A note held across a change of bass can become dissonant before resolving down by step. Chromatic lines intensify this process: the opening chorus of Weinen, Klagen, Sorgen, Zagen descends through a lamenting bass pattern later reworked for the Mass’s Crucifixus. Figured bass is the working grammar, not a separate accompaniment layer.

𝅘𝅥Dance & metre

Allemande, courante, sarabande and gigue supply different distributions of weight. In the solo suites, multiple stopping may establish a chord while a single moving line carries the dance forward. A sarabande’s second beat can bear the dissonance; a gigue can become a fugue without losing its compound-metre propulsion. The sacred music also borrows these rhythmic characters without always naming the dance.

𝄞Chorale & text

A chorale is inherited material with words attached in the congregation’s memory. Bach can state it in four-part harmony, stretch it over faster counterpoint, or conceal phrases within an instrumental texture. Recitative follows verbal syntax; an aria isolates a thought for repeated examination. Word-painting is local and audible: a long held note, a descending line or a restless figure does a particular job within a larger form.

𝆑Instrumental colour

Oboe d’amore, oboe da caccia, flute, recorder, viola da gamba and trumpet are not interchangeable solo colours. Their ranges and attacks help determine the kind of line Bach writes. The Brandenburgs repeatedly rearrange the hierarchy of soloists and accompanists; the harpsichord’s first-movement solo in No. 5 makes a continuo instrument dominate the ensemble. In the cantatas, an obbligato instrument can act as a second speaker alongside the singer.

𝄋Large-scale form

Repetition operates at several levels: a ritornello within a movement, a bass across variations, a chorale through a cantata, a sequence of keys across a collection. The Goldberg Variations preserve a harmonic foundation while changing metre, texture and contrapuntal procedure. The Well-Tempered Clavier tests the resources of every major and minor key without making every fugue follow the same plan.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1707–08
    Aus der Tiefen rufe ich, Herr, zu dirBWV 131Soloists, chorus, oboe, strings and continuo
    An early sacred concerto on Psalm 130. Chorale lines are superimposed on the individual supplication rather than reserved for a closing hymn.
  2. c. 1708–17
    OrgelbüchleinBWV 599–644Organ
    Forty-six completed chorale preludes in a projected collection with many more titles. A concise school of obbligato pedal playing and motivic accompaniment.
  3. 1714
    Weinen, Klagen, Sorgen, ZagenBWV 12Soloists, chorus, trumpet, oboe, strings and continuo
    The opening chorus uses a descending chromatic bass; Bach later adapted its central material for the Crucifixus of the B minor Mass.
  4. c. 1717–23
    Six Cello SuitesBWV 1007–1012Unaccompanied cello; five-string instrument specified for No. 6
    Preludes followed by dances. The Fifth asks for the top string to be retuned; the Sixth extends the register beyond the normal four-string cello.
  5. 1720
    Sonatas and Partitas for Solo ViolinBWV 1001–1006Unaccompanied violin
    Three four-movement sonatas alternate with three dance collections. The D minor Partita ends with the Chaconne, a sustained variation structure built from a recurring harmonic scheme.
  6. 1721
    Six Brandenburg ConcertosBWV 1046–1051Six different instrumental ensembles
    The dedication is dated 1721; the music draws on earlier work. Each concerto proposes a different relationship between solo group, ensemble and bass.
  7. 1722
    The Well-Tempered Clavier, Book IBWV 846–869Keyboard
    Twenty-four preludes and fugues, moving chromatically from C major and C minor to B major and B minor. No single temperament is prescribed by the title.
  8. 1723
    Inventions and SinfoniasBWV 772–801Keyboard
    Fifteen two-part and fifteen three-part pieces. Bach’s title page connects clear voice-leading with a singing style of execution and the acquisition of compositional ideas.
  9. 1724
    St John PassionBWV 245Soloists, chorus, orchestra and continuo
    The Evangelist’s narration is interrupted by arias, chorales and tightly organised crowd choruses. Bach revised the work repeatedly after its first Leipzig performance.
  10. 1727; revised 1736
    St Matthew PassionBWV 244Soloists, two choirs and orchestras, additional soprano ripieno
    A double-ensemble design with poetry by Picander. The opening chorus places a chorale above an exchange between the two principal choirs.
  11. 1731
    Wachet auf, ruft uns die StimmeBWV 140Soloists, chorus, horn, oboes, taille, strings and continuo
    Philipp Nicolai’s hymn supplies three structural pillars. Between them, the dialogue of soul and Christ becomes chamber music for soprano, bass and obbligato instrument.
  12. 1734–35
    Christmas OratorioBWV 248Soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Six cantatas for six services from Christmas Day to Epiphany, not one continuous concert work. Much of the music adapts earlier secular cantatas.
  13. 1735
    Italian ConcertoBWV 971Two-manual harpsichord
    A concerto without orchestra. Marked contrasts of forte and piano distinguish ensemble-like statements from solo passages; the slow movement sets an embellished line over a steady accompaniment.
  14. 1739
    Clavier-Übung IIIOrgan
    The E♭ major Prelude and Fugue BWV 552 frame chorale settings and four duets. Large settings with pedal stand beside smaller manualiter versions.
  15. c. 1740–50; published 1751
    The Art of FugueBWV 1080Unspecified; normally performed on keyboard or by instrumental ensemble
    A D minor subject is developed through fugues and canons, including mirror counterpoint. The surviving final, unfinished fugue introduces the notes B♭–A–C–B♮.
  16. 1741
    Goldberg VariationsBWV 988Two-manual harpsichord
    Aria, thirty variations and the returning aria. Canons at successively larger intervals alternate with character pieces and passages designed around two-manual technique.
  17. c. 1742
    The Well-Tempered Clavier, Book IIBWV 870–893Keyboard
    A second traversal of all twenty-four keys, assembled from music of different dates. The preludes range from compact inventions to extended binary movements.
  18. 1747
    The Musical OfferingBWV 1079Keyboard and chamber instruments; trio sonata for flute, violin and continuo
    Ricercars, puzzle canons and a trio sonata on the theme supplied by Frederick II. The six-part ricercar treats its chromatic subject in a particularly dense texture.
  19. 1748–49, assembled
    Mass in B minorBWV 232Soloists, chorus, orchestra and continuo
    A complete Latin Ordinary assembled from several decades of work. The Crucifixus reuses the lament bass of BWV 12; the following Et resurrexit changes metre, register and instrumental weight.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

The paper in Bach’s hand is the Canon triplex a 6 voci, BWV 1076: three written lines generate six sounding parts by canonic imitation. The portrait thus displays a piece of compositional craft rather than an instrument. Bach joined Lorenz Christoph Mizler’s Corresponding Society of the Musical Sciences in 1747; the canon also belongs to the learned exchange associated with that society. Haussmann painted a second version in 1748, now held by the Bach-Archiv.

The view places church and school side by side: Bach’s principal musical workplace and the institution that housed and trained his singers. His family lived in the Thomasschule, not in an isolated composer’s studio. The arrangement matters to the cantatas: the same establishment supplied education, accommodation, rehearsal and the labour of preparing music for the city’s churches. The engraving dates from the year of his arrival in Leipzig.

Martin Luther

Aus tiefer Not schrei ich zu dir — first stanza

1524
GermanAus tiefer Not schrei ich zu dir, Herr Gott, erhör mein Rufen. Dein gnädig’ Ohren kehr zu mir und meiner Bitt sie öffne. Denn so du willst das sehen an, was Sünd und Unrecht ist getan, wer kann, Herr, vor dir bleiben?
Our translationFrom deep distress I cry to you; Lord God, hear my calling. Turn your gracious ears towards me and open them to my petition. For if you choose to look upon the sin and wrong that have been done, who, Lord, can stand before you?

Luther’s paraphrase of Psalm 130 supplies the chorale of Cantata BWV 38 and two organ settings in Clavier-Übung III. In BWV 686 Bach writes six parts, including two for the pedals: the hymn is embedded in a texture whose sustained notes and imitative preparation recall older vocal polyphony. The German text is given in modernised spelling; the published hymn predates Bach by two centuries.

Philipp Nicolai

Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme — opening lines

1599
GermanWachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme der Wächter sehr hoch auf der Zinne, wach auf, du Stadt Jerusalem! Mitternacht heißt diese Stunde; sie rufen uns mit hellem Munde: Wo seid ihr klugen Jungfrauen?
Our translationWake up, the voice calls to us, of the watchmen high upon the battlement; wake up, you city of Jerusalem! This hour is called midnight; they call to us with ringing voices: where are you, wise maidens?

Nicolai published words and melody in 1599. Bach’s BWV 140, first performed in 1731, uses all three stanzas of the hymn, with new poetry between them developing the bridal imagery. In the opening chorus the sopranos carry Nicolai’s melody in long notes above active lower voices; in the fourth movement the tenors sing the second stanza against an independent string melody. The hymn refers to the parable of the wise and foolish virgins in Matthew 25.

VIListen

Recordings worth seeking out.

  • Goldberg VariationsGlenn Gould, pianoColumbia · 1955Short articulations and sharply differentiated voices make the canons immediately legible. Compare with a two-manual harpsichord performance: the instrument changes the physical meaning of the crossing parts.
  • St Matthew PassionCollegium Vocale Gent, Philippe HerrewegheHarmonia MundiHerreweghe’s recordings keep the chorales and narrative within a relatively light vocal scale. Follow the distinction between the Evangelist’s speech rhythm and the arias’ repeated reflection.
  • Mass in B minorDunedin Consort, John ButtLinnA small vocal ensemble makes the changes between individual lines and combined sonority particularly easy to hear; the accompanying documentation explains the performance choices.
  • Six Cello SuitesPablo Casals, celloEMI · 1936–39Historical recordings with audible portamento, flexible pulse and strongly projected bass notes. They document a performing tradition rather than a reconstruction of eighteenth-century cello playing.
  • Cantatas — Bach Cantata PilgrimageMonteverdi Choir, English Baroque Soloists, John Eliot GardinerSoli Deo GloriaThe recordings made around the 2000 pilgrimage let the cantatas be heard in groups related to the liturgical calendar, restoring connections that an isolated anthology obscures.

VIILineage

Drew fromDieterich BuxtehudeJohann PachelbelGeorg BöhmAntonio VivaldiArcangelo CorelliLutheran chorales
Bach
Passed toCarl Philipp Emanuel BachWolfgang Amadeus MozartLudwig van BeethovenFelix MendelssohnFrédéric ChopinJohannes BrahmsAnton WebernDmitri Shostakovich

Vivaldi supplied concerto procedures that Bach transformed through transcription; Buxtehude and the central German organ tradition supplied models for free and strict writing. The later inheritance is documented in particular works: Brahms arranged the D minor Chaconne for piano left hand, Webern orchestrated the six-part ricercar, and Shostakovich answered the keyboard cycles with his Op. 87. C. P. E. Bach inherited the training without continuing his father’s style unchanged.
