Life

Bach grew up in a house full of working musicians. His father, Johann Ambrosius, served the town of Eisenach; apprentices lived with the family, and relatives held church and civic posts across Thuringia. Johann Sebastian was born in 1685. His mother died when he was nine, his father the following year, and he moved to Ohrdruf to live with his elder brother Johann Christoph, an organist taught by Pachelbel. He sang in the school choir and learnt the organ at home. At fifteen he went to St Michael’s School in Lüneburg, where his voice helped pay for his education.

In 1703 Arnstadt hired him to examine its new organ and then to play it. Two years later he set off on foot to hear Dieterich Buxtehude in Lübeck. He stayed for months, far beyond his authorised leave, and came back to a reprimand. In 1707 he moved to Mühlhausen and married his second cousin Maria Barbara. His cantata Aus der Tiefen, BWV 131, already makes two people say different things together: one sings the psalmist’s plea, another a hymn in long notes above it.

1708–17Learning from other people’s scores

Weimar gave him a court organist’s post in 1708 and, from 1714, the rank of concertmaster, with a new cantata due every month. He copied and arranged Italian concertos, especially Vivaldi’s. On the organ, an orchestra’s answer to a solo violin could become an exchange between keyboards. The bass went to the feet. Vivaldi’s recurring refrains and chains of changing keys entered Bach’s own concertos, with additional voices threading their way between the tune and the bass.

The little chorale preludes of the Orgelbüchlein made equally exacting demands. A hymn might sit in long notes above a tiny figure that runs almost without stopping; the pedal has a part of its own. Bach fitted forty-six completed pieces into a book planned for many more. Each gives an organist a practical problem: how to keep the congregation’s tune audible while making the other lines sing too.

When he accepted a new job at Köthen in 1717, the Duke of Weimar refused to release him. Bach insisted. The duke had him imprisoned for almost a month, then dismissed him. Prince Leopold of Anhalt-Köthen, his next employer, was a keen musician with a good ensemble; the Calvinist court had little use for elaborate church cantatas. Bach could spend his time on instrumental music.

KöthenSix concertos, one violin, twenty-four keys

In 1721 he sent six concertos to the Margrave of Brandenburg. Each puts a different set of players in charge. The Second has trumpet, recorder, oboe and violin competing for attention; the Sixth leaves out violins altogether. In the Fifth, a harpsichord begins among the accompanying instruments and ends up holding the orchestra silent through a long solo. Bach could make a court entertainment behave like a small coup.

The manuscript of his unaccompanied violin sonatas and partitas is dated 1720. Here a single bow has to suggest the ensemble that has disappeared: bass notes, chords, separate voices. The D minor Chaconne turns its recurring harmonies through broken chords and great handfuls of stopped strings. The cello suites put similar demands into dance forms. Their allemandes and sarabandes keep the steps of the dance in the ear even when the player has only one note sounding.

In 1720 Bach returned from travelling with Leopold to find Maria Barbara dead and buried. He married the court singer Anna Magdalena Wilcke the following year. Their music books place lessons beside household entertainment. Writing to his old schoolfriend Georg Erdmann on 28 October 1730, he boasted of the children: “They are all born musicians.” His household could already form a vocal and instrumental ensemble, he added, since Anna Magdalena sang a clear soprano and his eldest daughter was good too.

Well-temperedA tuning that makes every key usable. The title leaves open which temperament Bach preferred; several unequal tunings can do this, as well as modern equal temperament.

The first book of The Well-Tempered Clavier, dated 1722, gives a prelude and fugue in every major and minor key. Its opening C major Prelude had an earlier, shorter life in the lesson book for his eldest son Wilhelm Friedemann. The two-part Inventions and three-part Sinfonias likewise teach the hands to act independently. Bach’s 1723 title page puts a singing manner of playing among their chief aims. He was training composers as well as keyboard players.

1723–50Sunday comes round again

Leipzig appointed him Thomaskantor in 1723, after Telemann and Graupner proved unavailable. He had schoolboys to teach, church music to direct and, in the early years, an extraordinary succession of cantatas to write. Parts had to be copied and rehearsed before Sunday. The cycle begun in 1724 often builds each cantata round one hymn: the first stanza becomes a large chorus, inner stanzas are recast as arias and recitatives, and the congregation’s melody returns at the close.

The choirThe number of singers Bach used on each part is still disputed. His surviving parts and his 1730 memorandum to the council underpin performances ranging from one singer per line to small choirs.

The council did not make the work easy. In that October 1730 letter to Erdmann, Bach described “a strange authority with little devotion to music” and asked whether his friend knew of another job. Leipzig was expensive; funeral fees fluctuated with the death rate. In a healthier year he had lost more than a hundred thalers. Earlier that summer he had sent the council a detailed account of the singers and instrumentalists he needed. He remained in Leipzig for the rest of his life.

The St John Passion was first heard in 1724, the St Matthew Passion in 1727. A tenor Evangelist tells the Gospel story; arias stop the clock to dwell on what has happened, and chorales let familiar hymn tunes enter the drama. In St Matthew, two choirs and orchestras call to one another. Sustained strings accompany Jesus’s speech until his cry of abandonment, when they fall silent. After Peter’s denial, a solo violin begins Erbarme dich before the alto can find the words.

From 1729 Bach also directed the Collegium Musicum at Zimmermann’s coffeehouse. Here he could play concertos and present secular cantatas away from the church authorities. Music travelled between the two establishments. In the Christmas Oratorio, performed over six feast days in 1734–35, movements written for secular celebrations received new words and a place in the Nativity story. Bach knew what a good piece could bear.

The later collectionsRules and surprises

Goldberg’s sleepless countForkel’s 1802 biography tells of Count Keyserlingk asking for music to occupy sleepless nights, played by the young Johann Gottlieb Goldberg. Neither man is named in the 1741 edition.

The four parts of Clavier-Übung gathered keyboard partitas, an Italian Concerto and French Overture, organ music, and finally the Goldberg Variations. Thirty variations follow the opening aria’s bass and harmonies. Every third one through No. 27 is a canon, the imitating voice entering at a successively wider interval. Where a tenth canon might come, Bach puts a quodlibet, a mixture of popular tunes. Then the aria returns, unchanged.

In 1747 he visited Frederick II at Potsdam, where his son Carl Philipp Emanuel worked. The king gave him a theme to improvise on. Bach took it home and made The Musical Offering: canons, two ricercars and a trio sonata with a flute part for the king’s instrument. He was also assembling the B minor Mass from music reaching back decades. The Crucifixus reworks a chorus from the 1714 cantata Weinen, Klagen, Sorgen, Zagen; its descending bass goes round beneath the suffering voices.

Failing sight and two unsuccessful eye operations preceded his death in July 1750. The Art of Fugue left related D minor subjects turned upside down, lengthened and combined; its unfinished fugue introduces the notes B♭–A–C–B♮, his surname in German notation. His children and pupils preserved scores, though many vanished. Mozart and Beethoven studied the music before Mendelssohn brought an abridged St Matthew Passion to Berlin in 1829. Later, Brahms gave the Chaconne to a pianist’s left hand, and Webern passed the six-part ricercar through an orchestra, a few notes at a time.

The Sound

𝄢Independent voices

Each part has something to say while the others are speaking. In the C major Fugue from The Well-Tempered Clavier, a new entry of the subject repeatedly begins before the previous one has finished: the overlap is called stretto. In The Art of Fugue, the tune can turn upside down or move in longer notes and still fit its companions.

♮The bass

The bass can make a whole movement ache. In Weinen, Klagen, Sorgen, Zagen, it descends by semitones while the voices lament above it; Bach returned to that music for the Mass’s Crucifixus. Elsewhere a held upper note becomes painfully wrong against a new bass note, then slips down to resolve. Figured bass puts those changing chords under the continuo player’s fingers.

𝅘𝅥Dances

The cello suites and violin partitas still have dancers’ feet beneath them. An allemande moves differently from a sarabande, with its lingering second beat, or a quick gigue. Bach can put a bass note on one beat and a melody on the next, letting a single instrument suggest several players. Chords arrive just often enough to keep the floor under the dance.

𝄞Hymn tunes

A Lutheran chorale arrives with words already attached in the congregation’s memory. In Wachet auf, the sopranos stretch Nicolai’s tune over the opening chorus; later the tenors sing another stanza against a flowing string melody. In Aus der Tiefen, a chorale and a psalm sound together. Two texts can speak across one another without either losing its tune.

𝆑Instrumental partners

Bach’s arias often have two protagonists: a singer and an instrument. The solo violin in Erbarme dich has begun its lament before the alto enters. The Brandenburg Concertos try a different game each time, pairing a high trumpet with recorder, oboe and violin in No. 2, then giving the harpsichord an enormous first-movement solo in No. 5.

𝄋Collections

Bach sets himself a rule and finds out how much music it will hold. The Goldberg Variations keep the aria’s bass and harmonies while changing the dance, the number of voices and the difficulty of the hand-crossing. The Well-Tempered Clavier visits every major and minor key, from C to B. A second book makes the circuit again, with new preludes and fugues.

Sister Arts

Bach holds a puzzle towards the painter: the Canon triplex a 6 voci, BWV 1076. Its three written lines generate six sounding parts. Haussmann painted him twice, in 1746 and 1748; the later picture passed to Carl Philipp Emanuel, who kept it among his collection of musicians’ portraits.

Church, school and home were neighbours. Bach’s family lived in the Thomasschule, whose boys supplied singers for Leipzig’s churches. This engraving dates from 1723, the year he arrived; a cantata’s copied parts had only a short distance to travel before the Sunday service.

Martin Luther

Aus tiefer Not schrei ich zu dir — first stanza

1524
GermanAus tiefer Not schrei ich zu dir, Herr Gott, erhör mein Rufen. Dein gnädig’ Ohren kehr zu mir und meiner Bitt sie öffne. Denn so du willst das sehen an, was Sünd und Unrecht ist getan, wer kann, Herr, vor dir bleiben?
Our translationFrom deep distress I cry to you; Lord God, hear my calling. Turn your gracious ears towards me and open them to my petition. For if you choose to look upon the sin and wrong that have been done, who, Lord, can stand before you?

Luther’s Psalm 130 paraphrase became Bach’s Cantata BWV 38 and two organ settings in Clavier-Übung III. In BWV 686 six parts sound at once, two of them played by the feet. The organist must keep Luther’s tune audible inside that crowded conversation.

Philipp Nicolai

Wachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme — opening lines

1599
GermanWachet auf, ruft uns die Stimme der Wächter sehr hoch auf der Zinne, wach auf, du Stadt Jerusalem! Mitternacht heißt diese Stunde; sie rufen uns mit hellem Munde: Wo seid ihr klugen Jungfrauen?
Our translationWake up, the voice calls to us, of the watchmen high upon the battlement; wake up, you city of Jerusalem! This hour is called midnight; they call to us with ringing voices: where are you, wise maidens?

Nicolai’s watchmen call the city awake at midnight. Bach set all three stanzas in Wachet auf, first performed in 1731: sopranos carry the opening hymn, then tenors take the second stanza against a string melody with its own dancing life. The words recall the wise and foolish virgins in Matthew 25.

Gardiner’s dramatic attack makes the words urgent, while the changing programmes reveal how differently Bach could approach the same Sunday.

Bach learnt from Buxtehude’s organ music and from the Vivaldi concertos he arranged for keyboard. His own scores became working material for later composers: Brahms put the D minor Chaconne into the left hand alone, Webern orchestrated the six-part ricercar, and Shostakovich wrote his own cycle of twenty-four preludes and fugues. At home, Carl Philipp Emanuel had learnt the craft directly from his father.
