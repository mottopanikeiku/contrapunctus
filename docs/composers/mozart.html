ILife

Mozart was born in Salzburg in 1756, the seventh child of Leopold Mozart and Anna Maria Pertl; only he and his sister Maria Anna survived infancy. Leopold was a violinist and composer in the archbishop’s service, and in the year of Wolfgang’s birth published an influential violin treatise. He taught both children. Maria Anna, called Nannerl within the family, became an accomplished keyboard player, and the first tours presented brother and sister together. The family’s travels were an educational system as well as a business: the children encountered instruments, performers, repertories and audiences that a Salzburg childhood alone could not have supplied.

The long tour of 1763–66 took them through German courts, Paris, London and the Netherlands. In London Mozart met Johann Christian Bach, whose clear phrases and Italianate melodic style offered a model different from the contrapuntal teaching associated with Bach’s father. Illness repeatedly interrupted the itinerary. Leopold managed engagements, publicity and money while supervising composition, and the surviving correspondence documents the costs and uncertainties behind the public demonstrations. Wolfgang learned to write for named performers and immediate occasions before he had an independent adult household.

Salzburg and ItalyComposing for particular voices

Three Italian visits with Leopold between 1769 and 1773 brought experience of opera seria, with its alternating recitatives and substantial arias for star singers. Mitridate, re di Ponto was staged in Milan in 1770; Lucio Silla followed there in 1772. These were professional theatre commissions, not simply childhood exercises. Mozart learned how a singer’s range, breath and capacity for passagework could shape a scene. His later operas retain that attention to the individual performer even when ensembles, rather than successive solo arias, carry the dramatic structure.

Back in Salzburg, employment under Archbishop Hieronymus Colloredo required church music, orchestral pieces and other services. The five violin concertos of 1773–75 and the piano concerto in E♭ K. 271 of 1777 show how different a concerto could become under his hands. In K. 271 the piano interrupts the orchestra almost immediately instead of waiting through a complete opening exposition. The soloist enters as a participant with an opinion, not as the ornamented repetition of material already laid out. The C minor slow movement brings the weight of an operatic lament into an instrumental work.

Names and nicknamesK. 271 was long called the ‘Jeunehomme’ Concerto through a mistaken identification. Its dedicatee was the pianist Victoire Jenamy. Mozart’s own surname and first names also appear in several forms; he often used the French ‘Amadè’, rather than the later standard ‘Amadeus’.

In 1777 Mozart travelled with his mother in search of a better appointment. Mannheim offered an excellent orchestra and the Weber family, including the singer Aloysia, with whom he fell in love. Paris supplied commissions but no permanent position. His mother died there in July 1778. He returned to Salzburg in 1779 and became court organist, but the conflict between his ambitions and service obligations remained. A Munich commission for Idomeneo, premiered in 1781, let him work with singers and players capable of sustaining a more elaborate dramatic design, including major choral scenes.

ViennaThe keyboard as public theatre

In 1781 a bitter dispute ended Mozart’s employment with Colloredo, and he settled in Vienna. He supported himself through teaching, performances, publication and commissions, with aristocratic assistance still important. This was not independence from patronage: it was a more variable mixture of patrons and markets. In 1782 he married Constanze Weber, Aloysia’s younger sister. Of their six children, two sons survived infancy. The marriage and the management of the household run through his letters alongside deadlines, pupils, lodging expenses and requests for payment.

Die Entführung aus dem Serail, first performed in Vienna in 1782, joined German dialogue to elaborate singing and the percussion associated with European imitations of Turkish bands. In the same city Mozart built a public career as a pianist. His subscription concerts of the mid-1780s required new concertos, and the orchestra became increasingly capable of answering the soloist in individual voices. In the D minor Concerto K. 466, syncopated strings generate tension before the piano introduces a new theme; in the A major K. 488, clarinets replace oboes and the central movement turns to F♯ minor.

The chamber works served smaller gatherings but demanded no less concentrated listening. Mozart dedicated six quartets to Haydn in 1785 after a long period of composition and revision. Their independence of parts can conceal how tightly the exchanges are organised. K. 465 begins with a slow introduction in which successive entries create dissonances before C major becomes secure. The two string quintets of 1787 add a second viola, enlarging the middle register rather than merely adding brilliance above the violins. The C major and G minor quintets use the same five instruments to make very different kinds of argument.

Working manuscriptsMozart’s surviving drafts, corrections and unfinished pieces contradict the idea that every work arrived complete without labour. The six ‘Haydn’ quartets are a particularly useful corrective: the polished surface is not a reliable record of how quickly a passage was composed.

Da PonteThe ensemble changes the situation

Lorenzo Da Ponte supplied the librettos for Le nozze di Figaro (1786), Don Giovanni (1787) and Così fan tutte (1790). In Figaro, adapted from Beaumarchais, a count’s sexual and social privileges meet organised resistance from his servants and his wife. Mozart does not assign one musical manner permanently to one class. Susanna can control a scene through the timing of her replies; the Count can lose control while retaining an impressive vocal line. The long Act II finale keeps adding people, information, tempos and keys without stopping for explanatory dialogue.

Prague’s enthusiasm for Figaro led to the commission for Don Giovanni. Its classification as a dramma giocoso accommodates comedy and death within the same work. Three stage bands playing dances in different metres turn social hierarchy into simultaneous musical layers. In the supper scene, the return of the Commendatore brings back the D minor sonority of the overture; trombones, sustained wind chords and the bass voice make a different physical world from the fast-moving servant comedy. Mozart revised the opera for Vienna in 1788, so choices between Prague and Vienna numbers remain part of performing the work.

Così fan tutte makes symmetry itself uncomfortable: two couples, disguised lovers, repeated tests and departures that are staged as if they were genuine. The music can give a character’s present emotion full weight even when the audience knows the situation is contrived. A duet does not have to certify permanent truth to express a real feeling in the moment. Across all three operas, ensembles allow several people to mean different things simultaneously; harmony and phrase structure hold their conflicting intentions together without making them agree.

1791Several unfinished accounts

Mozart’s finances grew less secure in the later 1780s, and letters to his fellow Freemason Michael Puchberg request loans. The last three symphonies, K. 543, 550 and 551, were entered in his catalogue within weeks of one another in 1788; the precise occasions he expected for them are not established. Their juxtaposition is striking enough without inventing a planned trilogy: E♭ major ceremonial writing, G minor agitation and a C major finale combining several themes in counterpoint. A later version of K. 550 adds clarinets and revises the oboe parts.

In 1791 he completed the Clarinet Concerto for Anton Stadler’s extended-range instrument and wrote two operas for different institutions: La clemenza di Tito for the Prague coronation of Leopold II as King of Bohemia, and Die Zauberflöte for Emanuel Schikaneder’s suburban Viennese theatre. The latter combines spectacular machinery, spoken dialogue, popular song, coloratura and a solemn initiatory plot. Mozart had joined a Masonic lodge in 1784, but the opera’s theatrical sources are wider than a single coded ritual. Papageno’s bodily needs are not answered in the same musical language as Sarastro’s institutional authority.

The Requiem remained unfinished when Mozart died on 5 December 1791, aged thirty-five. The commission came from Count Franz von Walsegg, in memory of his wife; its anonymous delivery generated later legends. Constanze arranged for completion, with Franz Xaver Süssmayr providing the version that established the work in performance. Mozart was buried at St Marx in a common grave under contemporary Viennese practice, not in the pauper’s burial imagined by later melodrama. The incomplete manuscript, the posthumous completion and the conditions of burial each have a documentary history distinct from the fictional death scenes built around them.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝅘𝅥Melody

A balanced opening phrase can contain a small irregularity that determines the rest of a movement. Vocal lines are fitted to breath and vowel; instrumental melodies can imitate that shape while extending it beyond a singer’s capacity. Ornament often changes the pressure on a cadence rather than simply decorating an already complete tune.

♮Harmony

Chromatic notes are often expressive neighbours or suspensions inside clear tonal movement. A shift into the minor can change the status of a familiar idea, while a delayed resolution lets two voices disagree temporarily. The slow introduction to K. 465 makes the listener wait for a stable account of C major.

𝄋Form

Concerto form gives the orchestra and soloist overlapping but non-identical responsibilities. The piano may enter with new material, interrupt a prepared design or make a familiar idea sound like an answer. In sonata movements, several distinct themes can belong to one tonal area; counting tunes is not the same as following the form.

𝄞Woodwinds

Oboe, bassoon, flute and clarinet acquire individual speaking parts, especially in the piano concertos. Winds can form a small ensemble inside the orchestra, complete a phrase the piano has left open or sustain a harmony while the keyboard moves around it. Stadler’s low clarinet register prompted music whose shape is inseparable from that particular sound.

𝄢Counterpoint

Imitation enters the ordinary fabric of quartets and sonatas rather than appearing only in designated fugues. The finale of the ‘Jupiter’ Symphony can combine five thematic ideas near its end because their intervals and rhythms have been designed to fit together. Learned technique becomes a closing dramatic event.

𝄾Dramatic time

An operatic ensemble can preserve several speeds of thought at once: one character pleads, another calculates, a third fails to understand. Changes of tempo and key let a long finale incorporate new information without stopping. Spoken dialogue, recitative, aria and ensemble each control time differently.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1773
    Symphony No. 25 in G minorK. 183Orchestra with four horns
    Syncopations, unison attacks and wide leaps drive the first movement. Four horns broaden the harmonic resources and weight of the wind group.
  2. 1775
    Violin Concerto No. 5 in A majorK. 219Solo violin and orchestra
    The soloist first enters at a slower tempo than the orchestral opening. The finale interrupts its minuet with a noisy minor-key episode conventionally labelled “Turkish”.
  3. 1777
    Piano Concerto No. 9 in E♭ major “Jenamy”K. 271Piano and orchestra
    An early solo interruption changes the orchestra–piano relationship. The C minor slow movement carries the declamatory weight of an operatic scena.
  4. 1781
    Idomeneo, re di CretaK. 366Opera seria in three acts
    A father’s vow endangers his son. Choruses, orchestral recitative and the sea-monster episode stretch the conventional sequence of solo arias.
  5. 1782
    Die Entführung aus dem SerailK. 384German Singspiel in three acts
    Konstanze’s demanding arias coexist with spoken dialogue, comic bass writing for Osmin and “Turkish” percussion in the orchestra.
  6. 1782–85
    Six Quartets dedicated to HaydnK. 387, 421, 428, 458, 464, 465String quartet
    From contrapuntal finales to the suspended tonality of the “Dissonance” introduction. The dedication acknowledges a living colleague, not a historical school.
  7. 1785
    Piano Concerto No. 20 in D minorK. 466Piano and orchestra
    Syncopated strings destabilise the opening before the piano enters with its own material. Beethoven later supplied cadenzas for the first and last movements.
  8. 1786
    Piano Concerto No. 23 in A majorK. 488Piano and orchestra with clarinets
    A major frames an Adagio in F♯ minor. Wind exchanges are integral to the first movement’s development, not just changes of colour behind the keyboard.
  9. 1786
    Le nozze di FigaroK. 492Opera buffa in four acts; libretto by Da Ponte
    One day in a household where a planned wedding threatens the Count’s authority. The Act II finale builds from a domestic dispute into a full ensemble through successive entrances.
  10. 1787
    String Quintets in C major and G minorK. 515 and 516Two violins, two violas and cello
    The added viola expands the centre of the texture. K. 516’s muted Adagio and slow introduction to the finale resist an uncomplicated move from G minor to major.
  11. 1787
    Don GiovanniK. 527Dramma giocoso in two acts; libretto by Da Ponte
    Comedy, attempted seduction and supernatural punishment. Prague and Vienna versions differ; the final ensemble after Giovanni’s destruction restores the surviving characters’ separate viewpoints.
  12. 1788
    Symphonies Nos. 39, 40 and 41K. 543, 550, 551Orchestra
    E♭ major, G minor and C major. The “Jupiter” finale makes contrapuntal combination part of a sonata design; No. 40 survives with and without clarinets.
  13. 1790
    Così fan tutteK. 588Opera buffa in two acts; libretto by Da Ponte
    A wager about fidelity produces a formally symmetrical experiment whose music repeatedly complicates the experimenters’ assumptions.
  14. 1791
    La clemenza di TitoK. 621Opera seria in two acts
    Written for the Prague coronation. Obligato clarinet and basset horn give two major arias unusually individual instrumental partners.
  15. 1791
    Die ZauberflöteK. 620German Singspiel in two acts; libretto by Schikaneder
    Popular theatre and initiation drama share a stage. The Queen’s high coloratura, Sarastro’s low bass and Papageno’s song define different modes of authority and desire.
  16. 1791
    Clarinet Concerto in A majorK. 622Basset clarinet and orchestra
    Written for Anton Stadler’s instrument with notes below the ordinary clarinet’s range. Modern basset-clarinet performances restore the low-register shape of the solo line.
  17. 1791
    Requiem in D minorK. 626Soloists, chorus, orchestra and organ
    Unfinished. The standard completion is by Süssmayr; modern editions offer alternatives. Basset horns and bassoons give the surviving opening a dark wind sonority.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Leopold plays the violin beside Wolfgang at the keyboard while Maria Anna stands with music. The drawing records the family’s Paris visit during the long tour of 1763–66. Its arrangement matters: the child composer appears within a working ensemble managed by his father, not alone at a desk receiving inspiration. Nannerl’s place in the group also restores the sibling partnership often removed from accounts of the tour.

Schinkel designed the setting for the Berlin Magic Flute of 1816; this print belongs to a later publication of his designs. The Queen occupies a celestial architecture that gives visual form to the separation already audible between her high coloratura and the lower voices of the other characters. It documents the opera’s nineteenth-century staging history, not the scenery of the 1791 premiere. Museum catalogue.

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Das Veilchen — opening

1774; published 1775
GermanEin Veilchen auf der Wiese stand, Gebückt in sich und unbekannt, Es war ein herzigs Veilchen. Da kam eine junge Schäferin Mit leichtem Schritt und munterm Sinn Daher, daher, Die Wiese her und sang:
Our translationA violet stood in the meadow, bent into itself and unknown; it was a dear little violet. Then came a young shepherdess, with light step and cheerful mind, this way, this way, across the meadow, singing:

Mozart set the poem as K. 476 on 8 June 1785. The violet’s imagined speech and the girl’s unknowing destruction of it turn the song into a small dramatic scene; Mozart adds the final exclamation Das arme Veilchen! The excerpt gives Goethe’s opening with modernised spelling, not a translation borrowed from a song edition. Köchel catalogue record.

Lorenzo Da Ponte

Le nozze di Figaro — forgiveness in the final scene

1786
ItalianIL CONTE: Contessa, perdono! LA CONTESSA: Più docile io sono, e dico di sì. TUTTI: Ah tutti contenti saremo così.
Our translationTHE COUNT: Countess, forgive me! THE COUNTESS: I am more yielding, and I say yes. ALL: Ah, then we shall all be content.

The Count has just refused the forgiveness that he now needs himself. Mozart slows the action for the Countess’s answer and then allows the ensemble to take up its harmony. The words establish the reversal of power; the sustained musical response gives it a duration beyond ordinary dialogue. Text: the 1786 Vienna libretto.

Mozart
Passed toLudwig van BeethovenFranz SchubertFrédéric ChopinRichard Strauss

Haydn was a colleague as well as a model. Beethoven’s cadenzas for K. 466 document a direct performing relationship with Mozart’s concerto style; Chopin’s admiration centred on the balance of vocal line and formal control. Richard Strauss repeatedly returned to Mozartian ensemble writing when several characters needed to occupy the same musical time.
