RichardStrauss

Born 11 June 1864, Munich, BavariaDied 8 September 1949, Garmisch-Partenkirchen, Germany

An orchestral storyteller who took opera to the harmonic edge in Salome and Elektra, then made conversation and recollection its central events.

Trained
Munich; composition with Friedrich Wilhelm Meyer; practical conducting apprenticeship with Hans von Bülow at Meiningen
Posts
Meiningen · Munich · Weimar · Berlin Court Opera, 1898–1918 · Vienna State Opera, co-director 1919–24
Collaborator
Hugo von Hofmannsthal: six operas, from Elektra to Arabella
Output
Tone poems, fifteen operas, concertos, chamber works and more than two hundred songs

ILife

Richard Strauss grew up hearing the horn at close quarters. His father Franz was principal horn of the Munich Court Orchestra, an exacting player who performed Wagner while disliking his music. The household’s approved models were Mozart, Haydn and Beethoven; Richard’s early chamber works, concertos and symphonies begin within that inheritance. His mother Josepha belonged to the Pschorr brewing family. Born in Munich in 1864, he studied privately rather than at a conservatoire, receiving composition lessons from Friedrich Wilhelm Meyer and learning what an orchestra could actually execute through his father’s colleagues. His two horn concertos, separated by nearly sixty years, preserve that early instrumental familiarity.

Hans von Bülow brought him to Meiningen in 1885, first as an assistant and then as his successor. Rehearsing an orchestra trained by Bülow supplied a practical education in balance, articulation and the management of complex scores. Another Meiningen musician, Alexander Ritter, urged him towards Liszt’s symphonic poems and Wagner’s music dramas. Strauss had already begun reconsidering his father’s prejudices; Ritter helped give the change a compositional direction. The orchestra could sustain an argument shaped by a character or a literary situation instead of by the four movements of a symphony.

1888–98The orchestra narrates

Don Juan, written in 1888 and premiered under Strauss at Weimar in 1889, established the new manner immediately. Its upward rush, long oboe melody and unison horn declaration require sharply differentiated kinds of attack. The ending does not merely stop the excitement: the music collapses into the minor and expires. Tod und Verklärung gives a dying artist an orchestral life review, while Till Eulenspiegel makes a horn and a high clarinet two aspects of the same disruptive figure. Programmes identify events, but the recurrence, combination and transformation of motives make the events musically legible.

Story and formTill Eulenspiegel announces rondo form in its full title; Don Quixote is a set of variations. Strauss did not discard inherited forms when he attached a story to them.

In Also sprach Zarathustra he set out from Nietzsche rather than attempting to illustrate a continuous plot. The opening C–G–C trumpet call withholds the third that would decide between major and minor; the orchestra supplies and then alters it. Later a fugue subject uses all twelve chromatic pitches without abolishing tonal function. The work ends with B major above persistent low Cs, leaving its tonal opposition unresolved. Don Quixote distributes Cervantes’s characters among a solo cello, solo viola and orchestral instruments; flutter-tonguing winds turn sheep into an imaginary army. Ein Heldenleben quotes Strauss’s own music as the hero’s works, making autobiography a compositional resource.

His conducting posts moved from Munich to Weimar, back to Munich and in 1898 to Berlin. In 1894 he married the soprano Pauline de Ahna, who had sung in his first opera, Guntram. She was an important interpreter of his songs, not simply the domestic character later represented in Intermezzo. The four songs of Op. 27 were a wedding gift; their sustained soprano lines and independent accompaniment anticipate the vocal writing of the operas. Their son Franz was born in 1897. The family itself became the subject of Sinfonia domestica, first performed in New York in 1904.

The theatreTwo one-act tragedies

Salome, premiered in Dresden in 1905, made Strauss an internationally successful opera composer. He set a shortened German translation of Oscar Wilde’s play, retaining its repetitions and charged images. A large orchestra separates the characters harmonically and supplies information their words evade. Jochanaan’s proclamations, Herod’s nervous interruptions and Salome’s long final address to a severed head occupy different musical registers. The final monologue can expand into a love scene while the stage picture makes such expansion intolerable. The opera’s continuous single act leaves no interval in which to escape that contradiction.

Elektra followed in Dresden in 1909, using Hugo von Hofmannsthal’s reworking of Sophocles. The orchestra is larger still, but its extremes include thin, exposed lines as well as massed percussion and brass. Elektra’s obsessive recollection of her murdered father fractures time: motives return as compulsions, not reassuring landmarks. Her recognition of Orest briefly permits an extended lyrical scene; the concluding dance destroys rather than releases her. Strauss employed overlapping triads and dense chromatic writing without making an irreversible conversion to atonality. Distinct tonal regions remain part of the drama.

HofmannsthalThe poet and composer negotiated by letter: the correspondence records cuts, pacing, vocal demands and disagreements about what an audience could grasp in performance. Their partnership was a working argument, not automatic agreement.

1911–29Comedy, ceremony, time

Der Rosenkavalier, premiered in 1911, replaced the mythic courtyard with an invented eighteenth-century Vienna. Its nineteenth-century waltzes are deliberate anachronisms. The Marschallin’s awareness of ageing gives the comedy its temporal depth, while Octavian’s trouser role allows three female voices to form the final trio. In Ariadne auf Naxos, tragedy and commedia dell’arte are forced to share a stage. The unsuccessful 1912 combination with Molière’s Le Bourgeois gentilhomme was replaced in 1916 by a newly composed prologue; chamber-sized forces create sharply different kinds of theatrical speech.

Eine Alpensinfonie, completed in 1915, takes a mountain ascent from night through sunrise, summit and storm back into darkness. Wind and thunder machines are only part of its scoring: long passages depend on the changing relationship of short motives and sustained basses. The wartime years also brought the completion of Die Frau ohne Schatten, first performed in Vienna in 1919. Hofmannsthal’s fairy tale makes marriage, fertility and refusal into a dense network of symbols. Strauss’s appointment as co-director of the Vienna State Opera lasted from 1919 to 1924; with Hofmannsthal, Max Reinhardt and others he also helped establish the Salzburg Festival.

Hofmannsthal died in 1929, leaving Arabella without the further revisions Strauss wanted. Stefan Zweig supplied the next libretto, Die schweigsame Frau. Their collaboration collided with the Nazi state: Zweig was Jewish, and Strauss’s insistence that his name appear at the 1935 Dresden premiere became politically contentious. Strauss had accepted the presidency of the Reichsmusikkammer in 1933. An intercepted letter to Zweig contributed to his dismissal in 1935, but that dismissal cannot turn his earlier acceptance of the regime’s cultural office into resistance.

Complicity and dangerStrauss never joined the Nazi Party, but held its musical office and continued to accept official commissions. His Jewish daughter-in-law Alice and his grandsons were endangered by the same state. Both facts belong in the account.

1945–49Late counterpoint

The destruction of German opera houses during the war forms part of the background to Metamorphosen, completed in 1945 for twenty-three solo strings. Its individually moving lines converge upon the funeral march from Beethoven’s Eroica; the score’s “In memoriam” does not by itself name one simple object of mourning. The Second Horn Concerto and Oboe Concerto, also late works, use smaller forces and more exposed textures. Strauss’s retrospective style was not a return to technical simplicity: the string writing of Metamorphosen requires the listener to follow several simultaneous lines.

He and Pauline lived in Switzerland after the war before returning to Garmisch in 1949. The four orchestral songs written in 1948 set Hermann Hesse and Joseph von Eichendorff; the title Four Last Songs and their familiar order were established after his death, and they were not literally his last compositions. Solo horn and violin emerge from the orchestra as partners to the soprano. In Im Abendrot, a motive from Tod und Verklärung returns beneath the question of death. Strauss died in September 1949; Kirsten Flagstad and Wilhelm Furtwängler gave the songs’ first performance in London the following year.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞Orchestration

Strauss assigns different motives to distinct registers and instrumental colours, allowing several lines to remain legible within a large ensemble. The solo violin of Ein Heldenleben, the cello of Don Quixote and the oboe of Don Juan function as individual speakers. A hundred players need not mean a hundred players sounding together.

♯Harmony

Triads remain audible even when they clash. The so-called Elektra chord superimposes E major and C♯ major, retaining the shared G♯ while setting E♮ against E♯. In Zarathustra, C and B become rival tonal centres; the final pages decline to reconcile them through a conventional cadence.

𝄢Counterpoint

The tone poems can combine motives that were first heard separately, creating a musical equivalent of simultaneous action. Metamorphosen distributes its argument among twenty-three individual strings rather than orchestral sections. The parts must remain independent even when they collectively produce a rich chord.

𝅘𝅥Voice

Strauss writes both rapid conversational declamation and long soprano arches that extend over orchestral changes. The Marschallin’s reflections depend on the first kind; the final trio of Rosenkavalier transforms the same drama into sustained ensemble singing. Breath, register and the balance of the orchestra govern the phrase as much as its contour.

𝄋Form & programme

A story does not remove the need for musical return. Till adapts rondo recurrence, Don Quixote variation, and Ein Heldenleben a broad sonata-like argument. Their programmes explain why material changes character; formal relationships explain why the episodes belong to one work.

𝄆Historical styles

The waltzes in Rosenkavalier belong to a later century than its setting; the opera makes its artificial past audible. Ariadne contrasts heroic opera with comic interruption inside a single small orchestra. These are staged collisions of musical conventions, not an attempt to reconstruct eighteenth-century performance practice.

Friedrich Nietzsche

Also sprach Zarathustra — Zarathustra’s prologue, §1

1883
GermanDu grosses Gestirn! Was wäre dein Glück, wenn du nicht Die hättest, welchen du leuchtest!
Our translationYou great star! What would your happiness be if you did not have those upon whom you shine!

Zarathustra addresses the sun before descending from his mountain solitude. Strauss’s sunrise makes the address audible without setting the words; later section headings draw on other chapters rather than following the book sentence by sentence. Nietzsche’s metaphor gives a reason for the opening’s direction, from a low pedal to a high brass declaration. The German text is the source, not a programme added by the film 2001.

The painting’s alternative title records the period’s tendency to confuse Judith, who kills Holofernes, with Salome, who demands Jochanaan’s head. Strauss’s opera concerns the latter; Klimt’s picture is not its source. Both belong to the same early-twentieth-century reconsideration of the biblical woman holding a severed male head. The date also coincides with Elektra: classical and biblical subjects were being detached from reassuring academic conventions.

Hugo von Hofmannsthal

Der Rosenkavalier — the Marschallin on time

1911
GermanDie Zeit, die ist ein sonderbar Ding. Wenn man so hinlebt, ist sie rein gar nichts. Aber dann auf einmal, da spürt man nichts als sie. Sie ist um uns herum, sie ist auch in uns drinnen.
Our translationTime is a strange thing. When you simply go on living, it is nothing at all. But then, all at once, you feel nothing but time. It is all around us; it is also inside us.

The Act I speech is written for a woman who understands that her young lover will leave her. Strauss largely withdraws the conversational bustle around the Marschallin, giving her words room within an orchestral continuity that does not stop. Hofmannsthal turns the passing of time into the comedy’s subject; the final trio will make her act upon the knowledge expressed here. The German libretto preserves the speech in its theatrical context.

VIILineage

Strauss
Passed toArnold SchoenbergAlban BergErich Wolfgang KorngoldFilm orchestration

The young Schoenberg learned from Strauss’s treatment of the large orchestra, and Strauss initially assisted him. Their later compositional choices diverged. Berg’s operas retain the idea that motivic and harmonic organisation can articulate theatrical psychology; Korngold carried the soprano-centred lyricism and instrumental precision into both opera and cinema.
