ILife

Dvořák was born in Nelahozeves, a village north of Prague, where his father kept an inn and worked as a butcher. He learnt violin locally and acquired further musical training at Zlonice before attending the Prague Organ School in 1857–59. There was no sudden passage from village fiddler to celebrated composer. He earned his living in ensembles, including the orchestra of Prague’s Provisional Theatre from 1862 to 1871, playing viola under conductors who included Smetana. Opera taught him how a middle part supports singers, how repeated figures sustain a scene and how instrumental colour carries across a crowded theatre.

His early compositions were extensive and often ambitious: chamber music, symphonies and operatic projects accumulated before he had a dependable publisher. Wagner’s influence is audible in the scale and harmonic movement of some of these works, but it did not become a permanent rule. In 1873 he married Anna Čermáková. Teaching and an organist’s post helped support the household while applications for an Austrian state stipend brought his scores to influential readers. The award first received in 1875 was not merely an honour: it provided money at a point when publication and commissions remained uncertain.

Brahms and SimrockNational style without quotation

Brahms, involved in assessing the stipend applications, recommended Dvořák to the publisher Fritz Simrock. The Moravian Duets led to the commission for the first set of Slavonic Dances in 1878. Written initially for piano duet and then orchestrated, the dances made Dvořák known beyond the Czech lands. Unlike a collection of harmonised folk tunes, they largely use newly composed melodies. Their identity lies in rhythmic types, phrase lengths, turns of harmony and changes of accent. Simrock’s commercial interest could help the composer while also pressing him towards small, marketable works instead of the operas and symphonies he wanted to write.

Opus numbersDvořák’s opus numbers are not a reliable chronological sequence: early works sometimes appeared late with misleadingly high numbers. Burghauser’s thematic catalogue is the safer guide to the order of composition.

The Stabat Mater, developed in 1876–77 during years in which the Dvořáks lost three children, established a different public identity. Its large choral structure uses recurrence and tonal return to connect ten movements rather than treating the sacred text as a string of separate numbers. British performances led to visits and commissions from 1884 onwards. The Seventh Symphony, written for the Philharmonic Society in London, is a concentrated D minor argument whose cross-rhythms and motivic development resist the idea of Dvořák as a supplier of pleasant national colour. The piano chamber works likewise give the inner voices substantial argumentative work.

Prague and VysokáForms that accommodate contrast

Dvořák’s country home at Vysoká and his work in Prague supported a period of sustained composition. The A major Piano Quintet of 1887 balances a long cello melody with dense exchanges among the five players; its dumka alternates reflection and faster episodes. The Eighth Symphony, completed in 1889, begins with a G minor string melody before the flute brings in the G major world named by the title. The Dumky Trio of 1890–91 makes six contrasting movements from a related principle of abrupt changes in pace and character, without requiring a conventional four-movement sonata cycle.

America, 1892–95Listening before prescribing

Jeannette Thurber engaged him to direct New York’s National Conservatory of Music in 1892. The institution admitted women and African American students, among them Harry T. Burleigh, whose singing introduced Dvořák to spirituals at close quarters. Dvořák publicly argued that African American musical traditions could help form an American school of composition. The proposal was contentious in a segregated society and was sometimes misreported as an instruction to paste existing tunes into symphonies. His own statements distinguish composing in the character of a musical tradition from quoting its melodies.

“Goin’ Home” came laterThe Largo melody of the Ninth Symphony is Dvořák’s. William Arms Fisher fitted it with the words “Goin’ Home” in 1922; the symphony does not quote a pre-existing spiritual of that title.

The Ninth Symphony was written in New York between January and May 1893, before his summer in Spillville, Iowa. Its cor anglais theme uses a pentatonic collection, but so does music from many other places, including Bohemia. Longfellow’s The Song of Hiawatha was a documented literary stimulus, especially for the scherzo’s dance; it should not be mistaken for direct access to Indigenous musical practice. In Spillville, a Czech-speaking community, Dvořák wrote the F major String Quartet and E♭ major String Quintet. Their concise themes and transparent textures are different responses to the American period from the symphony’s cyclic orchestral argument.

The Cello Concerto was composed mainly in New York in 1894–95. It gives the soloist a large role without continuously thinning the orchestra to accompaniment. In the slow movement Dvořák quotes his song Leave Me Alone, associated with his sister-in-law Josefina; after her death he expanded the finale’s closing recollection. Personal memory is therefore documented in the score’s history, but it acts through an already coherent musical plan. The solo cello repeatedly rejoins other instruments in conversation rather than claiming every important melody as private property.

The final theatreBallads and an unspeaking heroine

He returned permanently to Bohemia in 1895. The following year brought symphonic poems after Karel Jaromír Erben’s ballads, including The Water Goblin, The Noon Witch, The Golden Spinning Wheel and The Wild Dove. Their violence is not incidental picturesque detail: repetitions, changes of instrumentation and speech-derived rhythms track events and consequences. His later concentration on opera included The Devil and Kate, Rusalka and Armida. The symphonic repertory’s modern dominance can conceal how persistently he sought a theatre audience.

Rusalka, composed in 1900 to Jaroslav Kvapil’s libretto, gives the orchestra a precise dramatic responsibility. The water nymph’s bargain makes her unable to speak in the human world, so remembered motives and the responses of other characters carry what she cannot say. The Song to the Moon is only one part of that larger problem of communication. Dvořák continued his connection with the Prague Conservatory and became its director in 1901. He died in Prague in May 1904, leaving a generation of pupils that included Josef Suk and Vítězslav Novák, and a body of operas that remained central in Czech theatres.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄾Dance & accent

A furiant can make two written bars of triple metre sound briefly like three groups of two beats. Dvořák places these competing accents inside movements that still have clear tonal and thematic plans. The Slavonic Dances depend less on borrowed tunes than on the listener recognising a physical pattern and hearing it disturbed.

𝅘𝅥Melody & modes

Triadic outlines, repeated notes and pentatonic collections make themes immediately graspable. The Ninth’s Largo initially avoids the fourth and seventh degrees of D♭ major; it therefore avoids two of the scale’s strongest semitone pulls. Such a collection is not a certificate of one nationality: its meaning depends on rhythm, accompaniment and context.

𝄋Development

Short motives pass between voices, change their bass and reappear in new harmonic positions. The Seventh Symphony is a particularly concentrated example. In the Ninth, the technique extends across movements: earlier themes recur in later ones and can be combined, making memory part of the finale’s construction.

𝄡Chamber texture

The former orchestral violist gives middle voices active material. In the A major Piano Quintet, melody and rhythmic support circulate between piano and strings; accompaniment can become the next subject. In the Cello Concerto, the soloist can join a wind line or a solo violin rather than continually competing with the entire ensemble.

𝄐Narrative & contrast

The dumka alternates reflective and animated episodes; Erben’s ballads require sharper changes as speech becomes action. In Rusalka, recurrent material lets the orchestra remember the heroine’s origin during scenes in which she cannot speak. These are distinct techniques for organising contrast, not a single national formula.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1876–77
    Stabat MaterOp. 58 · B 71Four vocal soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Ten movements connected by thematic and tonal returns; a decisive work in Dvořák’s British reception.
  2. 1878; 1886
    Slavonic Dances, two setsOpp. 46 and 72Piano four hands; orchestral versions by the composer
    Sixteen newly composed dances using characteristic Slavonic dance types; the melodies are not simply a set of folk-song quotations.
  3. 1884–85
    Symphony No. 7 in D minorOp. 70 · B 141Orchestra
    Written for London’s Philharmonic Society. Motives are compact, the rhythmic layers frequently conflict, and the final major turn is hard-won within the formal argument.
  4. 1887
    Piano Quintet No. 2 in A majorOp. 81 · B 155Piano, two violins, viola and cello
    A cello-led opening, an extended dumka and a furiant scherzo; inner parts remain participants rather than harmonic padding.
  5. 1889
    Symphony No. 8 in G majorOp. 88 · B 163Orchestra
    A G minor opening is answered by a G major flute idea. The finale’s variations change orchestral weight as well as the shape of the tune.
  6. 1893
    Symphony No. 9 in E minor “From the New World”Op. 95 · B 178Orchestra, including cor anglais
    Composed in New York and premiered there under Anton Seidl on 16 December 1893. The finale brings back themes from all three preceding movements.
  7. 1893
    String Quartet No. 12 in F major “American”Op. 96 · B 179Two violins, viola and cello
    Written at Spillville. The viola introduces the first theme; pentatonic outlines and clearly differentiated rhythmic figures keep the four parts transparent.
  8. 1894–95
    Cello Concerto in B minorOp. 104 · B 191Cello and orchestra
    A substantial orchestral exposition precedes the solo entry. The closing recollections include material associated with Josefina, the composer’s sister-in-law.
  9. 1896
    The Water GoblinOp. 107 · B 195Orchestra
    Erben’s ballad provides a narrative of marriage, captivity and revenge. Distinctive rhythmic figures act as recognisable agents in the unfolding story.
  10. 1900
    RusalkaOp. 114 · B 203Opera in three acts
    Jaroslav Kvapil’s libretto makes a water nymph’s loss of speech central to the drama. Premiered at Prague’s National Theatre in 1901.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Schwaiger’s water creature belongs to the same Czech folkloric vocabulary as Erben’s ballad and Dvořák’s 1896 symphonic poem. This is a shared subject, not a documented source for the composition. The figure gives bodily form to a being whose musical identity Dvořák instead establishes through a recurrent, sharply articulated orchestral motive. The National Gallery’s record dates the painting a decade before the score.

Karel Jaromír Erben

Vodník (The Water Goblin) — opening

1853
CzechNa topole nad jezerem seděl Vodník podvečerem: „Sviť, měsíčku, sviť, ať mi šije niť. Šiju, šiju si botičky do sucha i do vodičky: Sviť, měsíčku, sviť, ať mi šije niť.“
Our translationOn a poplar above the lake the Water Goblin sat at evening: “Shine, little moon, shine, so my thread can sew. I sew, I sew myself little boots for dry ground and for water: shine, little moon, shine, so my thread can sew.”

The harmless domestic labour precedes abduction and a child’s death. Dvořák’s symphonic poem follows the ballad’s events closely enough for recurrent musical figures to acquire narrative functions. The opening’s short repeated phrases show why the poem offers a composer rhythmic material as well as a plot. Text: Oxford’s Czech and Slovak resources on Kytice.

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow

The Song of Hiawatha — the wedding dance

1855
EnglishTo the sound of flutes and singing, To the sound of drums and voices, Rose the handsome Pau-Puk-Keewis, And began his mystic dances. First he danced a solemn measure, Very slow in step and gesture, In and out among the pine-trees, Through the shadows and the sunshine,

Dvořák identified the feast and dancing in Hiawatha as a stimulus for the Ninth Symphony’s scherzo in his December 1893 New York Herald interview. Longfellow’s poem is a nineteenth-century literary construction using Indigenous materials, not an Indigenous voice to be treated as ethnographic evidence. The Dvořák catalogue’s discussion distinguishes the documented scherzo connection from less securely attested claims about the Largo.

Suk and Novák were pupils in Prague; Burleigh knew Dvořák at the National Conservatory in New York. The American legacy is not a single inherited style: Dvořák’s public argument helped legitimate African American source traditions within concert composition, while composers including William Grant Still made their own decisions about form, orchestration and cultural address.
