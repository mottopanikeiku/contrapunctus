17.1Life

Haydn was born in Rohrau, near the border between Lower Austria and Hungary, in 1732. His father was a wheelwright who sang and played the harp by ear; his mother had worked as a cook. Music began at home. After early schooling in Hainburg, he became a choirboy at St Stephen’s Cathedral in Vienna. The work gave him daily experience of church music and a singer’s understanding of phrases, but little systematic composition teaching. When his voice broke, he lost the position and had to earn his living outside the cathedral.

In the 1750s he taught, accompanied and played whatever engagements he could obtain. Working for the singing teacher and composer Nicola Porpora gave him practical instruction in Italian vocal style. He studied scores and keyboard music, including the sonatas of C. P. E. Bach, whose sudden contrasts offered another model besides the regular phrases of the fashionable galant style. Employment by Count Morzin in the late 1750s brought an orchestra and the first symphonies. In 1760 he married Maria Anna Keller; the marriage was unhappy and childless. His professional stability came from a different household.

EsterházyA laboratory with a payroll

In 1761 Prince Paul Anton Esterházy appointed Haydn vice-Kapellmeister. He became full Kapellmeister in 1766, under Paul Anton’s successor Nikolaus. His responsibilities included composing, rehearsing, directing performances, looking after instruments and dealing with musicians’ requests. The surviving contract even regulated his appearance and conduct. At Eisenstadt and the new palace of Eszterháza, the employer’s demands provided unusual continuity: Haydn could write for musicians whose abilities he knew and hear the results repeatedly. The early symphonies Le Matin, Le Midi and Le Soir draw solo players out of the orchestra.

The prince’s instrumentNikolaus played the baryton, a bowed bass instrument with additional strings that could be plucked. Haydn wrote a large repertory for it, especially trios with viola and cello. Patronage determined the very instruments for which he composed.

The palace had an opera theatre, and Haydn’s duties increasingly included adapting and conducting other composers’ operas as well as writing his own. Instrumental music was therefore one department of a theatrical working life. His symphonies learned to manage entrances, changes of pace and a listener’s mistaken assumptions. In the F♯ minor ‘Farewell’ Symphony of 1772, the finale turns into an Adagio and the players leave successively until two violins remain. The early biographical account connects the gesture with musicians who wanted to return to their families; the score itself makes departure part of the performance.

The six string quartets Op. 20, also from 1772, develop a less hierarchical ensemble. The cello can carry a theme, and three finales are fugues. Counterpoint lets four players sustain independent arguments. Op. 33, published in 1781–82, finds other ways to distribute attention through conversation, displaced accents and scherzos. The ‘Joke’ Quartet’s finale sets short fragments between pauses until the audience cannot be certain whether the piece has finished.

Beyond the estatePrint, Paris and Vienna

A new contract in 1779 gave Haydn greater freedom to sell his music outside the Esterházy establishment. Publishers, unauthorised copies and foreign commissions had already carried his name abroad; now he could cultivate those markets more directly. The six ‘Paris’ symphonies of 1785–86 were written for the Concert de la Loge Olympique, an orchestra much larger than his usual court forces. In Vienna he became a friend and colleague of Mozart, who dedicated six quartets to him in 1785. Each learnt from the other.

One theme, two functionsSome Haydn expositions bring back their opening idea in the dominant rather than introduce an entirely new lyrical tune. The change of key gives the theme a new formal function. Such ‘monothematic’ movements can contain many subsidiary ideas.

Nikolaus died in 1790, and his successor Anton reduced the musical establishment. Haydn retained his title and pension but gained freedom to travel. The violinist and impresario Johann Peter Salomon brought him to London in 1791. He returned for a second visit in 1794–95. The twelve London symphonies address a paying public through strong opening gestures, memorable themes, wind solos and large dynamic contrasts. The ‘Surprise’ chord catches that public timing in a single gesture. Haydn’s notebooks record a city of concerts, visits, social obligations and sharply observed practical details.

Late worksThe concert hall and the church

London also gave him experience of the large-scale Handel tradition. Back in Vienna, Baron Gottfried van Swieten supplied the German text for The Creation, drawing on an English libretto assembled from Genesis, the Psalms and Milton. Haydn’s opening ‘Representation of Chaos’ withholds the cadences that would make C minor stable. The first proclamation of light arrives in a forceful C major chord: the event is harmonic, orchestral and verbal at once. The oratorio received private performances in 1798 and its first public performance in Vienna in 1799. Haydn published it with German and English words.

He also resumed a reduced round of duties for Prince Nikolaus II, writing six late masses between 1796 and 1802. Within their symphonic scale, changes of tempo and scoring articulate the words of the Mass. The 1797 imperial hymn Gott erhalte Franz den Kaiser provided the slow movement of the ‘Emperor’ Quartet, where the melody remains recognisable while the other voices alter their relation to it. The Seasons, completed in 1801, replaced the biblical creation story with rural labour, weather, hunting and the passage of a human life, using van Swieten’s adaptation of James Thomson.

By the early 1800s Haydn’s declining health restricted composition; the unfinished quartet Op. 103 remained a pair of inner movements. His last public appearances were as an honoured listener. He died in Vienna in May 1809, during the French occupation. The works that reached Beethoven, his pupil in 1792–94, demonstrated how a movement can establish a rule, make an audience rely on it, and then change what that rule means.

17.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄋Form

A movement teaches the listener its materials and then alters their function. A theme first heard as an opening can return as a transition or a closing idea; the recapitulation may rewrite the route home. Sonata form unfolds through these expectations.

𝄾Rhythm

Repeated notes, unexpected rests and accents off the strong beat can make a regular metre seem to change underfoot. Minuets sometimes retain the three-beat bar while denying the dancer a dependable phrase. The placement of a note makes the joke.

♮Harmony

Long stretches of tonic and dominant make a distant key especially noticeable. Haydn can reinterpret a familiar theme in the minor, prolong a dominant beyond its expected release or introduce a remote harmony at a point of apparent closure. The opening of The Creation dramatises the withholding of a normal cadence.

𝄡Quartet texture

Four players exchange tasks. A cello theme can force the violin into accompaniment; an inner voice can supply the rhythmic disturbance that changes an entire phrase. The fugues of Op. 20 and the conversation of Op. 33 are different ways of giving the ensemble more than one active speaker.

𝄞Orchestration

The early day-time symphonies exploit named soloists within the court orchestra. The London works enlarge the public contrast between full ensemble and exposed wind or string lines. Timpani can launch an argument on their own, as in the roll at the start of Symphony No. 103.

𝄆Variation

Variations can change the surrounding texture while leaving a melody nearly intact, as in the ‘Emperor’ Quartet. The F minor keyboard variations alternate minor and major themes, then expand the return of the minor into a coda whose scale changes the balance of the whole piece.

  4. 1781

    The set containing the “Joke” and “Bird” quartets. Conversational exchanges and rhythmic misdirection keep all four players active.
  5. 1785–86

    An introduction, seven slow meditations and an earthquake. Commissioned for devotional use in Cádiz; the problem is how to sustain contrast across a sequence of slow movements.
  7. 1791–92

    The loud chord interrupts a quiet C major variation theme. The subsequent variations carry the movement’s argument forward.
  8. 1793

    A double-variation design alternating minor and major. Its expanded minor-key coda unsettles the apparent symmetry of the preceding sequence.
  9. 1795
    Symphony No. 104 in D major “London”Hob. I:104Orchestra
    The last numbered symphony. A severe D minor introduction precedes the major-key Allegro; the finale places its tune over a drone-like bass.
  10. 1796
    Trumpet Concerto in E♭ majorHob. VIIe:1Keyed trumpet and orchestra
    Written for Anton Weidinger. The soloist’s chromatic notes in the middle register advertise an instrument freed from the natural trumpet’s harmonic-series limits.
  11. 1796
    Missa in tempore belliHob. XXII:9Soloists, chorus and orchestra
    The “Mass in Time of War”. Timpani disturb the Agnus Dei’s appeal for peace; military signs enter the liturgical structure.
  12. 1797
    String Quartet in C major “Emperor”Op. 76 No. 3; Hob. III:77String quartet
    The slow movement varies Haydn’s new imperial hymn. The tune passes between players while counter-lines, register and texture change around it.
  13. 1796–98
    Die Schöpfung (The Creation)Hob. XXI:2Soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Creation narrated by three archangels, followed by Adam and Eve. Chaos, light, birds, beasts and the human voice receive distinct musical treatments.
  14. 1799–1801
    Die Jahreszeiten (The Seasons)Hob. XXI:3Three soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Van Swieten adapts James Thomson. Work songs, a summer storm, hunting horns and winter’s moral reflections fill four seasonal sections.

17.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

A later photograph of the palace complex where Haydn composed, rehearsed and directed opera for Prince Nikolaus. The image records the scale of the establishment. Court musicians worked within a household that also maintained servants, gardens, theatres and ceremonial rooms; Haydn’s symphonies and baryton trios were part of that system of daily expenditure.

Blake’s figure measures the world with compasses while Haydn’s oratorio makes an ordered world emerge through harmony. Contact is undocumented. Blake’s Urizen can represent restrictive rational law, whereas Haydn’s chorus celebrates creation. The print was made during Haydn’s second London visit, placing these opposed accounts of cosmic order in the same decade and city.

John Milton

Paradise Lost, Book VII — before the creation of humanity

1667; revised edition 1674
EnglishNow Heaven in all her glory shone, and rolled Her motions, as the great First Mover’s hand First wheeled their course; Earth in her rich attire Consummate lovely smiled; air, water, earth, By fowl, fish, beast, was flown, was swum, was walked, Frequent; and of the sixth day yet remained: There wanted yet the master-work, the end Of all yet done; a creature, who, not prone And brute as other creatures, but endued With sanctity of reason, might erect His stature, and, upright with front serene, Govern the rest, self-knowing

Milton, Genesis and the Psalms supplied the English source libretto that van Swieten adapted for The Creation. The passage moves from populated earth to the upright human figure, the same transition heard before Haydn’s tenor aria Mit Würd’ und Hoheit angetan. Text: Book VII.

17.8Lineage

Haydn
Passed toWolfgang Amadeus MozartLudwig van BeethovenFranz Schubert

Mozart’s six quartets dedicated to Haydn make the exchange explicit. Beethoven studied with Haydn but also revised the proportions of the symphony and quartet from inside forms that Haydn had kept changing throughout his career. Schubert inherited the small motif, the lyrical episode and the possibility that a return can alter the meaning of both.

Questions to take away

  1. What can one violin do above three players who hardly move?

    The C major quartet’s slow movement is in C minor: the first violin spins long ornamental lines over a sombre chordal accompaniment from the other three. It ends on a held G and runs straight into the Menuetto.

    String Quartet in C major, Op. 54 No. 2, Hob. III:57 · II. Adagio

  2. What happens to a finale that begins and ends slowly?

    The quartet ends slowly. A long Adagio, whose inner parts murmur in repeated sixteenths, gives way to a fifty-second Presto and then returns, shorter, to fade out. Shulman hears an experiment that looks ahead to Beethoven’s late quartets.

    String Quartet in C major, Op. 54 No. 2, Hob. III:57 · IV. Finale: Adagio — Presto — Adagio
