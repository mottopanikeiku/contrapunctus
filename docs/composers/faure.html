Life

The boy kept slipping into the chapel to play the harmonium. His father ran a teachers’ training school at Montgauzy, near Foix, and the little instrument in its chapel gave Gabriel Fauré his first musical happiness. In October 1854 his father took him to Paris and enrolled him at Louis Niedermeyer’s new school for church musicians. He was nine. For eleven years he lived among plainchant, organ exercises and the interweaving voices of Renaissance church music.

After Niedermeyer died in 1861, Camille Saint-Saëns took over the piano class. When the official lesson was done, he played the boys Schumann, Liszt and Wagner, composers far outside their syllabus. Fauré acquired a teacher, a champion and a friend who would stay beside him for sixty years. He left school in 1865 with prizes and the Cantique de Jean Racine: a French prayer unfolding over a flowing keyboard part, each new choral entry joining a phrase already under way.

1866–83After the sermon

His first post was at Saint-Sauveur in Rennes, where he played the organ from 1866 and taught piano to supplement his pay. The parish priest had grounds for doubting his devotion. Fauré slipped out during sermons for a cigarette, and in 1870 arrived for Mass in evening clothes after a night at a ball. He was asked to resign. Saint-Saëns helped him find another post in Paris; when war with Prussia broke out later that year, Fauré volunteered and fought in the actions to lift the siege of the city.

Back in Paris after the war, he worked at Saint-Sulpice with Charles-Marie Widor. The two sometimes improvised at the church’s separate organs, trying to catch each other with unexpected changes of key. He also joined the new Société Nationale de Musique, founded in 1871 to give French composers a hearing. Its concerts made room for the chamber music that the opera houses could not: his First Violin Sonata, warmly received in 1877, and the First Piano Quartet, begun the year before.

Pauline Viardot’s salon supplied another audience. Fauré fell in love with her daughter Marianne; they became engaged in July 1877, and she broke it off in November. That same year he became choirmaster at the Madeleine. In 1883 he married Marie Frémiet, daughter of the sculptor Emmanuel Frémiet; they had two sons. The services and private lessons paid for the household. His publisher bought songs outright, so the success of a song brought no growing stream of royalties.

Wagner at four handsFauré and André Messager’s Souvenirs de Bayreuth makes a ballroom quadrille from themes of the Ring. They played it together at parties.

He travelled with Messager to hear Wagner, taking in the Ring in Munich in 1879 and later visiting Bayreuth. Back at the piano, he could turn those formidable themes into a dance. His own First Piano Quartet has an equally mischievous scherzo: the strings pluck their chords while the piano skips between groups of two and three. In the slow movement the same four players produce a close, almost choral lament.

1887–94A different funeral

Three RequiemsThe five-movement score of 1888 grew into the seven-movement church version associated with 1893. The enlarged concert scoring was performed in 1900. Modern editions recover the smaller forces from surviving manuscripts and parts.

The Requiem was first heard at the Madeleine on 16 January 1888. Fauré had begun it the previous year and continued enlarging it through the 1890s. Its first orchestra was dark with low strings and organ; a solo violin shone above them in the Sanctus. The priest complained about the novelty. Fauré, who had spent years accompanying other people’s funeral music, had finally written a service he wanted to hear.

In a conversation with Louis Aguettant on 12 July 1902, he explained his view of death: “a happy deliverance, an aspiration towards happiness beyond”. The main body of the Dies irae, with its fire and judgement, is absent. There is fear in the baritone’s Libera me, and a violent choral interruption; the ending belongs to the sopranos, singing In paradisum over an organ figure that seems to keep the air moving beneath them. The final key is D major.

The flute of the Pavane, written in 1887, steps through its F♯ minor tune with exquisite manners; Robert de Montesquiou’s optional choral words give the dancers jealousies and petty quarrels. La bonne chanson is less well behaved. Fauré wrote its nine Verlaine settings in 1892–94 during his affair with the singer Emma Bardac. She tried out the songs as he brought them to her. The piano remembers melodies from earlier songs, the keys change under the singer’s feet, and the final song gathers the lovers’ happiness into spring.

1896–1920The outsider takes charge

In 1896 he became principal organist at the Madeleine and succeeded Massenet as composition professor at the Conservatoire. Ravel, Charles Koechlin, Florent Schmitt and Nadia Boulanger passed through his class. When the actress Mrs Patrick Campbell needed music for her London production of Pelléas et Mélisande in 1898, Koechlin helped his teacher with the hurried orchestration. In the concert suite, a flute and harp carry the Sicilienne; the spinning music keeps the strings turning beneath an oboe.

In June 1905, a month after the scandal over Ravel’s exclusion from the Prix de Rome final, Fauré took over from Théodore Dubois as director of the Conservatoire. He had never been a Conservatoire student. He brought in outside judges for examinations and competitions, upsetting professors accustomed to favouring their own private pupils, and widened the music taught there. The old guard called him Robespierre. His working day still left composition waiting for the summer holidays, when he escaped to a hotel by a lake.

Hearing trouble had begun in 1903. It became more than deafness: high and low notes sounded distorted and out of tune, so that a performance could betray the music he knew was on the page. He continued teaching and composing, then retired from the Conservatoire in 1920. In 1922 the President of the Republic attended a public tribute at the Sorbonne. Fauré sat through a concert of his own works that he could scarcely hear.

The late musicWithout the piano

Pénélope, first performed in Monte Carlo in 1913, gave him three acts in which to sustain a woman’s waiting. Wagner’s example is audible in the returning themes; Penelope’s voice still moves with the patience and breathing of a Fauré song. In the Second Piano Quintet, completed in 1921, the viola begins its long melody over C minor arpeggios. The piano’s motion continues as the strings enter, until a sudden chordal declaration stops the flow.

That same year he set four poems by Jean de La Ville de Mirmont, killed in the First World War, as L’Horizon chimérique. They are full of ships, distant places and departures dreamed of. Fauré was now in his seventies; his poet had died at twenty-seven. He dedicated the cycle to the young baritone Charles Panzéra, who first sang it in 1922 with his wife Magdeleine at the piano.

An old melody changedThe final String Quartet reuses themes from an abandoned violin concerto of 1878. In one of them Fauré changed F♯ to F♮ within E minor: a Phrygian inflection, with the second note of the scale only a semitone above the first.

On 9 September 1923, from Annecy, he told Marie that he had begun a string quartet, “without piano”. Beethoven had made the genre so formidable, he added, that everybody else had “the jitters”. After a lifetime of chamber works with a keyboard, he was alone with four strings. The viola speaks first, the violin answers, and in the Andante the phrases swell and recede almost bar by bar.

He finished the quartet in September 1924. Offered a private performance, he refused: his damaged hearing would have spoiled it. He died in Paris on 4 November. At his state funeral four days later, the Madeleine heard the Requiem again.

The Sound

♮Harmony

Fauré can change the key while a melody appears to be standing still. In La bonne chanson, the piano’s inner notes keep moving and familiar phrases come back in fresh chords. The connecting line is often tucked between the tune and the bass. This is harmony made by voices finding their way: one small step can open a surprisingly distant door.

𝄡Modes & church music

The final String Quartet answers its opening viola phrase with a violin melody containing F♮ in E minor. That little semitone gives it a Phrygian cast. Fauré’s schooling had made such modal turns ordinary musical speech. In the Requiem, the tenors’ unison prayer and the sustained low strings bring the habits of the church choir directly into the concert hall.

𝅘𝅥Rhythm

The First Piano Quartet’s scherzo changes between 6/8 and 2/4: three little notes to a beat become two, and the floor seems to shift beneath the dance. The strings pluck while the piano runs; in the trio they put on mutes. Fauré can make a thoroughly respectable chamber ensemble sound as if it has briefly forgotten how to walk.

𝄞Song

In Clair de lune the piano has already established its minuet before the singer enters. The voice follows Verlaine’s French, while the dance pursues its own tune below. Neither part has to copy the other’s breathing. In La bonne chanson, remembered melodies pass through the accompaniment and bind nine poems together, even when the singer has moved on to new words.

𝄢Chamber writing

The Second Piano Quintet begins with viola over piano arpeggios; the first violin is only one of the voices that join it. Later, the Andante starts with the four strings alone before the piano enters. Fauré keeps changing who carries the tune. The final String Quartet begins with another viola phrase, answered by the violin, and makes the exchange work without any keyboard.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1865
    Cantique de Jean RacineOp. 11Mixed chorus and keyboard; later orchestral versions
    A graduation work awarded the École Niedermeyer’s composition prize. Racine’s French paraphrase of the Latin hymn Consors paterni luminis rises through successive choral entries over a flowing keyboard accompaniment.
  2. 1875–76
    Violin Sonata No. 1 in A majorOp. 13Violin and piano
    Its successful first performance at the Société Nationale in 1877 helped establish Fauré as a composer. The quick scherzo sets the two players darting around one another.
  3. 1876–79; finale revised 1883
    Piano Quartet No. 1 in C minorOp. 15Piano, violin, viola and cello
    A C minor lament and a wonderfully skittish scherzo share four movements. Fauré rewrote the finale in 1883, three years after the first performance.
  4. 1887
    Pavane in F♯ minorOp. 50Orchestra, with optional mixed chorus
    A flute leads the stately dance. The optional chorus, to words by Robert de Montesquiou, adds flirtation and bad temper to its perfect manners.
  5. 1887–1900
    Requiem in D minorOp. 48Soprano and baritone soloists, chorus, organ and orchestra; several versions
    First heard at the Madeleine in 1888, then enlarged in stages. Low strings and organ support the prayers; In paradisum brings the ending into D major.
    Hear it in the Hall →
  6. 1892–94
    La bonne chansonOp. 61Voice and piano; later version with piano and string quintet
    Nine Verlaine songs written for Emma Bardac, who tried them out as Fauré composed them. Returning melodies make the final song remember the whole cycle.
  7. 1894
    Nocturne No. 6 in D♭ majorOp. 63Piano
    The opening melody takes long, irregular breaths; a central tune floats high over quick semiquavers. The first idea returns quietly after the storm has spent itself.
  8. 1898
    Pelléas et MélisandeOp. 80Incidental music for orchestra; concert suite
    Written for Mrs Patrick Campbell’s London staging, with help from Koechlin. The suite keeps the spinning wheel, the flute’s Sicilienne and Mélisande’s funeral music.
  9. 1919–21
    Piano Quintet No. 2 in C minorOp. 115Piano and string quartet
    The viola opens over restless piano arpeggios. Its second movement is a scherzo in which plucked notes, rapid bowing and longer melodies pass through one another without stopping.
  10. 1923–24
    String Quartet in E minorOp. 121Two violins, viola and cello
    His last work, completed in September 1924, begins with the viola and ends in E major. Fauré’s damaged hearing kept him from hearing a performance.

Sister Arts

Sargent played the piano, admired Fauré and helped his music find an audience in England. He inscribed this portrait to the composer as an affectionate souvenir. The black coat and grey background leave the face, turned slightly away, to do almost all the talking.

Mezzetin sings to his guitar while the garden statue behind him turns away. Verlaine borrowed the world of Watteau’s lovers and entertainers for Fêtes galantes. Fauré’s Clair de lune puts a minuet beneath its masked dancers: the piano maintains the social occasion while the voice admits how sad they are.

Paul Verlaine

Clair de lune

1869; set by Fauré in 1887
FrenchVotre âme est un paysage choisi Que vont charmant masques et bergamasques, Jouant du luth et dansant et quasi Tristes sous leurs déguisements fantasques. Tout en chantant sur le mode mineur L’amour vainqueur et la vie opportune, Ils n’ont pas l’air de croire à leur bonheur Et leur chanson se mêle au clair de lune, Au calme clair de lune triste et beau, Qui fait rêver les oiseaux dans les arbres Et sangloter d’extase les jets d’eau, Les grands jets d’eau sveltes parmi les marbres.
Our translationYour soul is a chosen landscape Which masks and bergamask dancers go enchanting, Playing the lute and dancing and almost Sad beneath their fanciful disguises. While singing in the minor mode Of conquering love and accommodating life, They do not seem to believe in their happiness, And their song mingles with the moonlight, With the quiet moonlight, sad and beautiful, Which makes the birds dream in the trees And the fountains sob with ecstasy, The tall, slender fountains among the marble statues.

Fauré set this poem in 1887 as Op. 46 No. 2. The piano starts the dance before the voice enters, then keeps its own melody going under the words. Verlaine’s revellers sing about happiness while scarcely believing in it; the fountains are left to sob.

Lineage

Drew fromCamille Saint-SaënsLouis NiedermeyerRobert SchumannFrédéric ChopinRenaissance church polyphony
Fauré
Passed toMaurice RavelCharles KoechlinNadia BoulangerFlorent Schmitt

Saint-Saëns opened the school piano to Schumann, Liszt and Wagner, then helped Fauré find work. Fauré’s own pupils took different paths: Ravel, Koechlin, Schmitt and Nadia Boulanger all passed through his Conservatoire class. Ravel dedicated his String Quartet to him in 1903; twenty years later the teacher finally began one of his own.
