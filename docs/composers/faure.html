ILife

Fauré learned his trade in a school for church musicians, not in the Paris Conservatoire. His father was a schoolmaster in the Ariège; at nine, Gabriel was sent to Louis Niedermeyer’s recently founded school in Paris, where plainchant, Renaissance polyphony, organ playing and counterpoint belonged to the ordinary curriculum. The education matters to the harmony. A melody need not sharpen its seventh degree to make a minor-key cadence; a chord can emerge from the independent movement of voices rather than announce a change of key. These possibilities were practical knowledge before they became a recognisable Fauré style.

After Niedermeyer died in 1861, Camille Saint-Saëns took over the piano class and introduced the pupils to Schumann, Liszt and Wagner. Teacher and pupil remained friends for more than sixty years. Fauré’s graduation work, the Cantique de Jean Racine of 1865, already joins the two educations: a flowing keyboard accompaniment beneath a choral texture whose entries are controlled by contrapuntal discipline. Its French text is Racine’s paraphrase of a Latin hymn, not a poem written for the concert hall. The bass supports long phrases without requiring the upper voices to stop at every bar-line.

The working musicianChurch, salon and chamber society

An organist’s post at Saint-Sauveur in Rennes followed in 1866. Fauré returned to Paris, served during the Franco-Prussian War, and in 1871 became involved with the new Société Nationale de Musique. Its concerts gave French composers a place for instrumental music outside an operatic establishment. The First Violin Sonata, completed in 1876, and the First Piano Quartet belong to that campaign. Their pianos are not orchestras in miniature: running figures, syncopated chords and independent bass lines provide motion against which the strings can sustain and exchange a melody.

A pianist’s rhythmFauré repeatedly places a melody across the accompaniment’s grouping. The resulting instability may come from accents and phrase-length, even when every note belongs to the key.

In 1877 he became choirmaster at the Madeleine and was briefly engaged to Marianne Viardot, daughter of the singer Pauline Viardot. Her family’s salon brought him into contact with performers, writers and patrons; the broken engagement did not remove him from that network. He married Marie Frémiet, daughter of the sculptor Emmanuel Frémiet, in 1883; they had two sons. Church duties and teaching paid the bills. The short piano piece and the song were forms he could cultivate between appointments, though the chamber works show that their concentration was not a limitation of ambition.

He travelled with Saint-Saëns and André Messager to hear Wagner, including the Ring in Munich in 1879. With Messager he made the four-hand Souvenirs de Bayreuth, a quadrille which subjects Wagner’s motifs to ballroom rhythms. Admiration and irreverence were compatible. Unlike the many French works built around an imitation of Tristan, Fauré’s mature music rarely makes chromatic tension a continuous emergency. A remote chord may arrive through one altered note, and the melody can continue as if its surroundings had always been there.

The 1880s and 1890sPrayer without a theatre of judgement

The Requiem began taking shape in the 1880s and received its first performance at the Madeleine in January 1888. Fauré enlarged it in stages; the familiar full-orchestra version appeared in 1900. He omitted the main body of the Dies irae sequence and ended with In paradisum, a text from the burial service rather than the Mass itself. Judgement still breaks into the baritone’s Libera me, but it does not organise the entire work. Low strings, organ and choral unison establish the centre of gravity; the solo violin in the Sanctus changes the light without requiring a new dramatic character.

Which Requiem?The 1888, expanded 1890s and full-orchestra versions differ in movements and scoring. Modern recordings of the smaller versions also depend on editorial reconstruction; their differences are not simply matters of tempo.

The Pavane of 1887 puts an F♯ minor flute melody over a measured accompaniment; its optional chorus adds Robert de Montesquiou’s teasing account of courtly lovers. In La bonne chanson, written in 1892–94 for Emma Bardac, the relationship between voice and piano is more unsettled. Nine Verlaine poems become a cycle through returning musical ideas, while changes of key continually revise a phrase’s meaning. The piano is responsible for remembering earlier songs. A listener who follows only the vocal line misses much of the argument.

In 1896 Fauré succeeded Théodore Dubois as principal organist at the Madeleine and Massenet as composition professor at the Conservatoire. His pupils included Ravel, Charles Koechlin, Florent Schmitt and Nadia Boulanger. He did not require them to reproduce his harmonic vocabulary. The class transmitted habits of voice-leading, proportion and critical listening that could support very different compositions. His incidental music for Sarah Bernhardt’s London production of Pelléas et Mélisande followed in 1898, with Koechlin helping to orchestrate music written against a theatrical deadline.

The directorNew institutions, damaged hearing

Fauré became Conservatoire director in 1905, after the controversy surrounding Ravel’s exclusion from the final round of the Prix de Rome. He changed appointments and teaching practices in an institution he had never attended as a student. Meanwhile, hearing trouble first evident in 1903 grew severe. The problem included distortion of pitch as well as loss of volume: the sounding result could contradict the music he knew internally. He continued composing, but eventually stopped attending performances. Deafness contributed to his retirement from the directorship in 1920.

The opera Pénélope, first performed in Monte Carlo in 1913, brings his methods to a three-act scale. Its returning themes and orchestral continuity acknowledge Wagner, while Penelope’s long vocal lines retain the measured inflection of French song. The later chamber music is more compressed. In the Second Piano Quintet, completed in 1921, the strings unfold above the piano’s persistent motion, and the scherzo passes its rapid figures between instruments rather than treating them as accompaniment to a single soloist. The music’s difficulties lie in balance, pulse and harmonic direction as much as in speed.

The late registerThe late piano and chamber works often reduce the conspicuous decoration. Their chromatic inner parts become more, not less, important: a plain surface can conceal a continuously changing bass.

L’Horizon chimérique of 1921 sets poems by Jean de La Ville de Mirmont, killed in the First World War. Its sea and departures are expressed through a singer’s breath and a piano part, not through orchestral illustration. Fauré finally wrote his only String Quartet in 1923–24, removing the piano on which so much of his chamber thinking had depended. He declined to hear it played because of his damaged hearing. He died in Paris that November and received a state funeral at the Madeleine, where colleagues performed the Requiem he had first written for the church’s ordinary services.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Harmony

Common notes connect chords whose roots lie far apart; chromatic inner voices make the connection sound continuous. In La bonne chanson, a sung note can remain fixed while the piano changes its harmonic meaning. The cadence is often postponed by voice-leading rather than by an emphatic dominant pedal.

𝄡Modes & church music

His Niedermeyer training made modal melody and contrapuntal church styles available alongside major–minor tonality. Flattened sevenths and plagal movement can soften the dominant–tonic hierarchy without abolishing a tonal centre. The Requiem gains weight from unison and sustained bass notes, not from constant chromatic saturation.

𝅘𝅥Rhythm

Accompaniment and phrase seldom agree completely about the bar-line. Syncopated chords, cross-accents and continuous semiquavers keep the piano moving beneath a vocal or string line that breathes more slowly. In the First Piano Quartet’s scherzo, articulation and displaced accents are structural information.

𝄞Song

French vowels determine the length and placement of a melodic gesture; the piano supplies continuities that the words cannot state. In a cycle, a returning accompaniment or interval can connect poems without repeating their text. Fauré’s late songs often restrict the vocal line while allowing the harmony underneath it to travel.

𝄢Chamber texture

The piano carries active middle voices and bass movement, not merely chords under the strings. In the late quintets, thematic strands migrate between instruments; ensemble balance must let an inner part emerge without turning every entry into a solo. The final String Quartet retains this interdependence after the keyboard has gone.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1865
    Cantique de Jean RacineOp. 11Mixed chorus and keyboard; later orchestral versions
    A French paraphrase of the hymn Consors paterni luminis. Choral entries grow over a continuous accompaniment; written at the École Niedermeyer.
  2. 1875–76
    Violin Sonata No. 1 in A majorOp. 13Violin and piano
    A four-movement sonata with a full, active piano part. The scherzo’s rapid exchanges require rhythmic agreement rather than a soloist with accompanist.
  3. 1876–79; finale revised 1883
    Piano Quartet No. 1 in C minorOp. 15Piano, violin, viola and cello
    Syncopation destabilises the first movement’s firm opening; the slow movement turns the same ensemble towards sustained, closely voiced lament.
  4. 1887
    Pavane in F♯ minorOp. 50Orchestra, with optional mixed chorus
    Flute melody and restrained dance rhythm; the optional Montesquiou text introduces the quarrels and poses of a courtly gathering.
  5. 1887–1900
    Requiem in D minorOp. 48Soprano and baritone soloists, chorus, organ and orchestra; several versions
    From the dark opening to In paradisum. Compare the smaller scoring with the expanded 1900 version rather than assuming one standard orchestral text.
  6. 1892–94
    La bonne chansonOp. 61Voice and piano; later version with piano and string quintet
    Nine Verlaine settings joined by recurring themes. Piano memories and rapid tonal changes bind the poems into a single work.
  7. 1894
    Nocturne No. 6 in D♭ majorOp. 63Piano
    A long-breathed opening gives way to intensified figuration and harmonic movement; the return is heard after that disturbance, not as an untouched repeat.
  8. 1898
    Pelléas et MélisandeOp. 80Incidental music for orchestra; concert suite
    Written for the London staging with Sarah Bernhardt. The suite includes the spinning music, the Sicilienne and Mélisande’s death.
  9. 1919–21
    Piano Quintet No. 2 in C minorOp. 115Piano and string quartet
    A concentrated late work with a fleet scherzo and an expansive Andante moderato. Listen below the first violin for the movement of the middle voices.
  10. 1923–24
    String Quartet in E minorOp. 121Two violins, viola and cello
    His final work, in three movements. The Andante makes sustained lines and changes of harmonic footing carry the expression without keyboard figuration.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Sargent was a pianist as well as a painter, knew Fauré personally and helped introduce his music to English musical circles. This portrait records a working friendship, not a resemblance invented after the fact. The dark suit and almost unpainted background concentrate attention on the head: compare the social setting of Fauré’s songs with the density of the musical argument they accommodated.

Watteau’s couples supply the eighteenth-century pictorial vocabulary behind Verlaine’s Fêtes galantes: outdoor entertainments, staged courtship and a departure whose direction is not entirely settled. Fauré’s Clair de lune and Pavane revisit that social theatre. This painting is a context for their masquerade, not a documented illustration commissioned for either score.

Clair de lune

1869; set by Fauré in 1887
FrenchVotre âme est un paysage choisi Que vont charmant masques et bergamasques, Jouant du luth et dansant et quasi Tristes sous leurs déguisements fantasques. Tout en chantant sur le mode mineur L’amour vainqueur et la vie opportune, Ils n’ont pas l’air de croire à leur bonheur Et leur chanson se mêle au clair de lune, Au calme clair de lune triste et beau, Qui fait rêver les oiseaux dans les arbres Et sangloter d’extase les jets d’eau, Les grands jets d’eau sveltes parmi les marbres.
Our translationYour soul is a chosen landscape Which masks and bergamask dancers go enchanting, Playing the lute and dancing and almost Sad beneath their fanciful disguises. While singing in the minor mode Of conquering love and accommodating life, They do not seem to believe in their happiness, And their song mingles with the moonlight, With the quiet moonlight, sad and beautiful, Which makes the birds dream in the trees And the fountains sob with ecstasy, The tall, slender fountains among the marble statues.

Fauré’s setting, Op. 46 No. 2, begins with a piano introduction long enough to establish the dance before the singer enters. Debussy set the same poem twice and later used its title for a piano piece. The text names the minor mode but does not prescribe a musical form; the important contradiction is between an enacted happiness and the dancers’ disbelief.

VIILineage

Fauré
Passed toMaurice RavelCharles KoechlinNadia BoulangerFlorent Schmitt

Fauré taught composers who did not sound alike. Ravel took contrapuntal precision into more sharply outlined instrumental colour; Nadia Boulanger carried the discipline of harmonic hearing into an international teaching practice. His own late works belong beside, rather than behind, the early twentieth-century music of Debussy.
