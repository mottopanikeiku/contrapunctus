Life

In August 1865, Janáček’s mother took her eleven-year-old son to Brno and left him at the Augustinian monastery. His father, a schoolmaster in Hukvaldy, was ill, and the family needed the scholarship. Nearly sixty years later, in his autobiographical sketch of 1924, Janáček recalled “a hard bed, harder bread. No caresses.” The monastery’s choirmaster, Pavel Křížkovský, taught him music. The boy sang at services, went to concerts and the theatre, and learned the profession that kept him in Brno for the rest of his life.

He trained as a teacher, studied at the Prague Organ School, and attended the conservatories in Leipzig and Vienna. In Prague he also formed a lasting friendship with Dvořák. Back in Brno he taught, conducted the Beseda choir and organised concerts; in 1881 he founded the Organ School, which he directed until 1919. His programmes included Beethoven’s Missa solemnis and Dvořák’s Stabat mater.

1881–1903Olga, Vladimír and Jenůfa

He married his former piano pupil Zdeňka Schulzová in 1881. They had a daughter, Olga, and a son, Vladimír, who died aged two in 1890. Olga died in February 1903, at twenty, while her father was finishing Jenůfa. The marriage continued. In his 1924 recollection Janáček imagined binding the opera “with the black ribbon of the long illness, pain and lamentation of my daughter Olga and my little boy Vladimír”. He dedicated it to Olga.

He had begun Jenůfa in 1894, adapting Gabriela Preissová’s play Její pastorkyňa (Her Stepdaughter), and he kept her prose. In the play a young woman bears a child outside marriage, and her stepmother, the Kostelnička, drowns the baby to save her from disgrace. The child is found beneath the melting ice on Jenůfa’s wedding day. In the first act a xylophone plays a repeated figure beneath the singing, representing the mill wheel. In the last act Jenůfa forgives the woman who killed her son, and the opera ends with Jenůfa and Laca together.

The notebooksJanáček’s speech melodies

Nápěvky mluvyJanáček called his notated scraps of conversation “speech melodies”. He often added the date, place and speaker’s circumstances beside the stave.

From 1897 he filled notebooks with the rise and fall of ordinary voices, heard in the market, on trains and in the street. His collection grew to more than four thousand entries and included barking dogs, mosquitoes and creaking floorboards. He held that the words alone were insufficient, and that a repeated syllable or a sudden high note could disclose anxiety that the sentence concealed. On 25 January 1925, on Brno’s Špilberk hill, he stopped to record a passing woman’s pleased remark about the view.

He had already spent years collecting Moravian songs with František Bartoš, and in time he brought a phonograph into the work. Their major joint collection appeared in 1901. In his own music the short phrases and uneven accents remained long after he stopped relying on folk-song arrangements. His male choruses on poems by Petr Bezruč deal with poverty, dispossession and national oppression, and were sung by the Moravian Teachers’ Choir. Writing to Bezruč on 1 October 1924, he recalled making from the poems “a storm of rage, despair and pain in sound”.

1904–16Jenůfa in Brno and Prague

Brno staged Jenůfa on 21 January 1904. Prague had refused it, and twelve more years passed before it reached the capital. Meanwhile Janáček continued teaching and composing in Moravia. His piano sonata 1. X. 1905 commemorated František Pavlík, who was killed during demonstrations for a Czech university in Brno. Janáček destroyed its final movement and later threw away the rest. The first two movements survive because the pianist Ludmila Tučková had kept a copy of them.

Whose orchestration?Karel Kovařovic altered Jenůfa for Prague, especially its instrumental writing. Charles Mackerras and John Tyrrell later restored the Brno version; Mackerras’s Decca recording includes Kovařovic’s fuller final scene as an appendix.

On 26 May 1916 the National Theatre performed the opera, with Kovařovic conducting his revised score. Janáček was sixty-one. Max Brod heard it, translated the libretto into German and brought it to Universal Edition. Vienna followed in 1918, New York in 1924.

1917–28Kamila and the theatre

At Luhačovice in 1917 he met Kamila Stösslová, a married woman thirty-seven years younger than he was. He wrote to her for the rest of his life, associated her with the women in his operas and imagined, in his letters, a life they shared. On 1 February 1928 he announced a new quartet: “Our life will be in it.” He first called it Love Letters and planned to use a viola d’amore. It became Intimate Letters, with an ordinary viola, which has many exposed passages.

Janáček had long read Russian literature. Ostrovsky’s The Storm became Káťa Kabanová, first performed in 1921; its heroine looks for love outside her marriage and is driven to her death. Tolstoy’s The Kreutzer Sonata became the First String Quartet in 1923. Its bowing can change within seconds from a sung line to a scraping, rapid repetition. In the opera The Makropulos Affair (1926), after Karel Čapek, a legal dispute leads to the story of a woman who has lived for three centuries.

The Cunning Little Vixen came from the newspaper. The Janáčeks’ servant Marie Stejskalová remembered laughing over Stanislav Lolek’s drawings and Rudolf Těsnohlídek’s story in Lidové noviny, then handing the paper to her employer. The opera reached the Brno stage in 1924. Among the animals, the foxes marry and the hens squabble. The humans drink, and Harašta shoots the vixen. In the final scene the Forester meets a little frog, which has to explain to him that it is the grandson of the one he remembers.

The last summersSinfonietta, Glagolitic Mass, last opera

The Sinfonietta of 1926 began as a fanfare for the Sokol gymnastics festival. Janáček had in mind a military band he had heard in Písek. The opening is scored for nine trumpets, two bass trumpets, two tenor tubas and timpani, and the full orchestra joins these instruments when the fanfare returns at the end. That summer at Luhačovice he also wrote most of the Glagolitic Mass, a setting of Old Church Slavonic words for soloists, chorus, orchestra and organ. The tenor part has high, exposed phrases, and near the end the organ has a movement to itself.

The final operaFor the 1930 première of From the House of the Dead, Břetislav Bakala, Osvald Chlubna and Ota Zítek altered the score and made its ending more celebratory. Later editions restored the prisoners’ return to work.

In July 1928 Janáček took From the House of the Dead to Hukvaldy to continue revising it. He had made the libretto himself from Dostoevsky’s Russian. In the opera prisoners tell their stories, chains rattle in the orchestra, and an injured eagle recovers and is released. Most of the men remain behind. Kamila and her son joined him in Hukvaldy. He caught pneumonia and died in Ostrava on 12 August, leaving the final revisions unfinished.

The Sound

𝅘𝅥Speech & melody

Janáček’s singers repeat a note, leap upwards, break off and start again. In Jenůfa the Kostelnička has to plead, lie and confess, and the length and pitch of each phrase follow what she is doing at that moment. He had trained his ear for character in his speech notebooks. A change of mind can take a few syllables, sung before the orchestra has finished its repeating figure.

𝄆Repetition

The xylophone at the start of Jenůfa repeats its figure while the voices sing around it. Short repeated figures of this kind run through Janáček’s music, and they can suggest a machine, a dance step or a recurring thought. He moves them between instruments or changes the chords beneath them, while the repeated notes themselves stay the same.

♮Harmony

A Janáček phrase can be tuneful and then end on an unexpected chord. In the Sinfonietta, short dance phrases meet with little preparation, and the brass can interrupt the strings before a cadence arrives. Held notes and repeated figures make these collisions audible, and familiar material is heard within a new chord.

𝄞Orchestration

The Sinfonietta opens with brass and timpani alone; the strings enter later. Low trombones play agile passages, and high woodwind cut through the orchestra. Janáček wrote for the awkward edge of an instrument. In the Glagolitic Mass, the organ plays solo, without the singers.

𝄋Dramatic shape

In The Cunning Little Vixen, a scene in the forest can be followed by men drinking at the inn; the animals continue their lives while the humans grow older. From the House of the Dead passes from one prisoner’s story to another. In both operas Janáček built the evening from these encounters, and short musical figures return so that characters and situations can be recognised.

Works

A selection from the composer’s works.

  1. 1894–1903; revised later
    JenůfaOpera in three acts
    Gabriela Preissová’s village tragedy, set in prose and dedicated to Janáček’s daughter Olga. In the first act a xylophone plays the figure of the mill wheel.
  2. 1900–11
    On an Overgrown PathPiano
    Two series of short pieces. The first ends with The Barn Owl Has Not Flown Away! The pieces draw on childhood recollections and the fear of death.
  3. 1915–18
    Taras BulbaOrchestra with organ
    Three episodes from Gogol, each ending in a death: Andrei, Ostap and finally their father Taras. The last movement follows the execution with an orchestral proclamation on a large scale.
    Recordings in the Hall
  4. 1919–21
    Káťa KabanováOpera in three acts
    Based on Ostrovsky’s The Storm, compressed into three acts. Káťa wants freedom and comes into conflict with her mother-in-law, the Kabanicha, who rules the household.
  5. 1922–23
    The Cunning Little VixenOpera in three acts
    A newspaper serial became an opera after the Janáčeks’ servant drew his attention to it. The opera ends with a conversation between the Forester and a small frog.
  6. 1923
    String Quartet No. 1 “The Kreutzer Sonata”String quartet
    Tolstoy’s story of jealousy and murder was the starting point. Singing phrases are cut short by repeated notes, tremolo and sudden changes of speed.
  7. 1926
    SinfoniettaOrchestra and additional brass ensemble
    The opening fanfare needs nine trumpets, two bass trumpets, two tenor tubas and timpani. It returns at the end with the full orchestra behind it.
  8. 1926; revised 1927
    Glagolitic MassFour vocal soloists, mixed chorus, orchestra and organ
    The Mass in Old Church Slavonic, with a solo for organ near the end. First performed in Brno in December 1927 and revised around the time of that première.
  9. 1927–28
    From the House of the DeadOpera in three acts
    Dostoevsky’s prison memoir supplied the inmates and their stories. Janáček died before completing his revisions; the 1930 première altered the ending.
  10. 1928
    String Quartet No. 2 “Intimate Letters”String quartet
    Written for Kamila Stösslová and originally conceived with a viola d’amore. The ordinary viola retained a leading role in the finished quartet.

Sister Arts

The girl in Kyjov costume cups a hand to her ear to catch a bird’s song. Mucha made this poster for the choir that performed Janáček’s demanding male choruses abroad. Its singers were Moravian teachers. Janáček had himself spent years teaching in Brno.

Lolek drew the foxes before Těsnohlídek wrote their story. The illustrated serial appeared in Lidové noviny in 1920. Marie Stejskalová, the Janáčeks’ servant, remembered laughing over it in the kitchen and suggesting that her employer make an opera. The comic animal scenes of the drawings were carried into the opera in the hens and the foxes’ wedding.

Lineage

Janáček
Passed toBéla BartókBenjamin BrittenGyörgy KurtágTwentieth-century Czech opera

Křížkovský trained the chorister; Dvořák became the young conductor’s friend. Janáček drew material from Moravian singers and Russian books, and his voice was unlike either man’s. Bartók was a fellow collector; Britten’s stage characters and Kurtág’s compressed pieces are later neighbours. Mackerras and Tyrrell restored Janáček’s scores for opera houses.
