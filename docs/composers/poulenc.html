ILife

Poulenc was born into a Paris family supported by the chemical business of his father, Émile. His mother, Jenny, taught him piano and introduced him to Mozart, Chopin and the popular songs heard at home. Concerts and the Opéra-Comique supplied a second education; Stravinsky’s ballets became an early and lasting enthusiasm. He did not attend the Conservatoire. From 1914 the pianist Ricardo Viñes taught him and introduced him to musicians including Satie. Viñes’s control of articulation and pedal mattered to Poulenc’s later insistence that a seemingly simple accompaniment still required exact playing.

The Rapsodie nègre of 1917 brought him public attention at eighteen. Its title and fabricated exotic text belong to the period’s colonial primitivism. The small ensemble and abrupt formal sections already point towards his practical strengths. During military service he continued composing, including the Mouvements perpétuels for piano. In 1919 he set six poems from Apollinaire’s Le Bestiaire. Their compact images required musical decisions on the scale of a few phrases: a bass pattern, a characteristic interval, a closing chord that arrives before the words can become a sentimental narrative.

Les SixA public label, a private apprenticeship

In 1920 the critic Henri Collet grouped Poulenc with Georges Auric, Louis Durey, Arthur Honegger, Darius Milhaud and Germaine Tailleferre as Les Six. Cocteau supplied publicity and an argument for directness against inherited Wagnerian rhetoric. Their harmonic techniques differed, and Poulenc wanted further training. From 1921 he studied with Charles Koechlin, concentrating on counterpoint and the construction of larger forms. Private study underpinned his apparently instinctive facility.

Les Biches, first performed by the Ballets Russes in Monte Carlo in 1924, brought together Poulenc’s score, Bronislava Nijinska’s choreography and Marie Laurencin’s designs. Changing combinations of dancers and the ambiguous behaviour of a fashionable gathering provide the action. The orchestra’s marches, dances and changes of colour are sharply cut. An offstage chorus belongs to the complete ballet; the familiar orchestral suite omits its numbers. Rhythmic accuracy allows the apparently casual social scene to keep its edge.

He bought Le Grand Coteau at Noizay in Touraine in 1927, establishing a working base outside Paris. Patrons also made large projects possible. Wanda Landowska commissioned the Concert champêtre, whose harpsichord writing addresses her powerful modern instrument. The Princesse de Polignac commissioned the Concerto for Two Pianos and the Organ Concerto. In the two-piano work, Mozart-like melodic periods coexist with percussive attacks and passages influenced by the gamelan Poulenc had heard at the 1931 Paris Colonial Exhibition. The orchestral textures keep these elements distinct.

The voiceBernac, poems and religious practice

The recital partnershipPoulenc and Pierre Bernac began their regular collaboration in 1935 and continued until Bernac’s retirement in 1959. The composer’s role at the piano was central to the interpretation of the songs.

Poulenc’s partnership with the baritone Pierre Bernac made text-setting a continuous practical discipline. Bernac could test a consonant, a breath or a register against the composer’s accompaniment. Apollinaire’s conversational turns and Éluard’s compressed associations demanded different kinds of phrase. In Tel jour telle nuit, the piano connects songs through pacing and harmonic memory; an apparently incidental accompaniment can determine the cycle’s ending. The placing of a syllable within a bar can alter its meaning.

In 1936 the violent death of the composer Pierre-Octave Ferroud prompted Poulenc to visit the shrine at Rocamadour. The visit renewed his Catholic practice and led to the Litanies à la Vierge noire for women’s voices and organ. The Mass in G followed in 1937. His relationships with men, including Lucien Roubert, continued alongside his religious life. The sacred works share the techniques of the songs and ballets: clear triads, unexpected harmonic turns, short repeated figures and strongly differentiated blocks.

During the German occupation he composed Figure humaine, an unaccompanied double-choir cantata on Éluard’s poems, in 1943. Its final movement sets Liberté. The singers carry every harmonic foundation and rhythmic connection; the climactic high soprano writing arrives after an extended accumulation. The work was first broadcast in London in 1945 in English.

The theatreThree different kinds of speech

Denise Duval became a crucial interpreter, beginning with the heroine of Les Mamelles de Tirésias, premiered in 1947 after Apollinaire’s surrealist play. For Dialogues des Carmélites, Poulenc turned to Georges Bernanos’s text about the Carmelite nuns executed during the French Revolution. Its first performance was in Italian at La Scala in January 1957; Duval sang Blanche in the French premiere later that year. In the final scene the repeated guillotine strokes remove voices from the singing community one by one. The scoring makes a historical action audible as the progressive reduction of a texture.

Speech and silenceLa Voix humaine contains only one onstage singer. The audience reconstructs the unseen lover’s replies through pauses, interruptions and the woman’s answers.

La Voix humaine, premiered by Duval in 1959, sets Cocteau’s telephone monologue. Its broken phrases need a different vocal timing from the public exchanges of Dialogues. The late Gloria, first performed in Boston in 1961, again places buoyant choral articulation beside sustained prayer. His final wind sonatas give the flute, clarinet and oboe sharply individual registers and kinds of melancholy. Poulenc died of a heart attack in January 1963; the Clarinet and Oboe Sonatas received their premieres after his death.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞Melody & speech

A memorable tune may begin with repeated notes or an ordinary scale fragment. French speech determines which syllable can take weight, and the accompaniment can contradict an apparently uncomplicated line. Bernac’s practical advice and Poulenc’s own work at the piano grounded the technique in performance.

♮Tonal cuts

Poulenc often puts clearly recognisable triads beside one another without the expected preparation. The surprise lies in the root movement or altered inner note. A phrase can sound harmonically familiar at each instant while taking an unexpected route between those instants.

𝅘𝅥Rhythm & genre

March, waltz, music-hall song and older dance types retain their characteristic accents. Sudden changes of pace or articulation interrupt them. Les Biches depends on these sharply profiled exchanges, and the concertos preserve similar contrasts without a stage.

𝄢Choral writing

Block chords alternate with independent lines; clear verbal delivery can become a demanding contrapuntal problem when two choirs interact. Figure humaine asks the voices to sustain a large harmonic argument without instruments. The Mass and Gloria distinguish prayer from acclamation through register, spacing and rhythmic density.

𝄡Winds & keyboard

Wind instruments are treated as identifiable speakers. The late sonatas exploit changes between registers and between quick articulation and a sustained line. Piano parts switch from percussion-like chord attacks to a legato harmonic support; even an accompaniment of repeated chords requires a chosen articulation.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1919
    Le Bestiaire ou Cortège d’OrphéeFP 15Voice and piano; version with chamber ensemble
    Six settings from Apollinaire’s illustrated collection. Their brevity leaves little room for a neutral introduction or an unnecessary final cadence.
  2. 1923
    Les BichesFP 36Orchestra and offstage mixed chorus; ballet, later orchestral suite
    Premiered in 1924 with choreography by Nijinska and designs by Marie Laurencin. The suite omits the vocal numbers of the complete ballet.
  3. 1927–28
    Concert champêtreFP 49Harpsichord and orchestra
    Written for Wanda Landowska and premiered in 1929. Baroque references and modern harmonic cuts share the same three-movement concerto.
  4. 1932
    Concerto for Two Pianos in D minorFP 61Two pianos and orchestra
    Commissioned by the Princesse de Polignac. Percussive keyboard exchanges, Mozartian phrase-making and gamelan-related passages remain deliberately distinguishable.
  5. 1934–38
    Organ Concerto in G minorFP 93Organ, strings and timpani
    A continuous sequence of contrasting sections. The reduced orchestra makes organ registration, string attack and timpani articulation central to the changes of character.
  6. 1943
    Figure humaineFP 120Double mixed choir, unaccompanied; poems by Paul Éluard
    Eight movements ending with Liberté. Written during the occupation and first broadcast in London in 1945; its harmonic and registral demands require exceptional choral control.
  7. 1953–56
    Dialogues des CarmélitesFP 159Opera in three acts; text after Georges Bernanos
    Premiered at La Scala in 1957. Short conversational units and recurring musical ideas lead to the final scene’s progressively diminishing chorus.
  8. 1959
    GloriaFP 177Soprano, mixed chorus and orchestra
    Six movements, first performed in Boston in 1961. Acclamatory rhythms and the soprano’s extended lines belong to different kinds of liturgical utterance within one score.
  9. 1962
    Clarinet SonataFP 184Clarinet and piano
    Dedicated to Arthur Honegger’s memory. Three movements distinguish an unsettled opening, a sustained Romanza and a rapid finale; premiered by Benny Goodman and Leonard Bernstein in 1963.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Blanche’s portrait of the circle includes the pianist Marcelle Meyer and Jean Cocteau as well as composers. It records a network of performers, writers and friends. Poulenc’s study with Koechlin supplied private technical discipline behind that sociable public image.

Apollinaire’s poems were published with Dufy’s woodcuts before Poulenc selected six of them in 1919. The book joins animal image and compressed verbal form. Compare the print’s bounded black-and-white shapes with the song’s short phrases: both work at a scale that makes every addition conspicuous.

Guillaume Apollinaire

Le Dromadaire

1911; set by Poulenc in 1919
FrenchAvec ses quatre dromadaires Don Pedro d’Alfaroubeira Courut le monde et l’admira. Il fit ce que je voudrais faire Si j’avais quatre dromadaires.
Our translationWith his four dromedaries Don Pedro d’Alfaroubeira Travelled the world and admired it. He did what I would like to do If I had four dromedaries.

The first of Poulenc’s six Bestiaire songs retains the poem’s disproportionately grand traveller and its modest conditional ending within the compass of Apollinaire’s five lines. He died in 1918, and the 1911 original is public domain; the English translation here is new.

Poulenc’s debt to Chabrier concerns harmonic audacity as much as humour; his study with Koechlin supplied disciplined contrapuntal work beneath the public image of Les Six. Stravinsky provided models for instrumental attack and juxtaposition. These relationships run through both sacred pieces and ballets.
