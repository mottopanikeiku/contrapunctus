ILife

Schumann grew up in Zwickau, where his father August was a bookseller and publisher. Reading and music developed together: Jean Paul’s novels, with their interruptions, doubles and changes of narrative voice, mattered alongside piano lessons. Sent to Leipzig to study law in 1828, he spent more time on music and literature; a period in Heidelberg did not reconcile him to a legal career. He returned to Leipzig to study piano seriously with Friedrich Wieck. Wieck’s daughter Clara, already performing publicly as a child, gave him an immediate example of the professional discipline he hoped to acquire.

A persistent problem with his right hand ended the prospect of a virtuoso career. The familiar account of a finger-strengthening device is not sufficient to establish the injury’s medical cause. Composition and criticism became his principal occupations instead. The early published piano works already had distinct literary and formal premises: the Abegg Variations derive a theme from letters, while Papillons relates its dances to the masked-ball ending of Jean Paul’s Flegeljahre. The individual miniatures do not explain a novel scene by scene. Their sudden juxtapositions recreate the experience of a narrative changing speakers and levels.

1834A journal with invented contributors

He helped establish the Neue Zeitschrift für Musik in 1834 and edited it for a decade. Its imaginary Davidsbund, or League of David, fought the musical ‘Philistines’ through reviews, dialogues and portraits. Florestan and Eusebius supplied contrasting critical voices: one impulsive, the other reflective. They were deliberate literary personae, not straightforward clinical records. Schumann recognised Chopin early, supported Berlioz, and later introduced Brahms to readers. His criticism could analyse form, characterise a performance and stage a dispute inside the same article.

Names in notesGerman notation makes H = B♮ and Es = E♭; As means A♭. Carnaval uses A–Es–C–H and As–C–H to encode Asch, the home town of his then fiancée Ernestine von Fricken. Those letters also occur in Schumann’s surname.

Carnaval, composed in 1834–35, turns these few pitches into a succession of masks, portraits and dances. Chopin and Paganini appear beside Pierrot, Harlequin, Florestan and Eusebius; ‘Chiarina’ points to Clara. The cipher does not make all the pieces sound alike, because rhythm, register and harmonisation continually change its identity. The concluding march of the Davidsbündler is in triple metre, and incorporates a tune Schumann associated with the old-fashioned opposition. A supposedly public musical battle is constructed from a private network of names, quotations and associations.

His relationship with Clara deepened after the end of his engagement to Ernestine. Friedrich Wieck opposed their marriage, questioning Robert’s finances and reliability and attempting to control contact between them. Letters and musical references carried messages across the separation. The C major Fantasie, Op. 17, began partly as a contribution to the projected Beethoven monument in Bonn, but its first movement also quotes An die ferne Geliebte, Beethoven’s song cycle to a distant beloved. Public homage and private address occupy the same score without requiring every theme to have a single biographical meaning.

1840–42Song, symphony, chamber music

After a legal struggle, Robert and Clara married on 12 September 1840, the day before her twenty-first birthday. That year he composed more than a hundred songs. Heine, Eichendorff, Chamisso and Rückert offered different relations between statement and implication. In Dichterliebe, a singer can insist ‘I bear no grudge’ while harmony and rising intensity complicate the claim. Piano introductions and postludes tell parts of the story that the voice leaves unstated. The cycle’s final postlude recalls earlier music after the poet has declared his songs and grief buried.

The piano after the voiceA song’s last sung word need not be its conclusion. Schumann often gives the pianist the harmonic resolution, remembered motif or contradiction withheld from the singer; stopping at the text’s last line loses part of the composition.

In 1841 he turned to orchestral music: the ‘Spring’ Symphony and the first version of the D minor Symphony later revised as No. 4. In 1842 he concentrated on chamber music, writing three string quartets, the E♭ major Piano Quintet and the Piano Quartet in the same key. The Quintet’s final fugue combines the finale’s material with the first movement’s opening theme. Such returns provide a large-scale counterpart to the recollections in his piano cycles. A work can change genre and instrumentation without abandoning the principle that earlier music acquires new meaning when heard again.

Clara’s performing career supported the household and brought Robert’s works before audiences, but their practical needs could conflict: practice, composition, teaching, touring and childcare required time and space. They shared musical study, including Bach, and wrote songs for a joint Rückert collection. Robert’s A minor Piano Concerto grew from a one-movement Fantasy of 1841; he added the Intermezzo and finale in 1845. Its opening exchanges the principal phrase between piano and oboe, establishing collaboration rather than a soloist’s prolonged contest with an anonymous orchestra.

DresdenNew forms of continuity

After travelling with Clara to Russia in 1844, he experienced a serious period of ill health. The family moved from Leipzig to Dresden later that year. His compositional interests did not simply contract: contrapuntal study produced the B–A–C–H organ fugues, while the C major Second Symphony and the opera Genoveva addressed large structures. The slow movement of the Second makes a sustained melodic line coexist with imitative inner parts. In the late 1840s, collections for young or domestic performers appeared alongside demanding chamber and dramatic works. Intended audience, technical difficulty and artistic ambition were separate decisions.

In 1850 he became municipal music director in Düsseldorf. The position initially produced enthusiasm and new music, including the Cello Concerto and the ‘Rhenish’ Symphony. The latter has five movements; its fourth adopts a solemn, contrapuntal manner before the finale restores a more mobile public character. His rehearsal technique and relations with the orchestra deteriorated, however, and by late 1853 he was effectively displaced from regular conducting. Accounts of these conflicts must distinguish observed failures of communication from retrospective explanations based solely on his subsequent illness.

1853–56The late scores and Endenich

The violinist Joseph Joachim and the young Brahms entered the Schumanns’ circle in 1853. Robert’s article ‘Neue Bahnen’ announced Brahms with expectations that the younger composer found burdensome. For Joachim he wrote a Violin Concerto; for other practical combinations he continued to compose songs, chamber pieces and concertante works. Some late music tightens its repetitions and exposes its texture rather than reproducing the restless contrasts of Carnaval. The resulting differences are compositional evidence. They do not by themselves establish a diagnosis or a timetable of mental decline.

In February 1854 Schumann suffered a severe crisis with auditory disturbances and attempted suicide by entering the Rhine. He was rescued and admitted, at his own request, to the private institution at Endenich. Clara was kept from visiting for most of his confinement under the doctors’ regime; Brahms and Joachim were among those who saw him. She reached him shortly before his death on 29 July 1856. Clara’s later performances and editions preserved much of his repertory, while the Violin Concerto remained unperformed publicly until 1937. The history of what was withheld is as necessary as the history of what she kept before listeners.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄾Displaced metre

Tied notes, offbeat accents and phrases beginning before the expected downbeat can make a stable metre difficult to hear. The pianist must know where the beat is to communicate its displacement. Regularising every accent removes the tension rather than clarifying it.

𝄡Inner voices

The principal line is not always the uppermost note. In the Humoreske, Schumann even notates an ‘inner voice’ on a separate staff that is not to be played as an additional part. The physical texture and the melody the listener is asked to infer are different things.

𝄋Fragment & cycle

Short dances and character pieces acquire meaning through order, return and interruption. Davidsbündlertänze recalls earlier music near its close; Carnaval binds sharply different personalities through tiny pitch cells. A fragment can be complete as a gesture while remaining incomplete as a story.

𝅘𝅥Song partnership

The piano establishes a situation before the singer enters and can qualify it after the singer stops. Dichterliebe begins without a secure tonic conclusion; its final postlude is a separate act of remembrance, not an accompaniment waiting for a missing stanza.

𝄢Contrapuntal memory

The Piano Quintet’s finale combines its own material with the opening movement’s theme. The Second Symphony similarly makes counterpoint a way of testing remembered ideas. Fugue is a dramatic procedure within a larger argument, not an isolated demonstration of school technique.

𝄞Orchestral texture

Doublings and active middle parts give the symphonies a particular density. Balance depends on articulation, tempo and the size of the string body; treating every inner part as an indistinct accompaniment can create the heaviness blamed on the notation. The 1841 and 1851 versions of the D minor Symphony make different decisions about this texture.

IIIWorks

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1829–31
    PapillonsOp. 2Piano
    A sequence of dances associated with Jean Paul’s Flegeljahre; the close recalls the opening dance as the ball disperses.
  2. 1834–35
    CarnavalOp. 9Piano
    Masks and portraits generated from the ASCH pitch ciphers; the final march remains in triple metre.
  3. 1834–35; revised later
    Symphonic ÉtudesOp. 13Piano
    A variation design that changes register, density and articulation as if redistributing a theme among orchestral groups.
  4. 1836–38
    Fantasie in C majorOp. 17Piano
    Three movements; the first’s Beethoven quotation and the last’s extended slow span complicate a simple virtuoso-sonata model.
  5. 1837
    DavidsbündlertänzeOp. 6Piano
    Eighteen pieces associated with Florestan and Eusebius; a motive from Clara’s music opens the cycle.
  6. 1838
    KreislerianaOp. 16Piano
    Eight movements named for Hoffmann’s fictional musician, with abrupt changes of speed, register and harmonic stability.
  7. 1840
    LiederkreisOp. 39Voice and piano; poems by Eichendorff
    Twelve songs including Mondnacht, Waldesgespräch and Zwielicht; landscape repeatedly changes into an uncertain speaking presence.
  8. 1840
    DichterliebeOp. 48Voice and piano; poems by Heine
    Sixteen songs in the published cycle; the piano’s final recollection outlasts the singer’s proposed burial of love and song.
  9. 1841
    Symphony No. 1 in B♭ major “Spring”Op. 38Orchestra
    An opening brass call supplies material for a four-movement symphonic design rather than a sequence of seasonal pictures.
  10. 1841; revised 1851
    Symphony No. 4 in D minorOp. 120Orchestra
    Connected movements and recurring material; the two versions differ in orchestration and details of formal continuity.
  11. 1842
    Piano Quintet in E♭ majorOp. 44Piano and string quartet
    A march-like slow movement and a finale that combines themes from different movements in counterpoint.
  12. 1845; from an 1841 Fantasy
    Piano Concerto in A minorOp. 54Piano and orchestra
    Expanded from an earlier Fantasy; the opening theme moves between piano and woodwind rather than belonging exclusively to the soloist.
  13. 1845–46
    Symphony No. 2 in C majorOp. 61Orchestra
    A brass motto frames an argument in which the Adagio’s singing line and imitative writing are central.
  14. 1850
    Cello Concerto in A minorOp. 129Cello and orchestra
    Three connected movements; the soloist’s continuity matters more than a conventional succession of orchestral contests.
  15. 1850
    Symphony No. 3 in E♭ major “Rhenish”Op. 97Orchestra
    Five movements, including a solemn fourth whose compact contrapuntal writing interrupts the surrounding public energy.
  16. 1853
    Violin Concerto in D minorViolin and orchestra
    Written for Joachim and withheld after Schumann’s death; the finale’s polonaise-like tread differs from a conventional fleet concerto conclusion.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

The two figures share a landscape without turning it into a public spectacle. This is a comparison with the nocturnal world of Eichendorff’s poems, especially Mondnacht, which Schumann set in Op. 39, not a claim that this painting inspired the song. The exposed tree roots and interrupted path give the view a physical resistance; in the song, the piano’s continuous movement and the voice’s slower phrases similarly occupy different measures of time.

Heinrich Heine

Im wunderschönen Monat Mai

1822–23; collected in Buch der Lieder, 1827
GermanIm wunderschönen Monat Mai, Als alle Knospen sprangen, Da ist in meinem Herzen Die Liebe aufgegangen. Im wunderschönen Monat Mai, Als alle Vögel sangen, Da hab ich ihr gestanden Mein Sehnen und Verlangen.
Our translationIn the wonderfully lovely month of May, when all the buds burst open, then in my heart love opened too. In the wonderfully lovely month of May, when all the birds were singing, I confessed to her my longing and desire.

Schumann opens Dichterliebe with this poem in 1840. Heine reports the confession but gives no answer from the beloved; Schumann makes the absence audible by withholding a final tonic resolution. The simple equation of spring and love is therefore unstable before the cycle has reached its explicit disappointments. 1827 text and scan.

E. T. A. Hoffmann

Beethovens Instrumentalmusik · from Kreisleriana

Collected in 1814
German proseDie Musik schließt dem Menschen ein unbekanntes Reich auf, eine Welt, die nichts gemein hat mit der äußern Sinnenwelt, die ihn umgibt und in der er alle bestimmten Gefühle zurückläßt, um sich einer unaussprechlichen Sehnsucht hinzugeben.
Our translationMusic opens to a person an unknown domain, a world with nothing in common with the outward world of the senses surrounding them, in which they leave behind all definite feelings to surrender to an inexpressible longing.

Hoffmann’s essays and fictions give the musician Johannes Kreisler a voice at once analytical and unstable. Schumann borrowed the name for Op. 16, not a plot to be illustrated. The rapid alternation of agitation and inward reflection has a literary counterpart in Hoffmann’s changes of speaker and frame. This passage is quoted from the Beethoven essay, not attributed to the fictional Kreisler as a historical person.

VIILineage

Schumann
Passed toJohannes BrahmsPyotr Ilyich TchaikovskyEdward ElgarGustav Mahler

Brahms inherited Schumann’s interest in variation, counterpoint and concealed connections, but also the practical support of Robert and Clara. Tchaikovsky valued Schumann’s character pieces and orchestral writing. The miniature cycle and the symphony are connected branches of this influence, not separate early and mature reputations.
