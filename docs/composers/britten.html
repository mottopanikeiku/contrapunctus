59.1Life

Benjamin Britten was born in Lowestoft on St Cecilia’s Day, 22 November 1913, in a house facing the North Sea. His father was a dentist; his mother, an amateur singer, organised music at home and encouraged his composing. He learnt piano and viola, filled exercise books with music, and heard Frank Bridge conduct The Sea at Norwich in 1924. Three years later he began lessons with Bridge. The older composer required every note to justify its place, and introduced a provincial schoolboy to music beyond the English choral tradition. The Variations on a Theme of Frank Bridge, written for strings in 1937, made that apprenticeship audible in ten sharply differentiated transformations.

At the Royal College of Music, where he studied from 1930 to 1933, Britten’s composition teacher was John Ireland. Concerts supplied another education: Mahler’s orchestration, Berg’s combination of formal calculation and theatre, and Stravinsky’s handling of rhythm. Britten drew selectively on these modernist languages. He learnt to set a tonal centre against notes which refuse to belong to it, and to make a familiar tune sound suspect through its accompaniment. His early Sinfonietta and choral variations A Boy Was Born already treat small motives as material for an entire structure.

Work and exileFilm, Auden and America

In 1935 he began composing for the GPO Film Unit. A documentary might need music for just a handful of players, fitted to a measured length of film. Night Mail brought his music together with W. H. Auden’s verse in 1936. Radio plays, theatre and films taught him how to distinguish characters with one instrument and how to make speech carry through an ensemble. He also met the tenor Peter Pears, who became his partner and the principal interpreter of his vocal music.

Britten and Pears travelled to North America in 1939. There Britten completed the Violin Concerto, wrote the Sinfonia da Requiem and collaborated with Auden on Paul Bunyan. Reading E. M. Forster’s essay on the Suffolk poet George Crabbe prompted him to look again at the country he had left. Crabbe’s The Borough supplied Peter Grimes, a fisherman whose apprentices die in his care. Serge Koussevitzky’s commission made an opera possible. Britten and Pears returned to Britain in 1942; as pacifists they applied for recognition as conscientious objectors, and gave recitals in place of military service.

A tenor’s instrumentBritten wrote for Pears’s clear consonants, flexible declamation and distinctive upper register: Grimes, Captain Vere, Peter Quint and Aschenbach are different uses of the same singer.

1945The Borough on stage

Peter Grimes opened at Sadler’s Wells on 7 June 1945, with Pears in the title role and Reginald Goodall conducting. Montagu Slater’s libretto altered Crabbe’s unequivocally brutal fisherman into a character capable of tenderness, violence and self-delusion. The chorus acts as a community: gossip hardens into a manhunt. Between scenes the orchestra supplies another perspective. In ‘Dawn’, high violin and flute lines, harp and clarinet figures, and low brass chords occupy separate registers; their refusal to blend matters as much as any representation of waves.

Britten also developed a portable chamber opera in The Rape of Lucretia and Albert Herring. In 1947 he helped found the English Opera Group; in 1948 Britten, Pears and the director Eric Crozier launched the Aldeburgh Festival. Performances in churches and halls placed new music beside older repertory and local music-making. Noye’s Fludde, first performed in Orford Church in 1958, gives children, amateur instrumentalists and the congregation parts alongside professionals. Its hymn singing helps drive the drama.

The ground bassA passacaglia repeats a bass pattern while the music above changes. Britten uses the inherited form as a constraint: a character may alter the words or orchestration without escaping the underlying sequence.

Billy Budd, first performed in 1951, confines an all-male cast aboard a warship; its long orchestral sequence of chords makes the unseen interview between Vere and Billy a central event. The Turn of the Screw followed in 1954, with thirteen instrumentalists and six singers. Its prologue leads to a twelve-note theme and fifteen variations separating sixteen scenes. The theme generates tonal and motivic relationships as well as all twelve pitch classes. The variations tighten the structure while the governess’s reliability remains in doubt.

Public ritualCoventry and the church parables

The War Requiem was commissioned for the consecration of the new Coventry Cathedral and first performed there on 30 May 1962. Britten separates three performing groups: soprano, chorus and large orchestra sing the Latin Mass; tenor, baritone and chamber orchestra give Wilfred Owen’s poems the intimacy of two soldiers speaking; boys’ voices and organ sound at a distance. The bell interval C–F♯ unsettles the opening prayer. In the Offertorium, Owen’s Abraham kills his son rather than accepting the angel’s reprieve, contradicting the liturgy’s promise to Abraham and his descendants.

A visit to Japan in 1956 had introduced Britten to Noh theatre. Curlew River, first performed at Orford in 1964, relocates the Noh play Sumidagawa to an English religious community. A procession of monks becomes a cast of characters, then returns to the framing hymn. Flute, horn, viola, double bass, harp, percussion and chamber organ replace the opera orchestra. Instrumental lines often take their timing from the singer. Two further church parables extended this experiment in ritual, restricted resources and musical time.

Writing for particular handsFriendship with Mstislav Rostropovich produced the Cello Sonata, Cello Symphony and three unaccompanied suites. The suites turn multiple stopping, pizzicato and register changes into the suggestion of several voices on one instrument.

The last theatreVenice and Aldeburgh

Snape Maltings opened as the festival’s concert hall in 1967. Britten’s final opera, Death in Venice, was completed in 1973: Pears’s Aschenbach speaks through accompanied recitative, while Tadzio’s world is assigned to dancers and tuned percussion. Heart surgery that year left Britten unable to resume his former performing life. He nevertheless wrote the Third String Quartet, whose final movement returns to the opera’s material, and the cantata Phaedra for Janet Baker. Made a life peer in June 1976, he died at the Red House that December. Pears remained there, preserving the working library and manuscripts around which the present archive was built.

59.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝅘𝅥The speaking voice

English stress determines rhythm: an unstressed syllable can hurry towards the important word. In Peter Grimes, public exchanges are sharply syllabic; Grimes’s ‘Now the Great Bear and Pleiades’ suspends ordinary conversation in a sustained line. That change of pace gives the voice its character.

♯Tonal friction

Triads and keys remain recognisable, but a foreign bass or recurring interval prevents them from becoming comfortable. The C–F♯ bells of the War Requiem are a tritone, dividing the octave equally and refusing to identify a tonic. The memory of that interval persists into the final F major.

𝄋Variation and constraint

The passacaglias in Peter Grimes and the Violin Concerto make repetition carry an argument. The Turn of the Screw applies variation to a twelve-note theme across an entire opera. What changes — register, tempo, instrumentation, harmonic emphasis — becomes audible because an underlying shape persists.

𝄞Orchestral separation

Britten often lets instruments retain their edges. The Serenade places tenor, horn and strings in different acoustic roles; its unaccompanied horn Prologue and Epilogue use natural harmonics, including pitches that diverge from equal temperament. In the War Requiem, physical separation distinguishes public ceremony from private testimony.

𝄢Borrowed forms

Purcell’s ground bass, the Baroque suite and the fugue are working tools. The Young Person’s Guide to the Orchestra first disassembles a Purcell theme into instrumental families, then lets a fugue accumulate those instruments before the original theme returns. Recognition is engineered through texture and register.

𝄐Ritual and heterophony

In the church parables, singers and instruments can give slightly different versions of a line at once — heterophony. Processional chant frames the action. The restricted ensembles and the absence of a conventional conductor require performers to listen closely to one another.

59.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1937
    Variations on a Theme of Frank BridgeOp. 10String orchestra
    Ten variations on a theme from Bridge’s Second Idyll. A march, waltz, funeral march and fugue turn a teacher’s material into a study of string techniques and musical character.
  2. 1939
    Les IlluminationsOp. 18High voice and string orchestra; Arthur Rimbaud
    French prose poems set in compact, contrasting movements. Written for the soprano Sophie Wyss and subsequently taken up by tenors including Pears.
  3. 1943
    Serenade for Tenor, Horn and StringsOp. 31Tenor, horn and strings
    Six English poems framed by a solo horn Prologue and its offstage return. Natural harmonics give the frame its deliberately unequal intonation.
  4. 1944–45
    Peter GrimesOp. 33Opera in a prologue and three acts; libretto by Montagu Slater
    A fisherman, his dead apprentices and a community that turns into a pursuing crowd. The Four Sea Interludes, Op. 33a, are a concert sequence extracted and reordered from the opera.
  5. 1945
    The Young Person’s Guide to the OrchestraOp. 34Orchestra, with optional narration
    Variations and Fugue on a Theme of Purcell, from his incidental music to Abdelazer. Made for the educational film Instruments of the Orchestra, released in 1946.
  6. 1950–51; revised 1960
    Billy BuddOp. 50Opera; all-male cast, chorus and orchestra
    E. M. Forster and Eric Crozier adapted Melville’s novella. The original four acts became two in the revision; Captain Vere’s retrospective frame remains.
  7. 1954
    The Turn of the ScrewOp. 54Six singers and thirteen instrumentalists; libretto by Myfanwy Piper
    Henry James’s ghost story organised as a theme and fifteen variations. The celesta is associated with Peter Quint; doubling makes thirteen players supply a much larger range of colours.
  8. 1961–62
    War RequiemOp. 66Three soloists, mixed chorus, boys’ choir, large orchestra, chamber orchestra and organ
    The Latin Requiem is interrupted by nine Owen poems. In the closing movement two dead soldiers recognise one another before the musical groups are brought together.
  9. 1964
    Curlew RiverOp. 71Church parable for male singers and seven instrumentalists
    William Plomer’s text adapts the Noh play Sumidagawa. A mother looking for her son crosses a river and learns that he has died there; chant opens and closes the performance.
  10. 1971–73
    Death in VeniceOp. 88Opera; libretto by Myfanwy Piper after Thomas Mann
    Aschenbach is a tenor; the boys are dancers. Piano-accompanied recitative, a baritone’s multiple roles and percussion associated with Tadzio divide the dramatic world.

59.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

George Crabbe

The Borough, Letter XXII — Peter Grimes

1810
EnglishHe nursed the feelings these dull scenes produce, And loved to stop beside the opening sluice; Where the small stream, confined in narrow bound, Ran with a dull, unvaried, saddening sound; Where all, presented to the eye or ear, Oppressed the soul with misery, grief, and fear.

Crabbe was born in Aldeburgh in 1754. These six lines describe Grimes seeking out the estuary after the deaths of his apprentices; spelling is modernised. The poem supplies the opera’s subject. Slater and Britten shift its moral balance, giving Grimes a capacity for imagination that Crabbe’s sustained account of abuse scarcely permits. The water is already described through sound — a restricted, repetitive stream.

Wilfred Owen

Anthem for Doomed Youth

1917; published 1920
EnglishWhat passing-bells for these who die as cattle? Only the monstrous anger of the guns. Only the stuttering rifles’ rapid rattle Can patter out their hasty orisons. No mockeries for them; no prayers nor bells, Nor any voice of mourning save the choirs,— The shrill, demented choirs of wailing shells; And bugles calling for them from sad shires. What candles may be held to speed them all? Not in the hands of boys, but in their eyes Shall shine the holy glimmers of goodbyes. The pallor of girls’ brows shall be their pall; Their flowers the tenderness of patient minds, And each slow dusk a drawing-down of blinds.

Britten places this sonnet in the opening movement of the War Requiem, giving it to the tenor and chamber orchestra. Owen’s substitution of guns for bells and shells for a choir confronts the actual choir singing the Requiem around him. The poem’s refusal of adequate funeral ritual interrupts the ritual being performed.

Constable paints the cultivated land behind his father’s house at East Bergholt in Suffolk. The connection is regional; there is no documented influence. Britten’s Aldeburgh music likewise belongs to a working county of gardens, churches, farms and fishing boats. Read the ordered plots against Crabbe’s estuary: both show a countryside shaped by human labour.

59.8Lineage

Drew fromHenry PurcellFrank BridgeGustav MahlerAlban BergIgor Stravinsky
Britten
Passed toHarrison BirtwistleOliver KnussenThomas Adès

Britten took Purcell’s grounds and English declamation into a language sharpened by Berg’s theatrical forms and Mahler’s orchestral separation. His institutional legacy matters alongside his scores: the English Opera Group and Aldeburgh gave later British composers singers, players and places in which new work could be tried. Thomas Adès has returned to the problem of opera as a conflict between the individual voice and the surrounding society.
