Life

Pierre Boulez arrived in Paris in 1943 after studying advanced mathematics in Lyon and rejecting the engineering career his father wanted for him. He was eighteen, from Montbrison in central France. Within two years he had won a first prize in harmony at the Conservatoire. His teacher, Olivier Messiaen, also held private classes where students took apart Debussy’s Jeux and Stravinsky’s The Rite of Spring.

Messiaen’s classroom demanded concentration. Horn and piano classes sounded nearby while the students took musical dictation. ‘You learned to listen without sound’, Boulez recalled to Jed Distler in a London interview in August 2000, explaining why he never composed at the piano. He took counterpoint lessons with Andrée Vaurabourg and studied twelve-note music with René Leibowitz. The latter introduced him to Schoenberg and Webern; by 1946 Boulez had broken with him, impatient with his reverence for Schoenberg’s methods.

A working musicianThe theatre and the piano

In 1946 he played the ondes Martenot at the Folies Bergère and on radio, having learnt the electronic instrument from its inventor. That October, Arthur Honegger recommended him to Jean-Louis Barrault for a production of Hamlet. By the year’s end Boulez was directing the company’s stage music, arranging pieces for its small band of players.

Each of the twelve piano Notations of 1945 occupies twelve bars; a rapid figure, a held chord or a repeated note can fill the little frame. The Second Piano Sonata, begun in 1947, spreads its jagged phrases across four movements. Its attacks leap between registers, several lines struggle for space, and the finale drives through fugal writing towards a quiet, dispersed ending.

What gets ordered?A twelve-note row orders pitches. In Structures Ia, Boulez also gives durations, loudnesses and kinds of attack their own ordered schemes. The pianist has to realise these distinctions note by note, even when the two instruments are moving at different speeds.

For that two-piano piece, begun in 1951, he borrowed a sequence from Messiaen’s Mode de valeurs et d’intensités. Teacher and pupil gave the premiere together in Paris in May 1952. The same month saw the publication of his essay ‘Schoenberg Is Dead’, attacking Schoenberg’s use of inherited forms with a new pitch language. Onstage, the twenty-seven-year-old was sitting beside the teacher whose music had supplied his starting material.

Paris, 1954A room for new music

The concerts that became the Domaine musical began at the Petit-Marigny in January 1954. The first programme put Bach beside Webern, Stravinsky, Nono and Stockhausen. With the patron Suzanne Tézenas as the association’s president and Boulez as its treasurer, the series gave this repertory a regular Paris address.

Records at the museumDuring the winter of 1945–46, Mady Sauvageot, curator of the Musée Guimet’s music collections, introduced Boulez to recordings from Africa and Asia. Decades later he recalled Central African wooden percussion when discussing the opening of the second movement of Le Marteau sans maître.

Le Marteau, first performed under Hans Rosbaud in Baden-Baden in June 1955, puts three short poems by René Char among nine interleaved movements. Its six instrumentalists play alto flute, viola, guitar, vibraphone, xylorimba and percussion. They join and separate in combinations small enough for each touch to register. In the third movement, the mezzo-soprano has only the alto flute beside her. By the last, her words have given way to humming; flute, gong and tam-tams carry the music towards silence.

Words and pathwaysTwo kinds of freedom

Boulez had met John Cage in Paris in 1949 and presented his prepared-piano music at private concerts. His own Third Piano Sonata, begun in 1955, offers a carefully bounded freedom: the pianist chooses routes through passages whose notes are already written. Only two of its five main sections were published complete during his lifetime. In Constellation–Miroir, red and green printing distinguishes blocks of chords from scattered points of sound; arrows show the pianist where to go.

Stéphane Mallarmé’s poetry gave him another way of thinking about a work’s shape. Pli selon pli grew around three settings for soprano and instruments, eventually forming a five-movement cycle. A great orchestral chord opens Don; the same chord closes Tombeau, after the singer has reached the word ‘mort’. Between them, a syllable may spread over many notes or a line of verse pass on a single breath. The cycle reached its fully orchestral form in 1962; Boulez was still revising it in 1989.

Orchestras and a laboratoryMaking time for the difficult

In 1971 he took up the chief conductorship of the BBC Symphony Orchestra and became music director of the New York Philharmonic. These appointments ran until 1975 and 1977 respectively. He also took on Wagner at Bayreuth: his 1976 Ring with director Patrice Chéreau initially provoked a scandal and returned for five summers. Conducting the whole cycle repeatedly made him consider how Wagner had returned to ideas invented twenty years earlier and found more in them.

In Paris, President Georges Pompidou had asked him in 1970 to establish a music research institute. The Ensemble intercontemporain followed in 1976, IRCAM’s opening in 1977. Specialist players and researchers could now work together over long periods. For Répons, developed with Andrew Gerzso and first performed in 1981, the computer took the clear attacks of harp, cimbalom, keyboards and percussion and sent back transformed, delayed versions.

The players’ positionsRépons places twenty-four instrumentalists in the centre, with the audience around them. Six soloists play farther out: two pianists, harpist, cimbalom player and two tuned-percussion players. Loudspeakers add another set of positions from which their transformed sounds can answer.

The central ensemble begins alone. When the soloists enter, six broken chords open the music into a surrounding shower of echoes. A conventional recording necessarily flattens that event. ‘The recording is a little bit like having a picture of a mobile by Calder’, Boulez told Distler in their August 2000 interview. The work reached its full length in 1984; its electronics continued to change.

The old manuscriptMore inside twelve bars

Boulez’s return to the piano Notations began with a photocopy. His former fellow student Serge Nigg sent it while seeking permission for a radio performance; Boulez no longer had the manuscript. Those tiny pieces became the seeds of much larger orchestral works. In a December 2010 interview with Wolfgang Schaufler, he described how ‘the material in fact composes with you, and you compose with the material’. His account makes revision sound like a conversation with his younger handwriting.

Dérive 2 also outgrew its first occasion. Written in 1988 for Elliott Carter’s eightieth birthday, it expanded into a long work for eleven players, completed in 2006, in which interruptions gradually occupy more time than the music they interrupt. Worsening eyesight began to curtail Boulez’s conducting in 2010. He died at home in Baden-Baden on 5 January 2016, aged ninety.

The Sound

♯Two pianos, many orders

In Structures Ia, a note’s pitch, length, loudness and attack can each belong to a different ordered sequence. The result demands sudden changes of touch rather than a smooth melodic line. Across Book I, isolated events give way to denser combinations; in Ic, rapid figures keep going almost without interruption. The two identical instruments can sound surprisingly unlike one another.

♮A small, changing ensemble

Le Marteau sans maître gives the voice an unusual family of companions. Alto flute can follow its breath; viola can sustain a note or pluck it; guitar and tuned percussion let their attacks fade. The first movement begins with paired instruments, flute with vibraphone and guitar with viola, then changes the partnerships. In the third, only voice and flute remain.

𝄞An attack with a long tail

The six soloists of Répons play instruments with sharply defined attacks: pianos, harp, cimbalom and tuned percussion. Electronics catch those attacks and prolong them through delayed, transformed repetitions. At the soloists’ first entrance, broken chords generate further broken chords around the room. The central ensemble, which has been playing alone, suddenly acquires a bright, moving circumference.

𝅘𝅥Several clocks in one room

Rituel, written in memory of Bruno Maderna, divides the orchestra into eight separated groups. Percussionists keep their groups moving; the conductor cues entrances, while different rates can continue together. The opening oboe and percussion offer a spare beginning for this growing ceremony. Repeated gestures and strokes of percussion make its returns recognisable even when the groups no longer share a beat.

𝄋Routes printed in colour

The Third Piano Sonata gives choices to the pianist without asking for improvised notes. Trope offers different arrangements of four sections; Constellation–Miroir sets out red chordal blocks and green points of sound, connected by arrows. A performance selects a route through this printed territory. Even the resonance is composed: silently depressed keys can let other struck notes set their strings vibrating.

𝄐A poem stretched into singing

In the second Improvisation sur Mallarmé, the soprano stretches the first quatrain of Une dentelle s’abolit into winding phrases. The next is syllabic: a phrase must fit one breath, helping determine its pace. Nine players surround her, four of them percussionists; returning maraca strokes punctuate the setting. Fourteen lines acquire room for breaths, echoes and lingering syllables.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1945
    Douze NotationsPiano
    Twelve pieces, each twelve bars long, with abrupt changes of speed, weight and register. Decades later Boulez enlarged several into orchestral works, finding whole stretches of music inside a few original gestures.
  2. 1947–50
    Piano Sonata No. 2Piano
    Four movements demand both explosive attack and the ability to keep simultaneous lines distinct. The finale’s fugal writing eventually dissolves into a quiet, widely spaced ending.
  3. 1951–52; revised 1953
    Structures, Book ITwo pianos
    Messiaen joined Boulez for the premiere of Ia, whose pitch sequence comes from his own Mode de valeurs et d’intensités. The complete book moves from separated, precisely weighted events towards the nearly continuous rapid writing of Ic.
  4. 1952–55; revised 1957
    Le Marteau sans maîtreMezzo-soprano and six instrumentalists; poems by René Char
    Three poems generate nine interleaved movements, with the instruments constantly changing partners. The voice’s final wordless humming leaves flute and deep metallic percussion to finish the work.
  5. 1955–57; further work 1958–63; unfinished
    Piano Sonata No. 3Piano
    Boulez published Trope and Constellation–Miroir from a projected five-part sonata. Alternative routes and colour-coded passages give the pianist choices within meticulously written music.
  6. 1957–62; revised to 1989
    Pli selon pli — Portrait de MallarméSoprano and orchestra
    Three settings of Mallarmé stand between Don and Tombeau. The huge chord that opens the cycle returns at its end, enclosing music in which words range from clear declarations to extended vocal sound.
  7. 1972–75; revised 1976 and 1987
    Rituel in memoriam Bruno MadernaOrchestra divided into eight groups
    A memorial to Maderna, who died in 1973, beginning with oboe and percussion and gathering larger groups into its ceremony. Each group has percussion to sustain its pulse, allowing several speeds to coexist.
  8. 1980–84
    RéponsSix instrumental soloists, chamber ensemble and live electronics
    The audience sits between a central ensemble and six surrounding soloists. Their first entrance releases a halo of electronically transformed arpeggios; the rest of the piece develops that exchange between struck sound and travelling echo.
  9. 1988–2006
    Dérive 2Eleven instrumentalists
    A birthday tribute to Elliott Carter grew into an extended ensemble work over eighteen years. Brief interruptions become longer than the passages they interrupt, before freer writing and a long coda change the pace.
  10. 1997
    Anthèmes 2Violin and live electronics
    Long harmonics mark the boundaries between the violin’s more active passages, while electronics transpose, layer and move its sound. At the end, dry strokes of the bow’s wood on D cut off the ringing texture.

Sister Arts

Boulez first saw Klee’s work in an Avignon exhibition in 1947, when he was twenty-two; more than forty years later he published Le pays fertile: Paul Klee. Here, Klee’s coloured rectangles overlap beneath a veil of dots. Separate shapes remain legible while contributing to a larger surface, a visual neighbour for Boulez’s interweaving instrumental lines.

Stéphane Mallarmé

Une dentelle s’abolit

1887
FrenchUne dentelle s’abolit Dans le doute du Jeu suprême À n’entr’ouvrir comme un blasphème Qu’absence éternelle de lit. Cet unanime blanc conflit D’une guirlande avec la même, Enfui contre la vitre blême Flotte plus qu’il n’ensevelit. Mais, chez qui du rêve se dore Tristement dort une mandore Au creux néant musicien Telle que vers quelque fenêtre Selon nul ventre que le sien, Filial on aurait pu naître.
Our translationLacework dissolves In doubt of the supreme Game, Half-opening, like a blasphemy, Only an eternal absence of bed. This unanimous white conflict Of one garland with its double, Fled against the pale pane, Floats more than it buries. But where dream gilds itself A mandore sleeps sadly In the hollow, musical nothingness, Such that towards some window, With no womb but its own, One might have been born its child.

Mallarmé’s lace, empty bed and sleeping mandore—a small lute—supply the text for the central movement of Pli selon pli. Boulez sets the sonnet for soprano and nine instrumentalists, including four percussionists. Maraca strokes return through the delicate setting; the fourteen lines expand into phrases whose pace can depend on the singer’s breath.

Recordings to own

  • Piano Sonata No. 2Maurizio PolliniDeutsche GrammophonThe incendiary choice: Pollini gives the opening attacks their full violence without losing the simultaneous lines. His volatile changes of speed make the finale’s quiet, thinning close especially startling.
  • Le Marteau sans maîtreHilary Summers; Ensemble intercontemporain, Pierre BoulezDeutsche GrammophonThe ensemble’s delicacy is the reason to choose this account: the silences feel as carefully placed as the notes. Summers is restrained, allowing the spare voice-and-flute movement to sit naturally among the instrumental pieces.
  • Répons · Dialogue de l’ombre doubleEnsemble intercontemporain, Pierre Boulez; Alain Damiens, clarinetDeutsche GrammophonThe soloists’ first electronically expanded entrance remains a sonic shock, even though stereo cannot place the audience inside the circle. The coupling is substantial: Damiens’s live clarinet answers its recorded double in Dialogue de l’ombre double.
  • Structures, Books I and IIAloys and Alfons KontarskyWergoAn uncompromising account, with a combative edge to Book I’s exchanges. Having both books together makes the change from its sharply separated attacks to the second book’s more varied textures particularly tangible.

Lineage

Drew fromClaude DebussyIgor StravinskyArnold SchoenbergAnton WebernOlivier Messiaen
Boulez
Passed toKaija SaariahoPhilippe ManouryPhilippe Hurel

Boulez studied Debussy’s Jeux and Stravinsky’s scores with Messiaen, then learnt the twelve-note music of Schoenberg and Webern through Leibowitz. The next generation inherited different things: Philippe Manoury’s two-piano sonata shows the impact of Boulez’s Second Piano Sonata, while Saariaho and Philippe Hurel worked at the IRCAM he had founded.
