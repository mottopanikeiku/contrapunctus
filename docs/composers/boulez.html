Life

Pierre Boulez was born in Montbrison in 1925, the son of an engineer. He studied mathematics in Lyon before choosing music against his father’s wishes and moving to Paris in 1943. At the Conservatoire, Olivier Messiaen’s classes introduced him to rhythm and to detailed analysis of Debussy and Stravinsky. Private counterpoint lessons with Andrée Vaurabourg supplied another kind of discipline. René Leibowitz introduced him to Schoenberg’s twelve-note method and the music of Webern. Boulez soon rejected what he regarded as Leibowitz’s excessive respect for inherited models, without abandoning the serial procedures he had learnt.

The twelve piano Notations of 1945 compress contrasting ideas into twelve bars each. The Second Piano Sonata, completed in 1948, works at the opposite scale. Its four movements recall the outline of a nineteenth-century sonata, but its collisions of register, irregular accents and discontinuous phrases put that outline under pressure. A fugal procedure in the finale is taken apart before it can deliver a conventional culmination. The title ‘sonata’ names the object of this argument.

Apprenticeship in publicTheatre and the series

Boulez earned his living in the Renaud–Barrault theatre company, initially playing the ondes Martenot and then directing its stage music. Arranging and conducting small ensembles taught him practical balance before he held a major orchestral post. His early encounters with music from Africa and Asia, through recordings at the Musée Guimet and later travel, also enlarged his instrumental imagination. These experiences complement the mathematical reputation of his compositions: the particular attack and decay of a sound remained central even when its position had been calculated.

More than a pitch rowIn integral serialism, ordered schemes can govern durations, dynamics and kinds of attack as well as pitch. The orders can operate independently. A composition can therefore sound unpredictable even though its materials are systematically organised.

For Structures Ia, written in 1951, Boulez took a twelve-note sequence from Messiaen’s Mode de valeurs et d’intensités and extended serial organisation into other musical parameters. Messiaen and Boulez gave its premiere for two pianos in Paris in 1952. The piece tests how far composition could be rebuilt from explicit relations. In later discussion he treated it as an experiment in reducing personal choice; the two subsequent pieces of the first book already vary the relation between system and composed surface.

His polemical essay ‘Schoenberg Is Dead’, published in 1952, attacked the retention of older forms within twelve-note composition. The same period brought contact with Stockhausen, Luigi Nono and Bruno Maderna at Darmstadt. Boulez’s major breakthrough, Le Marteau sans maître, interleaves nine movements in which René Char’s short poems recur. Alto flute, viola and mezzo-soprano can sustain or inflect a line; guitar, vibraphone, xylorimba and percussion articulate its surrounding attacks. Hans Rosbaud conducted the premiere at Baden-Baden in 1955.

Poetry and open formDifferent ways through a score

The Domaine musical concerts, begun in Paris in 1954, created a place where new work could be heard beside selected earlier music. The repertory’s need for exact rehearsal drew Boulez increasingly into conducting. His friendship and correspondence with John Cage exposed a different approach to indeterminacy. Boulez accepted limited performer choice but rejected the surrender of compositional responsibility to chance operations. In the Third Piano Sonata, begun in 1955, the pianist can choose among prescribed routes; the local material remains composed in detail.

A work can have versionsBoulez repeatedly enlarged or rewrote scores. A date range may describe several performed states. The revised version can change a work’s duration and proportions substantially.

Stéphane Mallarmé offered models for both syntax and form. Pli selon pli, assembled around three ‘Improvisations sur Mallarmé’, frames its soprano with a large, often delicately divided orchestra. Boulez’s ‘Improvisation’ studies how a poem’s sounds and structure can generate musical relations. Words are sometimes clear and sometimes stretched into vocal material. The five-movement cycle took shape between 1957 and 1962, but he continued revising it until 1989. The orchestral Notations similarly grew from the short piano originals into pieces with independent dimensions.

The conductorInstitutions as instruments

In 1969 Boulez became chief conductor of the BBC Symphony Orchestra; in 1971 he also succeeded Leonard Bernstein at the New York Philharmonic. He changed programmes and concert formats as well as performances, introducing informal events and giving contemporary music an unusually prominent place. His recorded repertory included Debussy, Ravel, Stravinsky and the Second Viennese School, later extending deeply into Mahler. At Bayreuth in 1976 he conducted Patrice Chéreau’s centenary staging of Der Ring des Nibelungen, whose nineteenth-century industrial setting made the social organisation of Wagner’s drama explicit.

At President Georges Pompidou’s invitation, Boulez developed the Paris institute that became IRCAM, opened in 1977. The Ensemble intercontemporain, founded in 1976, supplied a permanent group of specialist players. The combination made sustained research into performance and electronics possible, extending work beyond a festival’s rehearsal allowance and developing electronics that could respond to the players. Boulez also held the chair of Invention, Technique and Language in Music at the Collège de France, turning questions from the composing desk into public lectures.

The answer travelsRépons places six soloists around the audience, outside a central instrumental ensemble. Their sounds are electronically transformed and projected through loudspeakers. Spatial position becomes part of the relationship between solo statement and response.

ProliferationFrom a cell to an evening

Répons, first performed in 1981 and subsequently expanded, made the institute’s live-electronic work audible in concert. Harp, cimbalom, keyboards and tuned percussion provide sharply defined attacks whose transformed continuations move through the hall. Anthèmes 2 later applied live electronics to a single violin. Other late works require no computers: the eleven instruments of Dérive 2 turn recurring harmonic material and differently paced layers into a long, continuous structure. The piece grew from a short 1988 birthday tribute to Elliott Carter into the much larger version completed in 2006.

Boulez continued conducting into his eighties, but deteriorating eyesight and illness eventually stopped his public performances. He died at his home in Baden-Baden in January 2016. His catalogue remains relatively small beside his discography, partly because new composition so often took the form of revision. Its contrasting states are audible: the isolated, precisely weighted attacks of Structures, the instrumental relays of Le Marteau, and the sustained accumulations of Dérive 2 each solve different problems.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Serial relations

A series specifies an ordering from which the musical surface can be composed. Structures Ia extends ordering to duration, intensity and attack. Later works derive families of pitch collections from serial material, allowing local lines and chords to be composed more flexibly than a succession of complete twelve-note rows would suggest.

Harmonic multiplication

In the procedures associated with Le Marteau sans maître, segments of a row can act as chords. Transposing one segment according to the intervals of another produces a larger collection. Related collections share notes without implying tonic and dominant: their common material supplies continuity.

Attack and resonance

Plucked and struck sounds are recurrent building materials: guitar and vibraphone in Le Marteau; harp, cimbalom and keyboards in Répons. A brief attack can be answered by an instrumental sustain or an electronic continuation. The ear follows transfers of colour alongside the movement of a melodic line.

Tempo and gesture

Rapid figuration is often interrupted by held notes, rests or a change of instrumental group. These interruptions make a phrase’s boundary audible even when there is no stable metre. In later ensemble pieces, simultaneous rates of activity can coexist: harmony can move slowly beneath a rapid foreground.

Controlled mobility

The Third Piano Sonata gives the player specified alternatives. Different performances can take different paths through the same composed objects. Boulez builds this variability into the plan of the score.

Text and syntax

Char’s poems supply compact images for Le Marteau; Mallarmé’s verbal organisation helps shape Pli selon pli. A syllable can become a long melisma, and an instrumental passage can continue after intelligible words have stopped. The poem is a source of proportions, phonetics and formal relationships as well as meaning.

  1. 1945
    Douze NotationsPiano
    Twelve pieces of twelve bars each. Their sharply contrasted attacks and registers later became starting points for much larger orchestral compositions.
  2. 1947–48
    Piano Sonata No. 2Piano
    Four movements put inherited sonata, scherzo and fugal procedures under pressure. Registral leaps and changing rhythmic groupings make the pianist responsible for several simultaneous strands.
  3. 1951–52; revised 1953
    Structures, Book ITwo pianos
    Ia, Ib and Ic explore different degrees of serial control. Ia borrows its pitch sequence from Messiaen; the complete book was first performed by Yvonne Loriod and Hans Alexander Kaul in 1955.
  4. 1952–55; revised 1957
    Le Marteau sans maîtreMezzo-soprano and six instrumentalists; René Char
    Nine movements interleave three poem-based cycles. Alto flute, viola, guitar, vibraphone, xylorimba and percussion create a deliberately heterogeneous ensemble.
  5. 1955–57; unfinished
    Piano Sonata No. 3Piano
    Two of the five projected formants were published: Trope and Constellation–Miroir. Prescribed alternative routes make the performer’s choice part of the form.
  6. 1957–62; revised to 1989
    Pli selon pli — Portrait de MallarméSoprano and orchestra
    ‘Don’ and ‘Tombeau’ surround three Improvisations sur Mallarmé. The cycle moves between clearly projected words and a voice absorbed into instrumental texture.
  7. 1972–75; later revisions
    Rituel in memoriam Bruno MadernaOrchestra divided into eight groups
    A memorial for the composer-conductor who died in 1973. Groups of different sizes and their percussion articulate a ceremony of repeated responses.
  8. 1980–84; later revisions
    RéponsSix instrumental soloists, chamber ensemble and live electronics
    The central ensemble is surrounded by soloists whose sounds are transformed and redistributed electronically. The distinction between an attack and its response becomes spatial as well as temporal.
  9. 1988–2006
    Dérive 2Eleven instrumentalists
    Begun for Elliott Carter’s eightieth birthday and expanded into a large continuous span. Repeated harmonic material supports instrumental layers with different rates of movement.
  10. 1997
    Anthèmes 2Violin and live electronics
    An expansion of the solo Anthèmes. Sustained harmonic tones and rapid figures are answered by electronic transformations and changes of position in the listening space.

Editorial comparison

Boulez first encountered Klee’s work in Avignon in 1947 and returned to it in Le pays fertile: Paul Klee (1989). Klee’s dots, coloured fields and overlapping divisions make several organisations visible at once. Boulez valued his demonstrations of how elementary operations can generate a complex form. The painting’s title names the musical analogy explicitly.

Documented useStéphane Mallarmé

Une dentelle s’abolit

1887
FrenchUne dentelle s’abolit Dans le doute du Jeu suprême À n’entr’ouvrir comme un blasphème Qu’absence éternelle de lit. Cet unanime blanc conflit D’une guirlande avec la même, Enfui contre la vitre blême Flotte plus qu’il n’ensevelit. Mais, chez qui du rêve se dore Tristement dort une mandore Au creux néant musicien Telle que vers quelque fenêtre Selon nul ventre que le sien, Filial on aurait pu naître.
English · Contrapunctus translationA lacework fades away In the doubt of the supreme Game, Opening halfway, like a blasphemy, Only an eternal absence of bed. This unanimous white conflict Of a garland with its likeness, Fled against the pale windowpane, Floats more than it buries. But where a dream turns golden, A mandore sadly sleeps Within the hollow musical void, Such that towards some window, With no belly but its own, One might have been born its child.

This sonnet supplies the text of ‘Improvisation II’, the central movement of Pli selon pli. The mandore is a small plucked string instrument; the poem connects its hollow body with an absent bed and a possible birth. Boulez stretches syllables and surrounds the soprano with instrumental resonance. The French follows the 1887 printed text; the translation retains its unresolved syntax.

Lineage

Study, performance and later responses are different relationships—not a single teacher–pupil chain. Read the note for the connections.

Later connectionsKaija SaariahoPhilippe ManouryPhilippe Hurel

Messiaen’s ordered durations and Webern’s serial concentration were starting points. Boulez’s attention to instrumental colour also continues Debussy. The later influence runs through institutions as much as compositional imitation: IRCAM’s research and the Ensemble intercontemporain’s performers supported Saariaho, Manoury and composers who rejected parts of Boulez’s own aesthetic.
