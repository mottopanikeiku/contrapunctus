ILife

Felix Mendelssohn was born in Hamburg in 1809 and grew up in Berlin, where his father Abraham’s banking wealth supported an unusually extensive education. His grandfather was the Jewish philosopher Moses Mendelssohn; Felix and his siblings were baptised as Christians in 1816, and the family used the additional surname Bartholdy. He studied piano with Ludwig Berger and composition with Carl Friedrich Zelter, alongside his elder sister Fanny. The family’s concerts gave them something a classroom could not: repeated opportunities to hear their own music, revise it and discuss it with experienced performers.

Zelter’s teaching joined Bach’s counterpoint to classical forms. The boy wrote string symphonies, concertos and chamber works before the pieces usually treated as his starting point. At sixteen he composed the E♭ major Octet, Op. 20, for four violins, two violas and two cellos. Its eight independent parts are not simply two quartets taking turns: the first violin’s long ascent, the other instruments’ pulsing accompaniments and the finale’s fugal entries create different densities inside one ensemble. The scherzo’s soft, rapid articulation became a recurrent technical problem in his later orchestral writing.

1826–29Shakespeare and Bach

The overture to A Midsummer Night’s Dream, written in 1826, begins with four wind chords before the strings move into a hushed E minor scurry. It makes distinct musical populations out of Shakespeare’s fairies, lovers and tradesmen while retaining the tonal argument of a concert overture. Mendelssohn did not add the incidental music, including the wedding march and nocturne, until 1842, for a Prussian court production. The opening chords therefore returned across a gap of sixteen years, linking a teenage conception to the practical demands of a complete theatrical evening.

Not a first rediscoveryBach’s music had never vanished. Mendelssohn’s 1829 St Matthew Passion performance was nevertheless a decisive public event: a shortened, adapted presentation in Berlin’s Sing-Akademie, not a modern complete performance with period instruments.

In March 1829 he conducted the St Matthew Passion in Berlin, assisted by the singer Eduard Devrient and the Sing-Akademie’s musicians. The performance helped make Bach’s large vocal works a subject for public concert life. Mendelssohn’s later oratorios drew on that inheritance without simply reproducing it: narrative soloists, crowd choruses, reflective arias and contrapuntal movements serve nineteenth-century halls and choral societies. His promotion of earlier music also extended to Handel and Beethoven, while contemporary programmes included music by friends and colleagues rather than an exclusively historical canon.

TravelA sketch before the cave

His first British visit in 1829 brought conducting and piano appearances, followed by a Scottish tour with Karl Klingemann. He sent his family a musical sketch from Tobermory on 7 August; the visit to Staffa and Fingal’s Cave followed the next day. The familiar story of an overture conceived inside the cave reverses the order of the evidence. The Hebrides developed through later revision, retaining the low, falling opening figure but transforming it through a sonata design. The sea supplied a remembered experience, not a bar-by-bar programme of waves and weather.

Italy prompted another work whose eventual form resisted its initial circumstances. The A major ‘Italian’ Symphony was performed in London in 1833, but Mendelssohn continued revising it and withheld it from publication. Its finale, a fast Saltarello, turns to A minor and does not restore a major-key ending. The ‘Scottish’ Symphony, begun after the 1829 tour and completed in 1842, similarly required years of distance. Its four movements are intended to follow without substantial breaks; its final A major section changes metre and character rather than merely speeding up the preceding finale.

Publication is not chronologyThe numbered symphonies are ordered largely by publication. The ‘Italian’, No. 4, precedes the completed ‘Scottish’, No. 3; the ‘Reformation’, No. 5, was composed earlier than either completed score.

LeipzigAn institution and its music

After a difficult appointment in Düsseldorf, Mendelssohn took charge of Leipzig’s Gewandhaus concerts in 1835. He married Cécile Jeanrenaud in 1837; they had five children. Leipzig became the centre of work that combined conducting, composing, correspondence, teaching and the organisation of performances. In 1839 he conducted the first public performance of Schubert’s ‘Great’ C major Symphony, whose manuscript Schumann had brought to the publishers’ attention. In 1843 he helped establish the Leipzig Conservatoire, bringing practical ensemble music and a rigorous compositional curriculum into the same institution.

The D minor Piano Trio of 1839 and E minor Violin Concerto, completed in 1844, test classical forms from within. The concerto puts the soloist’s principal theme almost at the beginning, places the written cadenza before the first movement’s recapitulation, and connects its movements. Consultation with the violinist Ferdinand David helped make its passagework specific to the instrument. Elijah, first performed in Birmingham in 1846, addresses another kind of virtuosity: the large amateur chorus, which must become crowd, commentator and contrapuntal ensemble. Its English premiere was followed by further revision.

Fanny’s sudden death in May 1847 was followed by a sharp deterioration in Mendelssohn’s health. His String Quartet in F minor, Op. 80, belongs to that summer: tremolo, forceful accents and close contrapuntal exchanges replace the lightness often made to stand for his entire style. He died in Leipzig on 4 November, aged thirty-eight. Later antisemitic criticism, especially Wagner’s, helped caricature his music as facile; the manuscripts, revision history and practical demands of the scores preserve a less convenient record of labour.

IIThe Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞String articulation

Soft, rapid notes require coordinated bow strokes and carefully spaced entries. In the Octet scherzo and Midsummer Night’s Dream, the effect depends on rests and lightness of attack as much as on speed. A thick legato obscures the written texture.

𝄢Counterpoint

Bach’s procedures remain available inside sonata movements, scherzos and oratorios. The Octet finale builds an eight-part fugato and later recalls the scherzo: contrapuntal accumulation and cyclic memory reinforce one another.

𝄋Form without pauses

The Violin Concerto moves its cadenza before the recapitulation, allowing the orchestra to re-enter over the soloist’s continuing arpeggios. A held bassoon note links the first and second movements. Formal continuity is made with an audible sustained sound.

𝅘𝅥Song at the keyboard

The Songs without Words ask a percussion instrument to sustain a vocal phrase above a separately articulated accompaniment. Their titles do not imply missing poems that must be supplied; melodic rhythm, register and harmony already make distinctions that words might narrow.

♭Landscape & tonality

The Hebrides begins in the middle and low registers, with a descending figure that opens into changing harmonic perspectives. The ‘Italian’ Symphony moves from an A major first movement to an A minor finale. A geographical title does not predict either work’s tonal destination.

VSister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Turner visited Staffa in 1831, two years after Mendelssohn. His painting includes a steamship: Romantic access to the apparently remote landscape depended on modern transport. The overture and painting are independent responses to the same Hebridean destination, not an illustration and its soundtrack. Turner blurs sea, smoke and atmosphere; Mendelssohn keeps a precisely articulated motif audible while its orchestral surroundings change. Collection record.

William Shakespeare

A Midsummer Night’s Dream · Act V, scene 1

First published 1600
EnglishIf we shadows have offended, Think but this, and all is mended, That you have but slumbered here While these visions did appear. And this weak and idle theme, No more yielding but a dream,

Puck’s epilogue supplies a theatrical exit from confusion rather than an explanation of it. Mendelssohn’s 1842 incidental score returns to the 1826 overture’s wind chords at the end, making musical recollection serve a comparable closing function. The quick string writing is not generic woodland music: it belongs to a play that repeatedly changes the status of what its characters think they have seen.

Mendelssohn’s historical repertory and contemporary composing were interdependent. His Leipzig institutions helped sustain Schumann’s music, while the concert overture, piano song and fast scherzo became usable models for later composers. Fanny was an active musical interlocutor throughout, not merely the dedicatee of a family tradition.
