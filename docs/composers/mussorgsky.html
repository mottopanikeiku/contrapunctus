Modest Mussorgsky

Born 21 March 1839, Karevo, Pskov Governorate, Russia
Died 28 March 1881, Saint Petersburg

He made Russian speech, public crowds and the noises of ordinary life determine the shape of opera.

Life

Mussorgsky grew up on his family’s estate at Karevo, where his mother taught him the piano and his nurse supplied stories that he later associated with his earliest improvisations. The household belonged to the landowning class. The peasant voices he eventually put into songs and operas came from another social world. Sent to Saint Petersburg for schooling, he studied piano with Anton Gerke and entered the School of Guards Cadets in 1852. In 1856 he joined the Preobrazhensky Regiment. His first published piano piece, a polka, belonged to the drawing room.

Meeting Dargomyzhsky and then Balakirev redirected that education. Balakirev taught through playing, criticism and the examination of scores. Mussorgsky left the army in 1858, but composing did not provide a living. The emancipation of the serfs in 1861 disrupted the estate income on which his family depended, and he eventually took civil-service posts. The group later called the Five brought together different professions and musical methods: Borodin was a chemist, Cui a military engineer, Rimsky-Korsakov a naval officer. Their shared opposition to imported academic authority concealed substantial musical disagreements.

The spoken lineFrom song to theatre

In the 1860s Mussorgsky increasingly treated a song as a scene between particular people. Kalistrat, setting Nekrasov, opposes a mother’s promises of future prosperity to her son’s poverty. Other songs imitate a beggar, a seminarian or a child without giving them the polished, symmetrical phrases of a salon romance. The singer’s repeated notes, interrupted clauses and changes of pace follow the words; the piano can contradict the speaker. Mussorgsky chose, exaggerated and repeated details until an individual voice became legible in musical time.

Prose on stageDargomyzhsky’s The Stone Guest set Pushkin’s dramatic text with very little alteration. Mussorgsky’s unfinished The Marriage pursued the experiment with Gogol’s prose, where no verse metre regularises the dialogue.

He completed only the first act of The Marriage in 1868. Its abrupt exchanges and conversational rhythm were a laboratory for Boris Godunov, begun the same year. An earlier attempt at large historical opera, Salammbô, after Flaubert, had already supplied musical material he would reuse. There was also an orchestral experiment: the original St John’s Night on the Bare Mountain, completed in 1867. Balakirev’s hostility helped prevent its performance. Its hard attacks, compressed motives and accumulation of orchestral layers differ substantially from the smoother version by Rimsky-Korsakov that later became standard in the concert hall.

Boris GodunovA ruler and the people

The first Boris, completed in 1869, used Pushkin’s play and Karamzin’s history to construct seven scenes around the tsar’s guilt and the succession crisis. The Imperial Theatres rejected it. In the revision of 1871–72, Mussorgsky met a demand for a principal female role by adding Marina and the Polish scenes, changing the political geography. The Kromy forest scene gave collective disorder a new prominence. The revised opera reached the Mariinsky Theatre in 1874, with cuts. The two authorial versions have different dramatic emphases; combining their scenes produces a third construction.

Boris’s private disintegration is set against a crowd that changes its language with its circumstances. At the opening the people are ordered to implore him to accept the crown; at the coronation they sing praise among bells. In the monastery, Pimen’s measured narration turns a murder into historical evidence. In the inn, Varlaam’s song about the siege of Kazan interrupts a police investigation with a violently physical miniature. Mussorgsky gives the monk, the fugitive, the tsar and the crowd different rhythmic habits. The orchestra marks recollection and fear, but the words and the social situation keep altering the dimensions of the scene.

Whose score?Rimsky-Korsakov’s editions regularised harmony, voice-leading and orchestration. Pavel Lamm’s twentieth-century editions restored access to Mussorgsky’s texts. An opera recording’s edition matters as much as its cast.

The same method works on a smaller scale in The Nursery, the seven songs written in 1868–72. A child tells a story, receives a reprimand, remembers a beetle or plays with a toy horse. Mussorgsky wrote the texts himself, allowing syntax and music to develop together. The piano can establish a repeated action, stop while the speaker protests, then resume at a changed speed. These songs later interested Debussy, who heard in Mussorgsky an alternative to the division of opera into formal aria and connective recitative.

1874Pictures and closed rooms

Viktor Hartmann died in 1873. A memorial exhibition organised the following year brought together his architectural fantasies, travel drawings and theatrical designs. Mussorgsky’s Pictures at an Exhibition, written for piano in June 1874, gives ten pictures different genres, registers and scales. The changing Promenade introduces a moving observer. A troubadour sings before the old castle, unhatched chicks produce a scherzo, and a gate that Hartmann never built becomes the suite’s public conclusion. The Promenade enters the pictures themselves, most explicitly when the visitor addresses the dead in Cum mortuis in lingua mortua.

The piano writing is frequently unpianistic in a productive sense: block chords, exposed octaves and register changes make the keyboard suggest several distinct bodies of sound. Ravel’s 1922 orchestration is only one response, although its opening trumpet and saxophone in The Old Castle have become inseparable from many listeners’ memories. In the same year as Pictures, Mussorgsky wrote Sunless, six songs to poems by Arseny Golenishchev-Kutuzov. Their isolation, suspended phrases and sparse accompaniments are a different use of the same ability to make a small change of register or harmony carry dramatic information.

Unfinished historiesThe last decade

Khovanshchina, begun in 1872, deals with the struggles surrounding Peter the Great’s emergence: the streltsy, competing political factions and the Old Believers. Mussorgsky compressed events from different years. The drama was left without a complete orchestral score or a fully finished ending. The Fair at Sorochyntsi, after Gogol, was also unfinished; its comic village world reused music from the earlier witches’ sabbath. These projects occupied the same years as Songs and Dances of Death, four settings of Golenishchev-Kutuzov in which Death takes the roles of nurse, lover, dancer and victorious commander.

Completing KhovanshchinaRimsky-Korsakov, Ravel, Stravinsky and Shostakovich all worked on performing materials at different dates. Modern productions must still choose an orchestration and an ending.

Alcohol dependence, unstable accommodation and the loss of regular employment increasingly constrained Mussorgsky’s work. In 1879 he toured southern Russia as accompanist to the contralto Darya Leonova, playing his own music as well as supporting her repertory. After a collapse in 1881 he entered the Nikolaevsky Military Hospital, where Repin painted him over several sittings. He died there a week after his forty-second birthday.

Publication and performance initially depended on the very colleagues who doubted his musical choices. Rimsky-Korsakov preserved and promoted music that might otherwise have remained inaccessible, while changing its surface and sometimes its structure. The later return to Mussorgsky’s manuscripts revealed thinner orchestral textures, stranger chord connections and a sharper separation of voices. Shostakovich made new orchestrations of both historical operas and of Songs and Dances of Death; his own Fourteenth Symphony continued the latter’s confrontation between individual deaths and public language. Hearing the original piano songs beside the operas reveals Mussorgsky’s equally exact writing for a monumental crowd and for one speaking person.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Speech & melody

A repeated pitch can carry several syllables before a small interval changes the direction of a sentence. Mussorgsky lets Russian verbal stress determine phrase length and accent. Compare the children of The Nursery with Pimen’s even, chronicle-like delivery in Boris.

Harmony

Triads need not behave as tonic, dominant and preparation. Whole chord-shapes can move together, or one harmonic layer can remain while another shifts above it. Modal cadences and chromatic juxtapositions coexist: the church-like chorale of The Great Gate of Kyiv belongs to the same suite as the exposed dissonances of Catacombs.

Rhythm

The Promenade alternates 5/4 and 6/4; its uneven lengths are written into the bar structure, not produced by expressive rubato. In the songs, changes of metre accommodate questions, interruptions and lists. Repeated figures can also become coercive: a dance or military rhythm continues while the situation around it deteriorates.

Dramatic form

Boris Godunov juxtaposes scenes whose place, social group and musical genre change abruptly. A coronation, a chronicle, an inn song and a hallucination each take their own shape. Recurrent motives connect them, but the large-scale argument depends on which scenes follow one another — hence the importance of choosing an edition.

Texture & register

Unisons, octaves and widely separated blocks often replace continuous inner-part filling. In Pictures, a change from naked melody to chordal mass can suggest the entry of a community. The piano’s decay also matters: a struck chord can leave a silence that no sustained orchestral arrangement reproduces exactly.

Character

The accompaniment may know more than the singer. In Songs and Dances of Death, familiar genres become instruments of deception: the lullaby promises rest, the serenade promises love, the dance promises movement. Their recognisable rhythmic forms make Death’s language persuasive before the outcome exposes it.

Sister arts

Objects and texts, with the nature of each musical connection made explicit.

Documented encounter

Repin painted the composer in the Nikolaevsky Military Hospital shortly before his death. The open hospital gown, uncombed hair and direct gaze exclude the conventions of the official composer portrait. Repin and Mussorgsky belonged to overlapping artistic circles around Stasov; the painting records their encounter.

Documented use

Hartmann’s unbuilt gate, with its helmet-shaped tower, bell chamber and large central arch, supplied Mussorgsky with a proposal for public architecture. The final Picture combines a broad E♭ major theme, a quieter chant-like passage and bell textures; the Promenade’s returning material places the observer inside that imagined ceremony.

Documented use

Hartmann designed eggshell costumes for children in Julius Gerber’s ballet Trilby, choreographed by Marius Petipa. Mussorgsky’s Ballet of the Unhatched Chicks translates a theatrical contrivance into high-register staccato figures, grace notes and trills. The shell is part of a dancer’s costume: the source is already an artifice before music transforms it.
