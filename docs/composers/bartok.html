Life

In 1904 Bartók heard Lidi Dósa, a young woman from Transylvania, singing to the children in her care. He wrote down her songs. Here was Hungarian music unlike the urban entertainment that usually went by that name, and he wanted more of it. Two years later, collecting with Bartók, the writer Béla Balázs found that the frail-looking pianist could wear him out: “I was already very tired, but he kept urging and driving me on to collect more!” Balázs put the encounter in his diary.

1881–1907From the Academy to the village

Bartók was born in Nagyszentmiklós in 1881, when the town belonged to Hungary; it is now Sânnicolau Mare in Romania. His father, director of an agricultural school, died when he was seven. His mother Paula supported Béla and his sister by teaching, moving between towns before settling in Pozsony, now Bratislava. At the Budapest Academy he studied piano with István Thomán, who had studied with Liszt, and composition with Hans Koessler.

In 1902 Strauss’s Also sprach Zarathustra set him alight. His own Kossuth, written the next year, celebrated the Hungarian revolutionary and mocked the Austrian imperial anthem. Then came Dósa’s songs and the friendship with Zoltán Kodály. In 1906 they published a joint collection of Hungarian folk-song settings. Kodály also brought back music by Debussy from Paris; Bartók found pentatonic phrases there that recalled the songs he was collecting.

A singer’s timeParlando-rubato means a flexible delivery shaped by the words; tempo giusto keeps a regular pulse. Bartók’s transcriptions distinguish them, along with ornaments, changing verses and the small turns a singer makes at a phrase’s end.

A phonograph cylinder let him hear a performance again after the singer had gone. He collected thousands of Hungarian, Slovak and Romanian melodies, comparing them across borders. In 1913 he went to Biskra in Algeria to record rural Arab music; in 1936 he worked in Turkey with Ahmet Adnan Saygun. Back at his desk he classified songs by their syllables, phrase endings and melodic shapes, returning to his transcriptions to correct and compare them.

The tunes entered his scores in different ways. For Children gives young pianists Hungarian and Slovak melodies with accompaniments fitted closely to them. Elsewhere he invented songs that sounded as though someone might have sung them in a village. A quartet could absorb the uneven beats and pungent intervals without borrowing a tune at all. From 1907 he taught piano at the Academy, paying for a life divided between his own keyboard and other people’s songs.

1907–23Love and locked doors

He fell in love with the violinist Stefi Geyer in 1907 and wrote her a concerto. She did not return his love; the complete concerto remained unpublished in his lifetime. Music associated with her also enters the First Quartet, whose slow opening gradually works towards a fast dance. In 1909 he married Márta Ziegler; their son Béla was born the next year.

For Duke Bluebeard’s Castle in 1911, Balázs supplied two people and seven locked doors. Judith demands the keys; Bluebeard gives them up one at a time. Each room has its own orchestral sound, and the blood Judith keeps discovering stains the music with a semitone. The fifth door opens on Bluebeard’s kingdom: a tremendous C major chord, with an organ beneath it. The opera waited until 1918 for its Budapest première, after the ballet The Wooden Prince, another Balázs collaboration, succeeded in 1917.

The Miraculous Mandarin brings the violence into a city. Three men use a girl to lure customers whom they can rob; the stranger who arrives survives their attempts to kill him until she embraces him. Bartók began the score in 1918–19 and orchestrated it in 1924. Its sliding wind lines and pounding repetitions made a very different theatre from Bluebeard. The 1926 Cologne première provoked a local ban.

War and new borders had curtailed his collecting. He worked increasingly on the recordings and notebooks already in hand, while tours and Universal Edition carried his compositions abroad. The Dance Suite, commissioned for Budapest’s fiftieth anniversary in 1923, joined invented tunes in several folk idioms. That year he divorced Márta and married his piano pupil Ditta Pásztory. Their son Péter was born in 1924; Ditta would become his partner at a second piano.

1926–39The piano strikes, the cello sings

In 1926 he wrote the Piano Sonata, First Piano Concerto and Out of Doors. The piano could hammer like a drum, then hold a whole night in its dying sounds. In ‘The Night’s Music’, quiet dissonant chords lie behind isolated calls and a sustained melody. This is one of Bartók’s most personal sounds: something small and alive stirring while everything around it seems still.

The quartet’s archIn the Fourth Quartet, I answers V and II answers IV, around a solitary slow movement. The Fifth has the same five-part symmetry, with a scherzo at its centre.

The Second Quartet had ended slowly and desolately in 1917. The Third, in 1927, compressed its argument into connected sections of scraping bows, slides and close imitation. The Fourth, a year later, put a singing cello at its quiet centre and made the fourth movement entirely pizzicato, including strings snapped against the fingerboard. Elizabeth Sprague Coolidge commissioned the Fifth in 1934. Its central scherzo counts nine quavers as 4+2+3: an uneven dance with a perfectly dependable step.

For Cantata profana in 1930, he took Romanian texts about nine hunters transformed into stags and wrote his own melodies. Explaining the work to Octavian Beu on 10 January 1931, he described his guiding idea as “brotherhood between peoples, a brotherhood despite all war and strife”. He remained a Hungarian composer, he insisted, while welcoming Slovak, Romanian and Arabic influences. The nine sons cannot return to their father’s house: their antlers will no longer fit through the door.

In 1934 he left regular Academy teaching to prepare an edition of some 13,000 Hungarian songs at the Academy of Sciences. Mikrokosmos, assembled chiefly in 1932–39, brought his discoveries to the piano student in 153 pieces. Two hands learn to imitate, contradict and keep different accents. A child’s exercise can contain the same problem as a quartet.

Paul Sacher commissioned Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta in 1936. Bartók divided the strings into two orchestras: their opening fugue expands from A to an E♭ climax, while later movements throw phrases across the stage. In the Adagio, a xylophone repeats one note and timpani slide between pitches. The Sonata for Two Pianos and Percussion followed in 1937; Bartók and Ditta played at the Basel première in January 1938. In the Second Violin Concerto, written in 1937–38, the finale transforms the first movement’s themes and the slow movement is itself a set of variations.

1940–45Music in exile

After the Anschluss in 1938, Bartók moved his publishing to Boosey & Hawkes in London. Hungary’s drift towards Nazism made staying intolerable. The Sixth Quartet of 1939 begins each movement with a sorrowful Mesto; by the finale, sorrow occupies the whole movement. His mother died that December. Following a spring concert visit to America, he and Ditta left Europe in autumn 1940.

Work at ColumbiaIn New York Bartók transcribed and classified recordings from Milman Parry’s South Slavic collection. His American research continued the comparative work he had begun with cylinders in Hungarian villages.

In his 1943 Harvard lectures he explained how two different modes could share one home note. Lydian and Phrygian together could supply all twelve notes of the chromatic scale. He called this ‘polymodal chromaticism’; the piano examples below make the idea plain.

Concert work was scarce, and leukaemia was taking hold. Koussevitzky’s 1943 commission for the Concerto for Orchestra brought him back to a large score. Its second movement sends out wind players in pairs, each pair keeping its own interval; a grave central Elegy stands between the lighter movements. Koussevitzky conducted the Boston première in December 1944.

Menuhin commissioned the Sonata for Solo Violin in 1944. The Third Piano Concerto was for Ditta, with a quiet Adagio religioso interrupted by birdsong. Bartók died in New York on 26 September 1945, still a Hungarian citizen, leaving its last seventeen bars to be orchestrated by Tibor Serly and the Viola Concerto in sketches. His folk-music collections continued to appear after his death. In 1988 his remains returned to Budapest.

The Sound

𝅘𝅥Folk melody

In the Romanian Folk Dances, Bartók keeps the borrowed tunes recognisable and supplies new accompaniments. The Dance Suite uses invented melodies with familiar rural turns. Cantata profana takes Romanian words but gives them music of his own. His choices range from preserving a particular singer’s tune to writing a quartet in which no tune is quoted, though village rhythms are everywhere.

♮Modes and semitones

Two modes can share a home note and disagree about the others. C Lydian has F♯; C Phrygian has D♭, E♭, A♭ and B♭. Put them together and all twelve notes become available, with C still at the centre. In Bluebeard, a much smaller clash does the work: two adjacent notes keep announcing the presence of blood.

𝄾Unequal beats

The Fifth Quartet’s scherzo groups nine quavers as 4+2+3; its trio groups ten as 3+2+2+3. The small units remain equal while the larger steps change length. Bartók called this Bulgarian rhythm. Elsewhere a singer’s flexible, speech-shaped timing leaves the bar line loose, or a piano part pushes accents across a regular metre until it seems to limp.

𝄋Returns and arches

The Fourth Quartet has five movements: the outer pair share themes, the inner pair answer one another, and the slow third stands alone. The Sixth makes a sad introduction grow until it fills the finale. In the Second Violin Concerto, the last movement rewrites the first. Bartók often makes the ending familiar enough to recognise and different enough to hurt.

𝆏Night music

In Out of Doors, soft piano clusters linger while little calls flicker above them. Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta enlarges the night: one xylophone note quickens and slows, timpani slide, and strings hold the air around those noises. The Fourth Quartet adds a different shock when a plucked string snaps back against the fingerboard. Wood suddenly joins the music.

𝄢Counterpoint and space

The opening fugue of Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta begins on A and spreads through entries climbing and descending in fifths towards E♭. The string players sit in two groups, so later exchanges travel visibly and audibly across the platform. At the opposite extreme, the Solo Violin Sonata asks one player to suggest several pursuing voices with double stops and changes of pitch.

Sister Arts

Bartók brings the phonograph to Zobordarázs, now Dražovce, in 1907. A singer faces its horn while the neighbours gather around: the melodies in For Children began with people like these. The cylinder preserved a particular performance, including the details he could return to later with a pencil and manuscript paper.

István Kováts photographed Bartók at Gyergyószentmiklós, now Gheorgheni, during his first Transylvanian collecting trip in 1907. That summer he wrote to Stefi Geyer about the pentatonic songs he was discovering. The violin concerto he wrote for her and the musical discoveries made on the road belong to the same crowded year.

Csontváry travelled around Europe and the eastern Mediterranean looking for subjects; Bartók went abroad with his phonograph. Here the painter gives one enormous tree the picture to itself. Its few living branches against the open sky make a fitting companion to Bartók’s night pieces, where a single call can fill an otherwise quiet space.

Csontváry’s canvas is nearly six metres wide. The empty theatre stands close to us, with the coast and mountain far beyond: he makes a monumental picture from sharply separated distances. Bartók’s two string orchestras in Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta offer a musical counterpart, answering across the platform while the percussion occupies its own space.

Recordings to own

  • Violin Sonata No. 2 · Rhapsody No. 1Joseph Szigeti, violin; Béla Bartók, pianoVanguard · 1940The essential historical choice, live at the Library of Congress on 13 April. Szigeti slides between notes and Bartók rolls chords, answering his partner with an ease that many tidier performances lose.
  • Concerto for OrchestraChicago Symphony Orchestra, Fritz ReinerRCA Victor · 1955The wind pairs have character, and the finale’s fast counterpoint has teeth. Recorded on 22 October 1955; Reiner had studied with Bartók in Budapest.
  • Piano Concertos Nos. 1–3Géza Anda; Berlin Radio Symphony Orchestra, Ferenc FricsayDeutsche Grammophon · 1959–60A first choice for the complete set. Anda can make the early concertos strike hard without losing their dancing feet; the Third has room to sing. Fricsay keeps the orchestral replies alert.
  • Duke Bluebeard’s CastleChrista Ludwig, Walter Berry; London Symphony Orchestra, István KertészDecca · 1965A formidable pair of voices, with Decca’s Kingsway Hall sound giving the opening of each door its full reach. Ludwig’s Judith and Berry’s Bluebeard make the argument feel dangerously close.
  • The Six String QuartetsTakács QuartetDecca · 1996An excellent complete cycle for the dances as well as the dark slow movements. The Fourth’s plucked scherzo has physical bite, and the Sixth’s returning Mesto keeps its place in the unfolding argument.

Lineage

Drew fromJohann Sebastian BachLudwig van BeethovenFranz LisztRichard StraussClaude DebussyZoltán KodályHungarian, Romanian, Slovak and other rural musicians
Bartók
Passed toGyörgy LigetiGyörgy KurtágPierre BoulezWitold Lutosławski

Kodály was the indispensable companion in the field; village singers and instrumentalists supplied music that neither man could have invented at a desk. Debussy helped Bartók hear new possibilities in their modes, while Beethoven remained a guide to building a quartet from a few related phrases. Ligeti and Kurtág grew up with his music; Boulez kept returning to the orchestral scores as a conductor.
