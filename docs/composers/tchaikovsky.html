Born 7 May 1840, Votkinsk, Russia

Life

Tchaikovsky was born at Votkinsk, where his father managed an ironworks. Music belonged to the household before it became a profession: his governess Fanny Dürbach remembered the child’s intense responses to it, and he began piano lessons early. His formal training nevertheless pointed towards government service. In 1850 he entered the preparatory class of the Imperial School of Jurisprudence in Saint Petersburg, separated from his family. His mother died of cholera in 1854. After graduating in 1859 he worked in the Ministry of Justice, while concerts, opera and lessons gradually occupied more of his time.

When Anton Rubinstein’s Saint Petersburg Conservatory opened in 1862, Tchaikovsky joined its first generation of students. He studied composition with Rubinstein and theoretical subjects with Nikolai Zaremba, leaving government employment in 1863. The training mattered practically: he could organise large forms, prepare orchestral scores and teach harmony. In 1866 Nikolai Rubinstein recruited him for the new Moscow Conservatory. Tchaikovsky taught there until 1878, wrote criticism and translated pedagogical material. His early reputation grew through this sustained professional work.

Between institutionsA Russian composer with European forms

The conservatories and Balakirev’s circle had distinct identities, but their musicians exchanged ideas. Balakirev proposed the subject of Romeo and Juliet and criticised successive versions; Tchaikovsky listened, argued and revised. The overture-fantasy, first written in 1869 and given its final form in 1880, makes a sonata exposition carry a dramatic opposition: the feud is rhythmic and percussive, while the lovers’ music opens another register and tempo of attention. The Friar’s introductory chorale provides a harmonic frame that returns around the conflict.

His First Piano Concerto, completed in its initial form in 1875, survived Nikolai Rubinstein’s severe objections and was premiered in Boston by Hans von Bülow. Its large D♭ major introduction stands apart from the first movement’s main B♭ minor argument, demonstrating that a memorable melody need not recur to determine a work’s proportions. Swan Lake, composed in 1875–76, transferred that ability to the theatre. Its 1877 Moscow production did not establish the ballet’s later reputation; the familiar performing tradition owes much to the posthumous 1895 revival, with choreography by Petipa and Ivanov and alterations to the score by Riccardo Drigo.

1877Marriage, patronage and composition

The documentary recordTchaikovsky’s attraction to men is documented in letters and diaries. Later editions sometimes suppressed passages from those documents.

In July 1877 he married Antonina Milyukova, who had written to him declaring her love. The marriage rapidly became intolerable to him; by the autumn they were living apart, though they never divorced. The couple brought different expectations to the marriage; Antonina, too, was vulnerable. Tchaikovsky’s fear of exposure and desire to conform to marriage shaped the decision. The crisis coincided with work on Eugene Onegin and the Fourth Symphony.

Nadezhda von Meck’s financial support, beginning in 1877, allowed him to leave regular teaching. They deliberately conducted their relationship by letter, exchanging views on music, daily routines and personal distress. He dedicated the Fourth Symphony to her without publicly naming her. In a letter explaining it, he associated its opening brass idea with fate. The motto interrupts the first movement’s unstable waltz motion and returns in the finale, breaking into a communal dance based partly on the song In the Field Stood a Birch Tree.

Waltz under pressureThe Fourth’s first movement is principally in 9/8. Its recurring dotted figures disturb the easy three-beat sway; the tension is in the accompaniment and accent as much as in the melody.

Eugene Onegin, first performed by Moscow Conservatory students in 1879, puts a different kind of interruption on stage. Tatyana’s letter scene suspends ordinary household time; Onegin’s response replaces her expansive speculation with controlled phrases. Public dances then expose private failures: the country name-day party leads to Lensky’s challenge, while the final Saint Petersburg ball reveals Tatyana’s changed social position. Tchaikovsky called the work “lyrical scenes”, selecting episodes from Pushkin. The orchestra remembers emotions that the characters cannot recover by speaking about them.

The working composerTravel and the theatre

The years after 1878 brought long periods abroad as well as rented country houses near Moscow. He wrote the Violin Concerto in Switzerland in 1878, the String Serenade and 1812 overture in 1880, and the Piano Trio in memory of Nikolai Rubinstein in 1881–82. The trio’s enormous variation movement makes changes of genre into stages of remembrance before the opening lament returns. His operatic subjects ranged from Schiller’s The Maid of Orleans to Pushkin’s Mazepa. Across these genres he repeatedly reconsidered orchestration, transitions and entire movements.

He became an increasingly active conductor in the later 1880s, taking his music around Europe and visiting the United States in 1891 for concerts marking the opening of Carnegie Hall. The Fifth Symphony, written in 1888, brings a recurring motto through all four movements, ending with a major-key transformation rather than the Fourth’s disruptive return. The ballets required another discipline. For The Sleeping Beauty, premiered in 1890, Petipa supplied detailed choreographic requirements. Tchaikovsky turned prescribed numbers of bars, changes of tempo and character entrances into musical contrasts; the Lilac Fairy and Carabosse are distinguished by recurring orchestral and harmonic ideas.

The Queen of Spades, composed in 1890, sets gambling obsession against a deliberately reconstructed eighteenth century. The older styles of the pastoral entertainment and the Countess’s remembered French song make Hermann’s fixation sound increasingly displaced. The Nutcracker, premiered with Iolanta in 1892, uses the orchestra on an even smaller scale within a large theatrical apparatus. The celesta’s metal plates give the Sugar Plum Fairy a sharply defined attack and a decaying halo; the miniature overture omits the lower strings. Childhood in this score takes shape through register, instrument and scale.

1893A finale that refuses the expected ending

Tchaikovsky settled at Klin in 1892 and completed the Sixth Symphony the following year. He conducted its first performance in Saint Petersburg on 28 October 1893. The second movement’s 5/4 dance balances groups of two and three; the third builds a march that can sound like a finale, then yields to an actual finale marked Adagio lamentoso. At its opening the first and second violins divide the melodic line between them. The listener hears a continuity that neither section plays alone, while the score’s final pages progressively remove motion, register and dynamic force.

The death storiesThe documented contemporary diagnosis was cholera. Later suicide narratives, including a supposed secret court of honour, lack evidence strong enough to replace that account.

He died on 6 November, nine days after the premiere. His surviving correspondence shows pride in the work and plans for further activity. The Sixth’s formal decision is stark: a symphony that has already produced an exhilarating march ends in B minor, with the bass line sinking below an exhausted string texture. The practical legacy spread through musicians as different as Rachmaninoff, who made a piano arrangement of The Sleeping Beauty, and Stravinsky, whose The Fairy’s Kiss reworked Tchaikovsky’s songs and piano pieces into a new ballet.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Melody

A melody can be extended by repeating a small figure at successive pitches. Tchaikovsky combines that sequential technique with delayed cadences: a phrase seems ready to settle, then acquires another ascent or another accompaniment. His theatrical timing depends on knowing how long a listener can wait for the landing.

Rhythm & dance

Waltzes, polonaises, marches and folk dances organise bodies as well as bars. The Sixth’s 5/4 movement makes the accompaniment articulate two beats followed by three. In the Fourth’s opening movement, accent and harmonic repetition prevent the 9/8 metre from becoming a comfortable waltz.

Harmony

Long sequences, pedal notes and chromatic inner lines prolong a local emotion before a modulation changes the scene. Major and minor versions of a recurring idea can carry different formal roles: the Fifth’s motto begins in E minor and is recast in E major in the finale. The transformation is audible because its rhythmic identity survives.

Orchestration

Wind solos often introduce a new degree of privacy inside a large orchestral movement: the oboe in the Fourth’s slow movement, the horn in the Fifth’s. Ballet adds individual colours — celesta, harp, percussion — to a varied orchestral palette. The Nutcracker overture’s restricted register shapes its sound.

Form & recurrence

Recurrent material can interrupt the action. The Fourth’s motto breaks the finale’s dance; the Fifth carries its motto through altered tempos and keys. Romeo and Juliet makes the recapitulation a changed dramatic situation, while the First Piano Concerto lets its imposing introduction disappear after establishing the opening scale.

Theatre

An aria, a ballroom dance and an orchestral recollection each allow a different duration of thought. Tatyana’s letter scene expands private time; the public dances in Onegin make social time audible. In The Queen of Spades, older musical styles become objects of memory and obsession inside the opera’s present.

Sister arts

Documented useAlexander Pushkin

Tatyana’s letter, from Eugene Onegin

Chapter III, published 1827
RussianЯ к вам пишу — чего же боле? Что я могу еще сказать? Теперь, я знаю, в вашей воле Меня презреньем наказать. Но вы, к моей несчастной доле Хоть каплю жалости храня, Вы не оставите меня.
English · Contrapunctus translationI write to you — what more is there? What else can I say? Now, I know, it is in your power To punish me with contempt. But if for my unhappy fate You keep even a drop of pity, You will not abandon me.

Tchaikovsky’s letter scene enlarges a private act of writing into an extended sequence of orchestral anticipation, hesitation and release. Pushkin presents the letter as a translation from Tatyana’s French; the opera gives it directly to her singing voice. The original is available in the Russian text of the passage.

Shared context

One of Bakst’s seven paintings on the Sleeping Beauty story, commissioned as a mural cycle for James de Rothschild. It is separate from Petipa’s 1890 production. Bakst also designed Diaghilev’s London staging of Tchaikovsky’s ballet in 1921: the same artist carried the fairy tale between domestic decoration and the modern stage.

Documented useWilliam Shakespeare

Romeo and Juliet, Act II, scene 2

c. 1595
EnglishBut, soft! what light through yonder window breaks? It is the east, and Juliet is the sun. Arise, fair sun, and kill the envious moon, Who is already sick and pale with grief, That thou her maid art far more fair than she.

Balakirev’s proposed subject allowed Tchaikovsky to contrast the feud’s agitated sonata material with a love theme first presented by cor anglais and muted violas. Shakespeare’s change from public conflict to an intimately observed voice has a musical counterpart in that change of texture.

Later connectionsSergei RachmaninoffIgor StravinskySergei ProkofievDmitri Shostakovich

Rachmaninoff inherited the long sequential melody and the coordination of piano and orchestral mass. Stravinsky’s The Fairy’s Kiss makes the connection explicit through quotation. Prokofiev’s ballets and Shostakovich’s symphonies retain the usefulness of a march or waltz whose public confidence can be contradicted by its context.
