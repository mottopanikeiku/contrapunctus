Life

At Sontsovka, the Ukrainian estate his father managed, Prokofiev grew up hearing his mother play Beethoven and Chopin. She wrote down his first piano pieces. A trip to the Moscow opera in 1900 gave the eight-year-old an ambition of his own: The Giant, an opera for the family to perform at home. Sergei Taneyev recommended proper lessons, and Reinhold Glière spent the summers of 1902 and 1903 at Sontsovka, teaching the boy who was already filling manuscript books.

1904–14The prize was a piano

He entered the St Petersburg Conservatory at thirteen with a bundle of scores. Rimsky-Korsakov taught orchestration, Lyadov harmony and counterpoint; later Anna Esipova took charge of his piano playing. Nikolai Tcherepnin taught conducting and encouraged his taste for Haydn and Mozart. His fellow student Nikolai Myaskovsky became the friend to whom he sent scores and complaints for the rest of his life. In the final piano competition of 1914, Prokofiev played his own First Concerto. He won the Rubinstein Prize: a grand piano.

The Second Concerto had caused an uproar at Pavlovsk the previous year. In its enormous first-movement cadenza, the pianist seems to acquire an orchestra’s worth of hands before the real orchestra crashes back in. The score was later lost, and Prokofiev reconstructed and revised it in 1923. The Toccata, Op. 11, offers a smaller ordeal: repeated notes exchanged between the hands, with scarcely a moment to lift the fingers clear.

1914–17Diaghilev and an absent piano

In London in 1914 he met Sergei Diaghilev, who commissioned a prehistoric ballet and then rejected it. Prokofiev rescued Ala and Lolli as the Scythian Suite, with brass and percussion enough to make a pagan god sit up. Diaghilev tried again with a Russian folk tale, Chout — The Buffoon. After extensive revision it reached Paris in 1921, a succession of practical jokes delivered by an orchestra.

Dreaming violinThe First Violin Concerto, completed in 1917, opens with a melody marked sognando — dreamily — above trembling violas. At the end it returns with a flute beside it.

For the summer of 1917 he went into the country near Petrograd. “I deliberately did not take a piano to the country,” he recalled in his 1941 autobiography: he wanted to find orchestral ideas his fingers would never suggest. The Classical Symphony imagines Haydn alive in the twentieth century, still recognisably himself. Four movements fit into about fifteen minutes; the Gavotte takes enormous steps and lands in unexpected keys. Prokofiev conducted the première on 21 April 1918. He finished the twenty brief Visions fugitives in 1917, too.

1918–29Oranges in Chicago

With official permission to leave Russia, he travelled east in May 1918, through Siberia and Japan to San Francisco. America hired the pianist more readily than it staged the composer’s operas. Cleofonte Campanini commissioned The Love for Three Oranges for Chicago, then died before it could be produced. Prokofiev finally conducted it there on 30 December 1921, in French: a melancholy prince, a witch and three oranges containing princesses, with rival theatrical factions quarrelling round the edges.

Two weeks earlier he had played the Third Piano Concerto in Chicago under Frederick Stock. It opens with a clarinet singing alone; the strings suddenly run, and the piano leaps into their path. After a stay at Ettal in Bavaria, he married the singer Lina Codina in 1923 and settled in Paris. Their sons, Sviatoslav and Oleg, were born in 1924 and 1928.

Serge Koussevitzky promoted the music, though Prokofiev’s gratitude had limits. “You can’t rely on Koussevitzky’s taste,” he wrote to Myaskovsky from Sèvres on 3 January 1924, “though you can’t deny him a nose for things.” The heavily scored Second Symphony disappointed Paris in 1925. Diaghilev’s Le Pas d’acier brought Soviet factory life to the ballet in 1927; Balanchine’s The Prodigal Son followed in 1929. The Fiery Angel remained unstaged. Prokofiev took its obsessive music into his Third Symphony.

1927–38Coming home

A successful Soviet tour in 1927 began the gradual return. Commissions multiplied; he moved his family to Moscow in 1936 and continued travelling abroad until 1938. One early commission was the film Lieutenant Kijé, whose officer exists through a clerk’s mistake. Prokofiev’s concert suite equips this imaginary man with a wedding and a funeral. A tenor saxophone supplies some of his melancholy dignity; sleigh bells take him out for a ride.

Romeo and Juliet, largely written in 1935, had a harder passage to the stage. Its original scenario let both lovers survive. Prokofiev eventually restored Shakespeare’s deaths, while broken agreements with the Kirov and Bolshoi theatres sent the première to Brno in December 1938. Leonid Lavrovsky’s Kirov production followed in January 1940, with Galina Ulanova as Juliet. The knights stamp through their dance as though the floor belongs to them. Juliet’s quick scales grow into the long phrases of a young woman discovering how much she has to lose.

In 1936 Natalia Sats asked him for a children’s piece. Peter and the Wolf puts the grandfather in the bassoon and the wolf in three horns; even a small child can recognise trouble approaching. Two years later Sergei Eisenstein enlisted him for Alexander Nevsky. Sometimes Prokofiev composed to filmed scenes, sometimes Eisenstein filmed to his music. He also experimented with microphones. For the 1939 cantata he rebuilt the score, leaving a mezzo-soprano to search the battlefield in The Field of the Dead.

1939–45War at the keyboard

Seven quaversThe Seventh Sonata’s finale is marked Precipitato, impetuously. Its main theme divides the 7/8 bar into 2 + 3 + 2: the middle step is longer.

He began the Sixth, Seventh and Eighth piano sonatas together in 1939. Sviatoslav Richter introduced the Seventh in January 1943: its warm E major slow movement sits between two bouts of violence, the last hammering towards B♭ major. Emil Gilels gave the Eighth’s première in December 1944. Its opening Andante dolce takes its time, letting the melody wander before the faster music breaks in.

In 1941 Prokofiev left Lina for the writer Mira Mendelson. Evacuated after the German invasion, they worked in Nalchik, Tbilisi and Alma-Ata. Together they made a libretto from War and Peace, beginning with Prince Andrei overhearing Natasha at her window. Official demands enlarged the patriotic scenes; revisions continued for years. Cinderella, completed in 1944, reached the Bolshoi in November 1945, its waltzes interrupted by the terrible punctuality of midnight.

Prokofiev conducted the Fifth Symphony in Moscow on 13 January 1945. Flute and bassoon begin it in octaves; near the finale’s end the full orchestra suddenly shrinks to solo strings, piano, harp and percussion, rattling away before the final blows. The manuscript was written on paper Koussevitzky had sent from Boston to help Soviet composers. Soon after the première, Prokofiev fainted and suffered a concussion in the fall. His health never recovered.

1948–53The music left waiting

FormalismThe Party resolution of February 1948 named Prokofiev, Shostakovich, Myaskovsky and others. Performances disappeared from programmes, including works the authorities had previously rewarded.

The Sixth Symphony, first conducted by Mravinsky in 1947, was caught in the condemnation that followed. Prizes gave no protection. In January 1948 Prokofiev married Mira after a Soviet court invalidated his foreign marriage; on 20 February Lina was arrested on fabricated espionage charges. Sentenced to twenty years, she spent eight in prisons and camps. Illness and the loss of performances left him short of money.

The young Mstislav Rostropovich brought work and friendship: the Cello Sonata of 1949, then the Sinfonia concertante, remade from an earlier concerto. The Seventh Symphony, written for a children’s radio audience in 1952, ends quietly; Prokofiev also supplied a brisk alternative coda. He died on 5 March 1953, the day Stalin died. The Fiery Angel and the complete War and Peace were still waiting for the stage. Lina was still in a camp.

The Sound

♮Tonal side-slips

Prokofiev makes an ordinary chord arrive in the wrong place. The Gavotte of the Classical Symphony keeps its stately dance steps while the harmony takes an unexpected turn. The joke works because D major is still in our ears. In the Third Piano Concerto’s finale, the piano and strings can disagree about the key while keeping perfectly together.

𝄾Motor rhythm

A Prokofiev pulse can outlast everything happening above it. The Second Piano Concerto’s scherzo sends both hands through an unbroken stream of semiquavers in octaves. The Seventh Sonata’s finale makes seven quavers feel as dependable as four crotchets, repeating its 2 + 3 + 2 grouping. The accents give the pianist a firm footing even as the chords become more formidable.

𝆑The striking piano

The Toccata, Op. 11, begins with repeated Ds and turns the keyboard into a machine powered by two hands. Notes are passed back and forth, then crowded by semitone neighbours; the hands spring apart and close again. The Second Concerto gives that physical energy a much larger arena: in the first-movement cadenza, the pianist develops the music alone until the orchestra can scarcely enter loudly enough.

𝅘𝅥The singing line

The First Violin Concerto starts with a dream: a quiet solo violin over trembling violas. Prokofiev knew how to let a melody grow without hurrying its destination. The opening returns near the concerto’s end, now shared with the flute. In Romeo and Juliet, similarly sustained phrases make the lovers seem to have more time than the bustling people around them.

𝄞Instrumental characters

In Peter and the Wolf, a bassoon can scold and an oboe can waddle. Three horns give the wolf a size the flute-bird could never possess. The same ear chose a tenor saxophone for the imaginary officer in Lieutenant Kijé, and paired flute with bassoon to open the Fifth Symphony: two distinct voices singing the same tune an octave apart.

𝄋Old dances, new shoes

Haydn’s four-movement symphony gave Prokofiev a compact home for his harmonic mischief. The Classical Symphony’s Gavotte replaces the expected minuet, with wide leaps that almost trip over its courtly bow. In Cinderella, the old ballet sequence of waltzes, variations and a pas de deux lets each dancer speak in turn. The clock interrupts a form everybody thought they knew.

Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

    Piano Concerto No. 1 in D♭ majorOp. 10Piano and orchestra
    The grand opening keeps returning through a single movement of about fifteen minutes. Prokofiev won his Conservatory piano prize with it in 1914.
  2. 1912
    Toccata in D minorOp. 11Piano
    Repeated Ds set both hands to work; semitone clashes and leaps across the keyboard soon make the simple task perilous. The stream of semiquavers scarcely stops.

  3. 1912–13; revised 1923
    Piano Concerto No. 2 in G minorOp. 16Piano and orchestra
    The first-movement cadenza makes the pianist do much of the orchestra’s work; the scherzo then demands continuous semiquavers in octaves. Today’s score is Prokofiev’s 1923 reconstruction and revision of the lost original.
  4. 1914–15
    Scythian SuiteOp. 20Large orchestra, including expanded brass, percussion, piano and two harps
    Four movements rescued from Ala and Lolli, the ballet Diaghilev rejected. Pagan monsters and a nocturnal spell lead to a final sunrise.
  5. 1915–17
    Visions fugitivesOp. 22Piano
    Twenty miniatures, several over almost before they have begun. The markings run from Lentamente to Ridicolosamente: slowly, then ridiculously.
  6. 1915–17
    Violin Concerto No. 1 in D majorOp. 19Violin and orchestra
    A furious scherzo separates two dreaming outer movements. Completed in 1917, it waited until 1923 for Marcel Darrieux and Koussevitzky to give its Paris première.
  7. 1916–17
    Symphony No. 1 in D major “Classical”Op. 25Orchestra with paired woodwinds, two horns, two trumpets, timpani and strings
    Haydn brought up to date, in four movements and about fifteen minutes. Prokofiev wrote much of it on walks, having left his piano behind.
  8. 1919
    The Love for Three OrangesOp. 33Opera in a prologue and four acts, after Carlo Gozzi
    A prince’s melancholy, a witch’s curse and princesses inside oranges, with rival theatrical factions arguing over the entertainment. Prokofiev conducted the French-language première in Chicago in December 1921.
  9. 1917–21
    Piano Concerto No. 3 in C majorOp. 26Piano and orchestra
    A solitary clarinet begins a concerto that soon has the piano and orchestra snapping at each other. The middle movement gives a small E minor theme five markedly different lives.
  10. 1919–27
    The Fiery AngelOp. 37Opera in five acts, after Valery Bryusov
    Renata’s religious visions and erotic obsession end in a convent overtaken by hysteria. Prokofiev never saw it staged; he carried much of the music into his Third Symphony.
  11. 1933–34
    Lieutenant Kijé SuiteOp. 60Orchestra, with optional baritone in the Romance and Troika
    The five-movement biography of an officer invented by a clerical error. A cornet announces him, a tenor saxophone gives him a melancholy voice, and sleigh bells accompany his winter outing.
  12. 1935–36; revised 1939–40
    Romeo and JulietOp. 64Full-length ballet after Shakespeare; large orchestra with tenor saxophone, cornet, piano and celesta
    The first staging was in Brno in 1938; Lavrovsky’s Kirov production with Ulanova followed in 1940. The heavy knights’ dance and the lovers’ long phrases inhabit the same dangerous household.
  13. 1936
    Peter and the WolfOp. 67Narrator and orchestra
    Written for Natalia Sats’s children’s theatre, with a flute-bird and an oboe-duck among its cast. At the end the duck can still be heard inside the wolf: he swallowed her whole.
  14. 1938–39
    Alexander NevskyOp. 78Cantata for mezzo-soprano, mixed chorus and orchestra
    Prokofiev reorchestrated his music for Eisenstein’s film as a seven-movement cantata. After The Battle on the Ice, a lone mezzo-soprano searches The Field of the Dead.
  15. 1939–44
    Piano Sonatas Nos. 6–8, the “War Sonatas”Opp. 82–84Piano
    Begun together in 1939, then finished separately in 1940, 1942 and 1944. The Seventh races to a 7/8 finish; the Eighth opens with a long, unsettled Andante dolce.
  16. 1940–44
    CinderellaOp. 87Ballet in three acts; scenario by Nikolai Volkov after Charles Perrault
    The sisters have their dancing lesson, Cinderella her great waltz, and midnight the last word at the ball. First staged at the Bolshoi in November 1945.
  17. 1941–52
    War and PeaceOp. 91Opera in thirteen scenes with choral epigraph; libretto by Prokofiev and Mira Mendelson after Tolstoy
    Begins with Andrei overhearing Natasha at her window and expands to the invasion of 1812. Official demands enlarged the patriotic scenes; the composer never heard the whole opera staged.
  18. 1944
    Symphony No. 5 in B♭ majorOp. 100Large orchestra with piano, harp and percussion
    Prokofiev conducted the première in Moscow in January 1945. Near the finale’s end, the large orchestra dwindles to a rattling ensemble of solo strings, piano, harp and percussion.

Sister Arts

Dicksee painted the lovers parting: Romeo is about to descend from Juliet’s balcony. He had first drawn the scene for an illustrated Shakespeare. Prokofiev’s ballet returns to this intimacy at the start of Act III, after the public violence that has killed Mercutio and Tybalt. Morning has become an enemy.

Vereshchagin’s Napoleon I in Russia series takes up the invasion of 1812, the war in Tolstoy’s novel and Prokofiev’s opera. Here the attack comes close enough for individual bodies to fill the canvas. Prokofiev and Mira Mendelson worked on their adaptation while evacuated from Moscow after another invasion, in 1941.

Doré’s engraving comes from an 1867 edition of Perrault’s tales. Nikolai Volkov drew on the same story for Prokofiev’s ballet. Prokofiev made room for the embarrassing business of learning to dance: the sisters get a lesson before they go to the ball. Cinderella’s own great waltz arrives only after the fairy has intervened.

Roerich’s imagined pagan sanctuary belonged to the patron Maria Tenisheva, who gave the sketch to the Russian Museum in 1910. Its carved idols share the prehistoric world Diaghilev requested from Prokofiev for Ala and Lolli. The impresario rejected that ballet; its gods survived in the brass and percussion of the Scythian Suite.

Leo Tolstoy

War and Peace: Natasha at the window

1865–69; Volume II, Part III, Chapter II
Russian prose— Соня! Соня! — послышался опять первый голос.— Ну, как можно спать! Да ты посмотри, что за прелесть! Ах, какая прелесть! Да проснись же, Соня,— сказала она почти со слезами в голосе.— Ведь эдакой прелестной ночи никогда, никогда не бывало. Соня неохотно что-то отвечала. — Нет, ты посмотри, что за луна!.. Ах, какая прелесть! Ты поди сюда. Душенька, голубушка, поди сюда. Ну, видишь? Так бы вот села на корточки, вот так, подхватила бы себя под коленки — туже, как можно туже, натужиться надо,— и полетела бы. Вот так!
Our translation“Sonya! Sonya!” the first voice sounded again. “How can you sleep! Just look how lovely it is! Oh, how lovely! Do wake up, Sonya,” she said, almost in tears. “There has never, never been such a lovely night.” Sonya answered something reluctantly. “No, just look at that moon! Oh, how lovely! Come here. Darling, my dear, come here. Now, do you see? I would squat down like this, tuck my hands under my knees — tighter, as tight as possible, you have to make an effort — and fly away. Like this!”

Natasha wakes Sonya because the moon is too beautiful to sleep through. Below them, the widowed Andrei overhears and begins to want a life again. Prokofiev and Mira Mendelson chose this episode for their opera’s first scene; the great public history begins with two girls at a bedroom window. The Russian text follows the Russian Virtual Library edition.

Recordings to own

  • Piano Concerto No. 3Sergei Prokofiev; London Symphony Orchestra, Piero CoppolaHMV; reissued by Naxos Historical · 1932The composer’s own playing is quick and surprisingly light, with sharply graded touches and little patience for grandstanding. The finale has the excitement of soloist and orchestra catching each other on the run.
  • Piano Concerto No. 3Martha Argerich; Berlin Philharmonic, Claudio AbbadoDeutsche Grammophon · 1967The first choice for sheer nerve: Argerich attacks the piano’s leaps as though they were the easiest way across the keyboard. Abbado’s Berlin orchestra gives her brilliance an equally brilliant partner.
  • Piano Sonatas Nos. 7 and 9Sviatoslav RichterNaxos Historical, Early Recordings Vol. 2, 8.111387 · 1958The Seventh’s finale has a force that makes most rivals sound cautious. Beside it sits the quiet Ninth, written for Richter: an especially revealing pairing from the pianist who introduced both works.
  • Piano Sonata No. 8Sviatoslav RichterDeutsche Grammophon · 1961A first choice for the Eighth’s long opening movement. Richter gives the returning melody enough room to seem remembered, while keeping the faster music within the same immense span.
  • Romeo and Juliet, complete balletCleveland Orchestra, Lorin MaazelDecca · 1973Cleveland’s hard attacks make the feud dangerous, and Maazel lets the love music expand lavishly. The great virtue is continuity: the complete ballet moves with the urgency of an evening in the theatre.
  • Symphony No. 5Berlin Philharmonic, Herbert von KarajanDeutsche GrammophonA sumptuous orchestra with a real sting: the tam-tam at the first movement’s climax is tremendous. Karajan keeps the finale moving towards its unnerving last pages without letting the polished playing blunt their attack.
  • Alexander Nevsky, cantataVěra Soukupová; Czech Philharmonic Orchestra and Chorus, Karel AnčerlSupraphon · 1962The recorded sound is hard, but so is the battle: Ančerl gives the charging rhythms extraordinary bite. Soukupová’s firm, unshowy lament brings the scene back to one grieving person.
  • War and PeaceYelena Prokina, Alexander Gergalov and cast; Kirov Chorus and Orchestra, Valery GergievPhilips · 1991Prokina’s Natasha is the reason to choose this set: young, vulnerable and entirely at home in the Russian words. Gergiev supplies urgent theatre; the dry Mariinsky recording gives the orchestra less room than it deserves.

Prokofiev
Passed toDmitri ShostakovichDmitri KabalevskySviatoslav RichterMstislav RostropovichFrederick AshtonKenneth MacMillan

Tcherepnin encouraged Prokofiev’s Haydn and Mozart; the long ballet scores continue Tchaikovsky’s work of telling a whole evening’s story through dance. Mussorgsky supplied an operatic example for the quick exchanges and crowded public scenes. Richter introduced the Seventh and Ninth sonatas, and Rostropovich helped remake the cello concerto as the Sinfonia concertante. On stage, Frederick Ashton’s Copenhagen Romeo and Juliet of 1955 and Kenneth MacMillan’s London production of 1965 gave the same score new bodies.
