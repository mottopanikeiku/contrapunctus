SergeiProkofiev

Born 27 April (15 April Old Style) 1891; Prokofiev himself gave 23 April, Sontsovka, Ukraine, then Russian EmpireDied 5 March 1953, Moscow

A pianist who made the keyboard strike like percussion, and a theatre composer whose longest melodies keep their footing through abrupt changes of key.

Key ideas

  • Tonal side-slips. The “wrong note” often belongs to an entirely intelligible chord placed where the phrase has taught us to expect something else.
  • Motor rhythm. A recurring cell can stay fixed while the harmony above it changes violently.
  • Toccata pianism. The piano supplies attacks as well as sustained harmony.
“It seemed to me that if Haydn had lived in our day he would have retained his own style while accepting something of the new at the same time.”
— Autobiography, explaining the Classical Symphony of 1917

54.1Life

Prokofiev grew up on the Sontsovka estate in Ukraine, where his father managed the agricultural business and his mother played Beethoven and Chopin at the piano. He remembered hearing sonatas from another room after being put to bed. The keyboard was therefore familiar before the concert hall: his mother wrote down his first pieces, and a visit to Moscow opera performances in 1900 prompted the nine-year-old to compose The Giant for performance at home. Sergei Taneyev subsequently recommended professional instruction. Reinhold Glière spent the summers of 1902 and 1903 at Sontsovka, teaching composition and helping the boy turn his accumulating pieces into larger forms.

1904–14A pianist inside the conservatory

He entered the St Petersburg Conservatory at thirteen, bringing manuscripts of his compositions. Rimsky-Korsakov taught orchestration, Anatoly Lyadov harmony and counterpoint, Nikolai Tcherepnin conducting, and Anna Esipova piano. Tcherepnin’s attention to Haydn and Mozart mattered as much as the harmonic experiments Prokofiev pursued outside class. Nikolai Myaskovsky, an older fellow student, became a lifelong correspondent and a critic whose objections he took seriously. Their correspondence continued to test decisions about form and orchestration long after the Conservatory.

In the Toccata, Op. 11, repeated notes become an almost continuous percussion part; Suggestion diabolique turns changes of register into theatrical gestures. The First Piano Concerto, in D♭ major, compresses contrasting sections into a single movement whose opening proclamation returns as a structural marker. Prokofiev played it in the Conservatory’s 1914 final competition and won the Anton Rubinstein Prize for piano playing. The examiners judged the pianist with his published concerto before them.

The Second Concerto had already provoked hostile responses at its 1913 premiere in Pavlovsk. In its first-movement cadenza, the unaccompanied pianist takes over the movement’s development and accumulates weight until the orchestra returns. The original orchestral score was subsequently lost; Prokofiev reconstructed and substantially revised the concerto in 1923. The G minor concerto now performed therefore incorporates decisions made a decade after the premiere.

1914–17Diaghilev, then Haydn

In London in 1914 he met Sergei Diaghilev, whose Ballets Russes had made Stravinsky an international name. Diaghilev commissioned a ballet on a prehistoric subject, then rejected Ala and Lolli. Prokofiev salvaged its material as the four-movement Scythian Suite, first conducted by him in Petrograd in 1916. Its massed brass, percussion and repeating figures conjure an invented antiquity. Diaghilev’s replacement commission, Chout — The Buffoon — used a Russian folk tale. Revised for its eventual Paris production in 1921, it gave Prokofiev a stage on which rapid changes of instrumental colour could follow the mechanics of a grotesque plot.

Four linesIn his autobiography Prokofiev distinguished classical, modern, motoric and lyrical tendencies in his music. All four coexist; the lyricism of the First Violin Concerto predates his Soviet return.

The year 1917 produced the completed First Violin Concerto, the Classical Symphony and the last of the twenty Visions fugitives. The concerto opens with a violin melody marked sognando, dreamily, over quiet violas; the piano miniatures can reduce an argument to a few phrases. For the symphony he deliberately worked away from the piano, seeking orchestral ideas independent of his fingers. He explained its premise through an imagined contemporary Haydn: eighteenth-century forms and transparent textures could absorb twentieth-century harmonic turns and retain their identity.

The Classical Symphony retains four movements, paired woodwinds, two horns, two trumpets, timpani and strings. Its D major is repeatedly displaced by harmonies that are clear in themselves but unexpected in context. The Gavotte replaces the normal minuet, with conspicuously wide melodic leaps and changes of key that disturb its courtly tread. Prokofiev conducted the premiere on 21 April 1918. Within the music’s compact dimensions, the opening Allegro’s exposition, development and return emerge through abrupt changes of texture.

1918–23Eastward to America

He left Russia in May 1918 with official permission, travelling across Siberia to Vladivostok, then through Japan to the United States. Prokofiev retained contacts at home and expected foreign work to advance his career; Rachmaninoff had left for good as a political exile. Prokofiev gave recitals in Japan and reached San Francisco in August. American engagements initially marketed him as a startling pianist; obtaining performances of operas was harder. Chicago offered the decisive commission, although the death of the opera company’s director Cleofonte Campanini delayed the project. Meanwhile Prokofiev met the singer Lina Codina, who performed under the name Lina Llubera.

The Love for Three Oranges, completed in 1919, reached the Chicago stage on 30 December 1921, conducted by its composer and sung in French. Its source was Carlo Gozzi’s eighteenth-century theatrical fairy tale, mediated through the Russian theatrical world of Vsevolod Meyerhold. A prince must be cured of melancholy; rival theatrical factions interrupt the action; oranges contain princesses. The familiar march joins brief vocal exchanges, percussion and orchestral interjections in undermining operatic solemnity. Prokofiev uses the speed and discontinuity of spoken comedy, reserving lyricism for particular changes in the action.

Two weeks earlier he had introduced the Third Piano Concerto in Chicago with Frederick Stock. Completed that summer in Brittany from material accumulated over several years, it begins with an unaccompanied clarinet before the strings accelerate and the piano enters. The middle movement subjects a compact theme to five variations; the finale sets its opening, sharply articulated material against a much broader lyrical episode. The piano can hammer, spring between registers and sing within the same movement. His 1932 recording with Piero Coppola preserves the composer as soloist, an unusually direct document against which to compare later choices of tempo, articulation and freedom.

1923–36Paris and the gradual return

After a period at Ettal in Bavaria, Prokofiev married Lina in 1923 and established his family in Paris; their sons Sviatoslav and Oleg were born in 1924 and 1928. Diaghilev remained an important employer. Le Pas d’acier, staged in 1927, represented Soviet industrial life for a Western audience; The Prodigal Son, choreographed by George Balanchine in 1929, made space for much longer lyrical writing. Between them stands the failure to secure a production of The Fiery Angel, his opera after Valery Bryusov about religious obsession and erotic delusion. He transferred much of its musical material into the Third Symphony, giving it a form independent of the plot.

Paris also heard the densely contrapuntal Second Symphony in 1925, a two-movement experiment whose orchestral weight contrasts with the Classical. Prokofiev’s Soviet concert tour of 1927 reopened another professional world, and further visits and commissions gradually increased his commitments there. From 1933 he spent substantial periods in the USSR, before settling his family in Moscow in 1936. Tours abroad continued until 1938. His public advocacy of greater melodic simplicity accompanied this change; the long lines of The Prodigal Son had already anticipated it.

Film offered a different kind of commission from the opera house. For Lieutenant Kijé, released in 1934, he supplied music for a bureaucratic satire in which an officer created by a clerical error acquires an imaginary career. The concert suite, Op. 60, gives that non-existent man a birth, romance, wedding, sleigh ride and burial; the tenor saxophone helps separate its sentimental tune from the military instrumentation surrounding it. As with his ballet suites and later Alexander Nevsky, making a concert work meant choosing a new order and new proportions for music written to serve images.

1935–39The stage, the classroom, the screen

Which premiere?Brno, 30 December 1938, was the first staging of Romeo and Juliet. The Kirov production of 11 January 1940 established the familiar large-scale Soviet ballet. The Bolshoi mounted Lavrovsky’s production in 1946.

Romeo and Juliet was largely composed in 1935, but negotiations with the Kirov and Bolshoi theatres delayed production through postponements and revisions. The original scenario even allowed the lovers to survive. Prokofiev removed that ending, made concert suites from the score, and saw the first staging take place in Brno before Leonid Lavrovsky’s Kirov production with Galina Ulanova as Juliet in 1940. The music differentiates social behaviour with unusual precision: the heavy, repeated tread of the knights’ dance belongs to the Capulet household, while Juliet’s quick scales and changes of tempo give way to sustained phrases as her relationships develop.

Peter and the Wolf, written in 1936 for Natalia Sats’s children’s theatre, applies the same characterisation to a smaller orchestra. A listener learns to distinguish Peter’s strings, the bird’s flute, the duck’s oboe and the grandfather’s bassoon before hearing their themes combined and transformed by the story. Three horns make the wolf audible. The narrative teaches instrumental recognition and musical memory: when a voice returns inside another situation, the listener must connect its present sound with its earlier identity. The instrumental assignments help shape the form.

With Sergei Eisenstein’s Alexander Nevsky in 1938, Prokofiev worked on a film in which editing and composition could determine each other. Sometimes he wrote to existing footage; sometimes Eisenstein shot or cut images to music already prepared. The Teutonic invaders’ heavy sonorities and choral Latin oppose the Russians’ more direct melodic writing: the scoring participates in the film’s political argument. Prokofiev also experimented with microphone placement and recorded balance. For the seven-movement cantata of 1939 he reorganised and reorchestrated the material. The solo mezzo-soprano’s Field of the Dead changes the scale from mass combat to one person searching among the fallen.

1939–45War in several forms

Prokofiev began the Sixth, Seventh and Eighth piano sonatas, collectively known as the “War Sonatas”, together in 1939, before the German invasion of the USSR. The Sixth, completed in 1940, places its A major under pressure from chromatic clashes and brings first-movement material back in the finale. The Seventh, completed in 1942 and premiered by Richter in January 1943, moves from a restless opening to an E major Andante caloroso, then to the B♭ major Precipitato. Its recurring 7/8 pattern groups the quavers 2 + 3 + 2: a stable asymmetry supplies the propulsion.

In 1941 Prokofiev left Lina and began living with Mira Mendelson, a literary collaborator as well as his partner. Following the invasion in June, evacuation took them away from Moscow; his wartime places of work included Nalchik, Tbilisi and Alma-Ata. Mira helped shape the libretto of War and Peace, drawing on Tolstoy’s novel. Its first scene begins with private listening — Prince Andrei overhears Natasha at night — before the drama moves through courtship, humiliation and the invasion of 1812. Official demands for a more emphatic patriotic treatment enlarged the public scenes. Successive revisions changed its dimensions, and Prokofiev never heard the whole opera staged during his lifetime.

The Eighth Sonata, completed in 1944 and premiered that December by Emil Gilels, takes more time over its melodic statements than the Seventh; its opening Andante dolce keeps the violence of the later development in reserve. Cinderella, composed in 1940–44 and first staged at the Bolshoi in November 1945, also brings lyricism into contact with menace. Its waltzes, variations and character dances recover the architecture of nineteenth-century ballet, but the clock music turns regular pulse into a threat. The sisters’ awkward dances and Cinderella’s sustained lines remain distinct even when both use the same orchestra and recognisable dance forms.

The Fifth Symphony was composed in 1944 and first conducted by Prokofiev in Moscow on 13 January 1945. Its opening movement is an Andante rather than the expected fast Allegro; a flute and bassoon launch the theme before the orchestral argument expands. A driven scherzo and an Adagio precede a finale whose apparent momentum towards a conventional victorious ending is unsettled by a reduced, sharply articulated ensemble near the close. Piano, harp, percussion and solo strings momentarily expose the machinery inside the full orchestra.

1945–53Prizes, prohibition, illness

A fall and concussion in 1945 aggravated already serious health problems and curtailed Prokofiev’s performing life. He continued composing, including the Sixth Symphony in E♭ minor, premiered under Yevgeny Mravinsky in 1947. Across its three movements, the finale’s apparent release is interrupted by returning material from the first movement. Meanwhile official success remained possible. The Seventh Sonata had received a Stalin Prize in 1943, and further prizes followed. A composer honoured for particular scores could subsequently be condemned in a campaign against his methods.

FormalismThe Central Committee resolution of 10 February 1948, associated with Andrei Zhdanov, attacked Prokofiev, Shostakovich, Myaskovsky and others. “Formalism” was a political accusation about music’s supposed alienation from the people.

The 1948 campaign restricted performances and damaged his income while illness limited his ability to answer it through public appearances. In January he had married Mira, after Soviet authorities treated his foreign marriage to Lina as invalid. On 20 February Lina was arrested on fabricated espionage charges; sentenced to twenty years, she spent eight years in prisons and camps before release in 1956. Prokofiev himself was not arrested. Lina survived him by decades and later worked to preserve his legacy.

The final cello worksThe Sonata, Op. 119, is for cello and piano; the Sinfonia concertante, Op. 125, is for cello and orchestra. The latter extensively transforms the earlier Cello Concerto, Op. 58.

Collaboration with the young Mstislav Rostropovich produced the Cello Sonata in 1949 and the Sinfonia concertante in 1950–52. The Seventh Symphony, completed in 1952, answered a commission intended for young listeners with restrained orchestration and a finale that can end quietly; an alternative brisk coda also survives. Prokofiev died in Moscow on 5 March 1953, the same day as Stalin, whose state mourning largely displaced notice of the composer’s death. Lina was still imprisoned. The fully staged Fiery Angel, the complete War and Peace and several other scores would reach audiences only after their composer could no longer revise them.

54.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Tonal side-slips

The “wrong note” often belongs to an entirely intelligible chord placed where the phrase has taught us to expect something else. A major triad shifts by a semitone, a melody continues over a displaced bass, or a cadence reaches the correct root with an altered third. The effect depends on remembering the key.

The Classical Symphony and the short piano pieces offer exposed examples. In the Sixth Sonata, incompatible thirds and semitone friction become part of the movement’s thematic identity. Follow the bass as well as the tune when the surface seems to have slipped sideways.

𝄾Motor rhythm

A recurring cell can stay fixed while the harmony above it changes violently. The scherzo of the Second Piano Concerto keeps both hands moving in parallel octaves; the Seventh Sonata’s finale maintains a seven-quaver bar with a characteristic 2 + 3 + 2 grouping.

Regular pulse is equally useful for theatre. Marches establish collective behaviour; interruptions make individual actions audible. In Cinderella, a clock’s recurrence becomes a deadline. Listen for the distinction between the unchanging subdivision and the accents or chord changes laid across it.

𝆑Toccata pianism

The piano supplies attacks as well as sustained harmony. Repeated notes, interlocking hands, wide jumps and chordal blows make the physical distance between registers audible. The Toccata in D minor, Op. 11, is a concentrated study in this writing; the Second Concerto expands it into cadenzas whose length and harmonic development replace orchestral work.

This percussive writing demands careful balance. The outer fingers may have to project a melodic strand through repeated inner notes, and pedal has to preserve a harmonic field while keeping the next semitone collision clear. The contrast with a genuinely legato passage is part of the composition.

𝅘𝅥The lyric line

Prokofiev’s melodies often combine stepwise motion with one disproportionately large leap. A phrase can remain easy to follow even when its accompaniment enters a remote key. The First Violin Concerto begins sognando; the Third Piano Concerto’s finale interrupts its sharply articulated argument with an extended lyrical span; the Eighth Sonata allows a long melody to precede its faster music.

In Romeo and Juliet, the difference between a quick figure and a sustained phrase helps mark Juliet’s changing behaviour. The tune has dramatic work to do before it is extracted for the concert hall.

𝄞Instrumental character

The bassoon can be an elderly speaker, the flute a bird, three horns a wolf: Peter and the Wolf makes explicit a method used throughout the stage works. The tenor saxophone in Lieutenant Kijé and Romeo and Juliet adds a colour outside the ordinary nineteenth-century orchestral family.

The Fifth Symphony begins with flute and bassoon in octaves and reduces its finale to a small, exposed ensemble before the last orchestral blows. In the film Alexander Nevsky, recording and microphone placement join the instrumental choices; the concert cantata is a separate reorchestration.

𝄋Neoclassicism

The Classical Symphony preserves a small orchestra and recognisable movement types, then exaggerates a leap or displaces a harmonic arrival. It plays on the listener’s knowledge of eighteenth-century phrase structure. A gavotte needs its regular tread before the harmony can wrong-foot it.

The same respect for inherited construction runs through the sonatas: contrasting themes, development and return remain audible beneath dissonance. Cinderella extends the principle to ballet through waltzes, variations and pas de deux. Sonata form and theatre supply working structures.

54.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1911–12
    Piano Concerto No. 1 in D♭ majorOp. 10Piano and orchestra
    A single continuous movement with slow and scherzando sections; the opening orchestral proclamation returns to hold the design together. Prokofiev played it to win the Conservatory’s Anton Rubinstein piano prize in 1914.
  2. 1912
    Toccata in D minorOp. 11Piano
    Repeated notes shared between the hands become a continuous stream of semiquavers. Chromatic movement and changes of register transform the pattern without granting the pianist a conventional lyrical respite.
  3. 1912–13; revised 1923
    Piano Concerto No. 2 in G minorOp. 16Piano and orchestra
    Four movements, including a first-movement cadenza of exceptional structural weight and a scherzo in almost continuous parallel octaves. The performed score is the substantial 1923 reconstruction after the original orchestral manuscript was lost.
  4. 1914–15
    Scythian SuiteOp. 20Large orchestra, including expanded brass, percussion, piano and two harps
    Four movements salvaged from the rejected ballet Ala and Lolli. Violent repeating figures give way to the thinner textures of “Night”; the last movement builds towards sunrise.
  5. 1915–17
    Visions fugitivesOp. 22Piano
    Twenty miniatures, sometimes only a page long. Character comes from a register, an interval or an articulation; the set places quiet diatonic writing beside densely chromatic fragments.
  6. 1915–17
    Violin Concerto No. 1 in D majorOp. 19Violin and orchestra
    A fast central scherzo between two more spacious movements. The opening violin line, marked sognando, returns near the end. Completed in 1917, but first performed in Paris in 1923 by Marcel Darrieux under Koussevitzky.
  7. 1916–17
    Symphony No. 1 in D major “Classical”Op. 25Orchestra with paired woodwinds, two horns, two trumpets, timpani and strings
    Four movements in roughly fifteen minutes, with a gavotte instead of a minuet. Haydn’s formal proportions coexist with wide melodic leaps and sudden tonal displacements; premiered under Prokofiev in Petrograd in April 1918.
  8. 1919
    The Love for Three OrangesOp. 33Opera in a prologue and four acts, after Carlo Gozzi
    Theatrical factions argue over the kind of opera the audience will receive. Rapid declamation, orchestral interjections and the celebrated march propel the fairy tale. Premiered in French in Chicago in December 1921.
  9. 1917–21
    Piano Concerto No. 3 in C majorOp. 26Piano and orchestra
    Clarinet song, fast piano passagework and abrupt changes of harmony share the opening movement. A theme and five variations forms the centre; the finale’s broad lyrical episode interrupts its more angular outer material.
  10. 1919–27
    The Fiery AngelOp. 37Opera in five acts, after Valery Bryusov
    Renata’s visions and obsession govern an orchestral fabric built from recurrent motifs. Not staged during Prokofiev’s lifetime; much of its material entered Symphony No. 3, Op. 44, composed in 1928.
  11. 1933–34
    Lieutenant Kijé SuiteOp. 60Orchestra, with optional baritone in the Romance and Troika
    Five concert movements derived from the film about an imaginary officer. Tenor saxophone, military calls and sleigh bells give his invented biography distinct instrumental settings.
  12. 1935–36; revised 1939–40
    Romeo and JulietOp. 64Full-length ballet after Shakespeare; large orchestra with tenor saxophone, cornet, piano and celesta
    The complete dramatic context matters: the knights’ tread, Mercutio’s quick movements and Juliet’s changing phrases interact before returning in altered circumstances. First staged in Brno in 1938; Lavrovsky’s Kirov production followed in 1940.
  13. 1936
    Peter and the WolfOp. 67Narrator and orchestra
    A musical tale written for Natalia Sats’s children’s theatre. Each character has an instrumental identity; their combinations turn an introduction to timbre into a lesson in remembering and recognising themes.
  14. 1938–39
    Alexander NevskyOp. 78Cantata for mezzo-soprano, mixed chorus and orchestra
    Seven movements reworked from Eisenstein’s 1938 film. “The Battle on the Ice” accumulates contrasting layers; “The Field of the Dead” replaces massed conflict with a solo voice. The film score itself has no opus number.
  15. 1939–44
    Piano Sonatas Nos. 6–8, the “War Sonatas”Opp. 82–84Piano
    No. 6 in A major (1940): four movements, with the opening material returning in the finale. No. 7 in B♭ major (1942): a 7/8 Precipitato after the E major slow movement. No. 8 in B♭ major (1944): an extended opening Andante dolce and a fast finale.
  16. 1940–44
    CinderellaOp. 87Ballet in three acts; scenario by Nikolai Volkov after Charles Perrault
    Traditional ballet numbers — waltzes, variations, character dances — support unusually distinct portraits of the sisters and Cinderella. The clock turns regular rhythm into the mechanism that ends the ball. Premiered at the Bolshoi in November 1945.
  17. 1941–52
    War and PeaceOp. 91Opera in thirteen scenes with choral epigraph; libretto by Prokofiev and Mira Mendelson after Tolstoy
    Private scenes of Natasha, Andrei and Pierre lead into the public catastrophe of 1812. Extensive revisions enlarged the patriotic and military episodes. The composer never heard the entire opera staged.
  18. 1944
    Symphony No. 5 in B♭ majorOp. 100Large orchestra with piano, harp and percussion
    An Andante in sonata form, a motoric scherzo, an Adagio and a fast finale. Near the end the orchestration abruptly shrinks to an exposed ensemble before the final tutti. Prokofiev conducted the premiere in January 1945.

54.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Dicksee and Prokofiev work from the same Shakespeare play. The painting concentrates the lovers’ encounter into an embrace on the balcony; the ballet gives the encounter a sequence of approaches, withdrawals and sustained phrases. Compare the image with the end of Act I. The contrast between the private meeting and the Capulets’ public ceremonial behaviour, heard in the knights’ dance usually detached from the score, is already built into Shakespeare’s arrangement of scenes.

One of Vereshchagin’s Napoleon I in Russia paintings, dealing with the same 1812 campaign that occupies the war scenes of Prokofiev’s opera. The advancing soldiers fill the foreground, bringing the viewer into the movement of bodies. The link is the campaign; use of the canvas as a source is undocumented. Set it beside the opera’s Borodino scene and Tolstoy’s insistence that no general can encompass a battle’s simultaneous actions.

Doré’s illustration belongs to an edition of Perrault’s tales; Nikolai Volkov also used Perrault for the scenario of Prokofiev’s Cinderella. The shared source matters because the fairy’s transformation, the ball and the time limit require different kinds of stage time. Prokofiev gives each a musical mechanism: changes of orchestral colour for transformation, extended dances for the ball, and repeated clock figures for the limit.

Roerich’s fenced sanctuary belongs to his imagined ancient Rus. He later devised the scenario and designs for Stravinsky’s The Rite of Spring; Diaghilev’s subsequent prehistoric commission to Prokofiev produced Ala and Lolli and, after its rejection, the Scythian Suite. The link is the Ballets Russes interest in a reconstructed pagan past. Roerich’s repeated posts and carved figures offer a visual counterpart to the suite’s blocks of repeated rhythm.

Ах, какая прелесть! Ты поди сюда. Душенька, голубушка, поди сюда. Ну, видишь? Так бы вот села на корточки, вот так, подхватила бы себя под коленки — туже, как можно туже, натужиться надо,— и полетела бы. Вот так!
Our translation“Sonya! Sonya!” the first voice sounded again. “How can you sleep! Just look how lovely it is! Oh, how lovely! Do wake up, Sonya,” she said, almost in tears. “There has never, never been such a lovely night.” Sonya answered something reluctantly. “No, just look at that moon! Oh, how lovely! Come here. Darling, my dear, come here. Now, do you see? I would squat down like this, tuck my hands under my knees — tighter, as tight as possible, you have to make an effort — and fly away. Like this!”

Prince Andrei hears this conversation without the women knowing he is below their window. Prokofiev and Mira Mendelson made the Otradnoye episode the opera’s opening scene: Natasha’s delight reaches a listener within the drama before it reaches us as an operatic declaration. Haydn supplied proportions and formal expectations; Beethoven supplied a continuing model of thematic development; Mussorgsky offered precedents for speech-shaped operatic declamation. Tchaikovsky’s full-length ballets remain the relevant comparison for Romeo and Juliet and Cinderella, even where Prokofiev changes the harmonic language. Prokofiev and Shostakovich learnt from each other, testing motor rhythm, caricature and inherited forms under changing Soviet demands. Richter and Rostropovich transmitted the music through direct collaboration, while Ashton’s 1955 and MacMillan’s 1965 Romeo and Juliet productions made the score the basis of distinct choreographic readings.

Questions to take away

  1. How does the accompaniment change the character of a melody?

    Written in 1912, the Second Sonata puts long melodies, a short scherzo and a tarantella finale into four movements. Listen for the accompaniment: changing the notes beneath a melody often changes its character more drastically than changing the melody itself.

    Piano Sonata No. 2, Op. 14 · Complete · Allegro ma non troppo — Scherzo — Andante — Vivace

  2. How can repeated notes keep changing their weight and direction?

    Two hands share a stream of repeated notes; chromatic lines and displaced accents keep changing what those notes mean. Martha Argerich plays the Toccata Prokofiev wrote while he was still a St Petersburg Conservatory student.

    Toccata in D minor, Op. 11
