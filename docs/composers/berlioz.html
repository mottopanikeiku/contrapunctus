Life

When the young Hector Berlioz read aloud the death of Dido, his voice failed him. His father closed the book and said that he was tired, so the boy did not have to explain his tears. Berlioz recorded the kindness in his Mémoires. Louis Berlioz was a doctor in La Côte-Saint-André, between Lyon and Grenoble, and taught his son Latin, history and geography. He expected Hector to follow him into medicine. The boy learnt the flute and guitar, studied maps of distant islands and was deeply moved by the story of Virgil’s abandoned queen.

In 1821 he went to Paris to study medicine. His first visit to the dissecting room at the Pitié hospital, he later wrote, ended with a flight through a window. On the second visit he managed perfectly well. Music was the more durable obstacle. He heard Salieri’s Les Danaïdes at the Opéra and began singing its airs over the cadavers. At the Conservatoire library he copied and memorised Gluck’s scores. His father argued, threatened and withheld support. Hector took composition lessons with Jean-François Le Sueur.

He entered the Conservatoire in 1826, studying with Le Sueur and Anton Reicha. A Messe solennelle and the opera Les Francs-juges, which never reached the stage, gave him experience with large forces. The Prix de Rome took longer. After failing the preliminary examination in 1826, he entered a cantata every year from 1827 and won in 1830 with the fourth. He never became a pianist. In the Mémoires, writing in London in the spring of 1848, he credited that deficiency with freeing him from “the tyranny of finger habits”.

1827–30A woman at the Odéon

The memoiristBerlioz began the Mémoires in London in 1848, printed them privately in 1865 and left their public release until after his death. They appeared in 1870. His recollected conversations are attributed as such here.

On 11 September 1827 he saw an English company perform Hamlet at the Odéon. Harriet Smithson played Ophelia. “Shakespeare struck me like a thunderbolt,” he wrote, recalling the evening. He knew no English and had read Shakespeare only in Letourneur’s French translation. A few days later he returned to see Smithson as Juliet. In his mind the actress and the playwright became bound up together. He sent Smithson extravagant letters, though she did not know him. She was not in the hall for the first performance of the symphony he wrote with her in mind.

In 1828 Berlioz heard François-Antoine Habeneck’s Conservatoire orchestra play Beethoven. His Symphonie fantastique, first performed on 5 December 1830, follows an artist whose beloved keeps returning to his thoughts. The movements depict a ball, a scene in the country and an opium dream in which he has killed her and is led to execution. After the scaffold comes his own funeral, attended by witches.

The idée fixeThe beloved is represented by a long melody that returns in all five movements. In the witches’ sabbath an E♭ clarinet plays it as a shrill dance.

Two harps play in the ballroom scene. A cor anglais calls to an oboe placed offstage; at the end of the country scene, the oboe does not reply. Four timpanists play distant thunder. Just before the execution, a solo clarinet begins the beloved’s melody and is cut off by the orchestra. In the finale the funeral chant Dies irae is combined with the witches’ round, and the strings play with the wood of their bows. A printed programme set out the story for the audience.

The Rome prize sent him to the Villa Medici. He walked in the Italian countryside, heard shepherds’ pipes and wrote Le Roi Lear in Nice. Back in Paris, Smithson heard the Fantastique and its sequel Lélio in December 1832. They met, married in October 1833 and had a son, Louis, the following year. Money troubles and professional disappointment strained the marriage; her health declined, and they separated in the 1840s. The singer Marie Recio became his travelling companion and, after Smithson’s death in 1854, his second wife.

1834–40Paganini pays

Paganini owned a Stradivarius viola and wanted a work to play on it. Berlioz showed him the first movement of Harold en Italie. According to the Mémoires, Paganini objected that he was silent for too long: “I have to play all the time.” Berlioz continued the work without him. The solo viola, cast as a traveller, appears in mountain scenes and in a pilgrims’ procession, and has long stretches in which it does not play. When Paganini heard the symphony in December 1838, he sent Berlioz 20,000 francs.

The money freed Berlioz from reviewing for a time. “I had money; Paganini had given it to me to make music, and I made it,” he wrote in his account of composing Roméo et Juliette in 1839. The orchestra alone plays the lovers’ great scene. Singers tell the story, and at the end Friar Laurence reconciles the feuding families. Berlioz dedicated the score to Paganini, who died without hearing it.

Public commissions required music for different spaces. The Grande Messe des morts was performed at the Invalides in December 1837, with four extra brass groups stationed apart from the orchestra. They play in the Tuba mirum. Later the unaccompanied choir sings Quaerens me, and the Sanctus has a part for a single tenor. The Grande Symphonie funèbre et triomphale of 1840 was performed in the streets by a wind band, at the ceremony that reburied the dead of the July Revolution.

The working weekWriting about other people’s music

Practical knowledgeThe orchestration treatise of 1844 explained what instruments can do in particular registers and combinations. In 1855 Berlioz added a chapter on conducting, and Richard Strauss later expanded the whole book.

At the Journal des débats, Berlioz spent working days on other people’s operas. In his prose he explained technical problems, staged absurd conversations and dismantled theatrical institutions. A post at the Conservatoire library supplied another income. Conducting tours took him to Germany, Russia and Britain, where he could rehearse his own music. Parisian theatres were harder to persuade. Benvenuto Cellini failed at the Opéra in 1838; Liszt revived it in Weimar in 1852.

La Damnation de Faust, written in 1845–46, lost money at its Paris concert performances. Its scenes move from a Hungarian plain to a German tavern, Marguerite’s room, the abyss and heaven. Berlioz drew on his youthful Huit scènes de Faust, composed after he read Gérard de Nerval’s translation of Goethe in 1828. In Marguerite’s D’amour l’ardente flamme, set in her room, a cor anglais plays before she sings.

1854–69Dido returns

L’Enfance du Christ succeeded in Paris in 1854. In it the Ishmaelites take in the exhausted refugee family, and two flutes and a harp play for them. Berlioz then returned to the Virgil he had read with his father. In Les Troyens, composed principally in 1856–58, Cassandra foresees the destruction of Troy; in Carthage, Dido receives the survivors, loves Aeneas and loses him to his duty. Berlioz wrote the libretto himself.

Only the Carthage portion reached the stage during his lifetime, heavily cut, at the Théâtre-Lyrique in November 1863. Both halves were first staged together at Karlsruhe in December 1890, over two evenings. His last opera, Béatrice et Bénédict, after Shakespeare’s quarrelling lovers, had already been given at Baden-Baden in 1862, with spoken dialogue and a nocturnal duet for Héro and Ursule. Marie died that year. Louis died in 1867. Despite worsening illness, Berlioz accepted a final Russian tour in the winter of 1867–68. He died in Paris on 8 March 1869.

The Sound

𝄞Instrument as dramatic character

The viola in Harold en Italie has its own recurring melody. Other events take place around it: pilgrims pass, a serenade is heard, brigands feast. The part contains silences as well as notes, which disappointed Paganini when he first saw it. In the funeral symphony, a solo trombone plays the oration that a human speaker might have given at the graveside.

𝅘𝅥Unsettled melody

The Fantastique’s idée fixe begins by holding G across a barline. It rises to C, falls back and rises higher to E. Long notes are tied over into the next bar, and the phrase reaches a cadence only after some length. In the finale the E♭ clarinet plays the melody in short, clipped notes as a dance, at the point where the beloved arrives at the witches’ sabbath.

𝄐Across the room

The oboe answering the Fantastique’s cor anglais plays offstage, and later it stops answering. In the Requiem, four extra brass groups play the summons to judgement from separate places. The Te Deum places organ and orchestra apart.

♮A familiar chord in an unusual context

The Fantastique moves from the C minor of its introduction into C major when the long allegro begins. The pastoral movement is in F major and opens with a cor anglais and a distant oboe. The beloved’s melody returns during the movement. At the close there are bare calls and rumbling timpani.

𝄾Wood against strings

The waltz, scaffold march and witches’ dance in the Fantastique each move at a markedly different gait. In the finale, the slow Dies irae is combined with the fast round dance. The violinists and violists then turn their bows over and strike the strings with the wood, producing a dry, rattling sound.

𝄋Narrator

In Roméo et Juliette, a small chorus supplies the outline of the play and solo voices comment on it. The orchestra takes the love scene and Queen Mab’s flight. Voices return for the reconciliation, when Friar Laurence must persuade the families to stop fighting.

Works

A selection from the composer’s works.

  1. 1830
    Symphonie fantastiqueOp. 14Orchestra with harps, bells and expanded percussion
    The beloved’s melody returns in all five movements, from its first statement on flute and violins to the E♭ clarinet’s parody of it at the witches’ sabbath. Four timpanists play the thunder at the end of the country scene.
    Recordings in the Hall
  2. 1831
    Le Roi LearOp. 4Orchestra
    Berlioz wrote the overture in Nice during his Italian stay. It opens with a slow introduction, which is followed by the fast main section.
  3. 1834
    Harold en ItalieOp. 16Solo viola and orchestra
    Paganini wanted more notes for himself and declined the solo part. The solo viola plays through much of this four-movement symphony of Italian scenes.
  4. 1834–38
    Benvenuto CelliniOp. 23Opera
    A sculptor has to cast his statue while love, rivals and the carnival get in the way. The opera failed in Paris in 1838; Liszt revived it at Weimar in 1852.
  5. 1837
    Grande Messe des mortsOp. 5Tenor, chorus, large orchestra and four additional brass groups
    The four brass groups answer the choir’s vision of judgement. The later Quaerens me is sung unaccompanied.
  6. 1839
    Roméo et JulietteOp. 17Solo voices, choruses and orchestra
    Paganini’s gift of 20,000 francs helped pay for the time to compose it. The love scene and Queen Mab scherzo are orchestral; the final reconciliation needs Friar Laurence and the choruses.
  7. 1840
    Grande Symphonie funèbre et triomphaleOp. 15Wind band; later optional strings and chorus
    Written for the tenth anniversary of the July Revolution and a procession through Paris. A solo trombone plays the central funeral oration.
  8. 1841; orchestrated by 1856
    Les Nuits d’étéOp. 7Voice and piano; later voice and orchestra
    Six poems by Théophile Gautier, on a spring outing, ghosts, bereavement and an invitation to sail away. In Le Spectre de la rose, the ghost of a rose visits the young woman who wore it to a ball.
  9. 1845–46
    La Damnation de FaustOp. 24Soloists, chorus and orchestra
    The work includes the Hungarian March and Marguerite’s romance D’amour l’ardente flamme. The first Paris concert performances lost Berlioz money.
  10. 1849
    Te DeumOp. 22Tenor, multiple choirs, orchestra and organ
    First performed at Saint-Eustache in 1855. The organ is placed apart from the orchestra, and the two answer each other across the church.
  11. 1850–54
    L’Enfance du ChristOp. 25Soloists, chorus and orchestra
    Herod’s dream sends the Holy Family into exile; an Ishmaelite household finally offers shelter at Sais. The children’s welcome includes a trio for two flutes and harp.
  12. 1856–58
    Les TroyensOpera in five acts
    Berlioz wrote his own libretto from Virgil, with two acts set at Troy and three at Carthage. In his lifetime only the Carthage portion was staged, with substantial cuts.
  13. 1860–62
    Béatrice et BénédictComic opera in two acts
    Shakespeare’s sparring lovers in an opera with spoken dialogue. Héro and Ursule sing a long, quiet nocturnal duet.

Sister Arts

Marguerite sits with downcast eyes and her hands in her lap; the spinning wheel stands beside her. Delacroix’s seventeen lithographs accompanied Stapfer’s translation of Goethe in 1828, the year Berlioz read Nerval’s version and began his Huit scènes de Faust. In the later Damnation, Marguerite is again left alone in her room, listening and waiting while Faust travels.

William Shakespeare

Romeo and Juliet · Act III, scene 5

First published 1597
EnglishWilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day. It was the nightingale, and not the lark, That pierced the fearful hollow of thine ear; Nightly she sings on yon pomegranate-tree. Believe me, love, it was the nightingale.

Juliet hears a bird and insists it is the nightingale, because if it is the lark, Romeo must leave. Berlioz saw Smithson play Juliet at the Odéon in September 1827. His orchestral love scene is also set at night, with interrupted speech and a separation approaching. It does not set these lines.

Virgil

Aeneid · IV, 651–654

First century BCE
LatinDulces exuviae, dum fata deusque sinebat, accipite hanc animam meque his exsolvite curis. vixi et quem dederat cursum fortuna peregi, et nunc magna mei sub terras ibit imago.
Our translationDear relics, while fate and the god allowed it, receive this life and release me from these cares. I have lived, and completed the course fortune gave me; and now a great image of me will pass beneath the earth.

Dido addresses the things Aeneas has left in her room. As a boy, Berlioz broke down while reading her death to his father; the doctor tactfully closed the book. In the final act of Les Troyens, her farewell to her city and memories comes before the Carthaginians’ public curse on Aeneas’s descendants.

Lineage

Drew fromGluckLudwig van BeethovenShakespeareVirgil
Berlioz
Passed toFranz LisztRichard WagnerRichard StraussGustav MahlerNikolai Rimsky-Korsakov

Gluck taught him how to use an orchestra in the theatre; Beethoven opened the symphony to a new scale of ambition. Liszt transcribed the Fantastique for piano and revived Benvenuto Cellini. Strauss expanded the orchestration treatise. A precedent for the distant instruments and intrusive marches in Mahler’s symphonies can be heard in Berlioz’s separated brass and returning idée fixe.
