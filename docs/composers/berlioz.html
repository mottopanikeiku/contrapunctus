Born 11 December 1803, La Côte-Saint-André, FranceDied 8 March 1869, Paris

Key ideas

  • Instrumental identity. The cor anglais in the Fantastique, viola in Harold and tenor trombone in the funeral symphony are characters with distinct registers and physical properties.
  • Melodic span. The idée fixe extends far beyond a balanced four-bar tune.
  • Space. Offstage oboe, separated brass choirs and an organ opposing an orchestra make distance measurable.

21.1Life

Berlioz grew up in La Côte-Saint-André, between Lyon and Grenoble, in the household of a physician who taught him Latin and expected him to study medicine. He learned flute and guitar, but never acquired the pianist’s routine of composing at a keyboard. Virgil’s account of Dido’s death affected him as a schoolboy; forty years later he would set it in Les Troyens. Sent to Paris in 1821, he attended anatomy lectures while spending his evenings at the Opéra. The theatre’s performances of Gluck offered a different education: declamation, orchestral gesture and the pacing of a dramatic scene.

He abandoned medicine, studied with Jean-François Le Sueur and entered the Conservatoire in 1826. His first large works included a Messe solennelle and the unfinished opera Les Francs-juges, whose overture survived independently. The Prix de Rome required a prescribed cantata; Berlioz finally won it in 1830, on his fourth attempt. The prize sent him to the Villa Medici, but the decisive musical experiences had already happened in Paris. Conservatoire performances under François-Antoine Habeneck introduced him to Beethoven’s symphonies, while an English theatrical company introduced him to Shakespeare.

1827–30A beloved made audible

In September 1827 he saw Harriet Smithson as Ophelia in Hamlet, and then as Juliet. His limited English did not prevent an obsession with either the actress or the plays. Smithson initially knew little of him. The Symphonie fantastique, first performed on 5 December 1830, converted that asymmetry into a programme: an artist imagines his beloved, takes opium and dreams of his execution and a witches’ sabbath. The programme was a public document designed to explain how one melody could inhabit five otherwise sharply differentiated movements.

Idée fixeThe recurring melody represents the beloved, but its orchestration changes its identity. In the finale an E♭ clarinet turns its long-breathed line into a dance with snapped accents and ornamental squeals.

The symphony begins with a slow introduction and a C major sonata movement, then passes through a waltz, a pastoral scene, a march and a finale incorporating the Dies irae. Harps distinguish the ballroom; a cor anglais exchanges calls with an offstage oboe in the countryside. At the pastoral movement’s end the oboe no longer answers, and four timpanists produce distant thunder. In the march, a solo clarinet recalls the beloved immediately before the orchestral execution. Instrument, register and physical distance carry information that a piano reduction cannot preserve.

Italy supplied another set of musical memories. Berlioz disliked aspects of Roman musical life, but walked in the countryside and heard the pipers whose drones and rhythms entered his imagined Italy. Returning to Paris, he met Smithson, who heard his music in 1832; they married in 1833. Their son Louis was born the following year. Differences of language, professional disappointments, financial strain and Smithson’s declining health made the marriage increasingly unhappy. They separated in the 1840s. Berlioz later married the singer Marie Recio, his companion on many conducting tours, after Smithson’s death in 1854.

Public musicSoloist, crowd and city

Paganini asked for a work involving his Stradivarius viola, but rejected the first movement of Harold en Italie because the soloist had too little to play. Berlioz continued with a symphony in which the viola represents a traveller who observes scenes without controlling them. After hearing the work in 1838, Paganini gave him 20,000 francs. The gift helped support the composition of Roméo et Juliette, dedicated to the violinist. Here voices explain and comment, but the lovers’ great scene is entrusted to the orchestra. Shakespeare’s dialogue becomes the alternation and eventual interweaving of instrumental phrases.

The state commission for the Grande Messe des morts in 1837 allowed a different arrangement of forces: four additional brass groups placed apart from the main orchestra, massed voices and a formidable percussion section. Yet the unaccompanied Quaerens me and the tenor solo of the Sanctus depend on restraint. The Grande Symphonie funèbre et triomphale, commissioned for the July Revolution’s tenth anniversary in 1840, was conceived for a marching wind band. Berlioz’s large forces answered specific spaces and ceremonies.

The practical orchestratorThe Grand traité d’instrumentation et d’orchestration modernes appeared in 1844. Its examples explain instruments’ compass, register, balance and combinations. Richard Strauss later expanded it.

The working criticSuccess abroad, obstacles at home

Berlioz earned much of his living from journalism, especially for the Journal des débats. Reviewing other people’s operas consumed time he wanted for composition, but sharpened a prose style capable of technical explanation, comic dialogue and institutional satire. His Mémoires organise experience as deliberately as his programmes do; their conversations and scenes require comparison with letters and contemporary reports. Conducting tours in Germany, Russia and Britain gave him access to orchestras and audiences unavailable in Paris. They also taught him how much rehearsal a new orchestral language required.

Opera remained the hardest institution to enter. Benvenuto Cellini failed at the Paris Opéra in 1838; Liszt later helped revive it in Weimar. La Damnation de Faust, composed in 1845–46, was presented in concert and lost money. Calling it a ‘dramatic legend’ allowed Berlioz to move from Hungarian countryside to German tavern, bedroom, abyss and heaven without accommodating stage machinery. He incorporated and revised music from his earlier Huit scènes de Faust. The orchestra connects these discontinuous places through recurring figures, while Marguerite’s ballad and romance impose an individual voice on the spectacle.

The last theatreTroy and Carthage

L’Enfance du Christ, completed in 1854, brought an unusual Parisian success with quieter resources: the shepherds’ farewell, the Holy Family’s flight and the hospitality of the Ishmaelites. Les Troyens, written principally in 1856–58, returned to the epic scale of Virgil. Its first two acts concern Cassandra and the fall of Troy; the remaining three centre on Dido in Carthage. The two heroines know different kinds of catastrophe, and Berlioz distinguishes their public duties from their private speech. The ballet music, ceremonial processions and nocturnal love duet belong to a single political and musical architecture.

Only the Carthage portion reached the stage in his lifetime, at the Théâtre-Lyrique in 1863, with cuts. The complete opera had to wait until 1890. His last opera, Béatrice et Bénédict, based on Much Ado About Nothing, had been performed at Baden-Baden in 1862; its nocturnal duet pairs two women’s voices with a restrained orchestra. Marie died in 1862 and Louis in 1867. Berlioz made a final Russian tour that winter, despite severe illness, and died in Paris in March 1869. His scores left later composers exact instructions for sounds that their institutions were still learning to produce.

21.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄞Instrumental identity

The cor anglais in the Fantastique, viola in Harold and tenor trombone in the funeral symphony are characters with distinct registers and physical properties. A melody’s transfer to another instrument can alter the story even when its pitches remain recognisable.

𝅘𝅥Melodic span

The idée fixe extends far beyond a balanced four-bar tune. Sustained notes, irregular phrase lengths and renewed upward reaches delay its cadence. Berlioz’s melodic construction often follows a speaker’s accumulating clauses.

𝄐Space

Offstage oboe, separated brass choirs and an organ opposing an orchestra make distance measurable. In the Requiem’s Tuba mirum, successive entries change the listener’s sense of the room before they increase its volume.

♮Harmony

Diatonic melody can sit beside abruptly displaced chords or a bass that changes the meaning of a sustained note. The opening of the Fantastique places C minor before C major; the pastoral movement’s F major is undermined by the recurring beloved and by a closing that refuses a consoling reply.

𝄾Rhythm & articulation

March, waltz, fugue and recitative remain audibly different kinds of motion. In the sabbath, the Dies irae and the witches’ round eventually sound together; the strings’ col legno strokes replace a sustained singing tone with the dry impact of bow wood.

𝄋Dramatic form

He distributes narrative between printed programme, voice and orchestra. Roméo et Juliette selects from the play, assigning explanation to singers, intimacy to instruments and reconciliation to the final vocal scene. The omissions determine the form.

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

21.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Delacroix’s seventeen Faust lithographs accompanied Albert Stapfer’s French translation in 1828. Berlioz read Gérard de Nerval’s translation that year and composed his Huit scènes de Faust in 1828–29. The two artists approached Goethe independently: Marguerite’s enclosed room and repetitive labour oppose Faust’s restless movement. In La Damnation, her solitary songs similarly arrest the succession of public scenes.

William Shakespeare

Romeo and Juliet · Act III, scene 5

First published 1597
EnglishWilt thou be gone? It is not yet near day. It was the nightingale, and not the lark, That pierced the fearful hollow of thine ear; Nightly she sings on yon pomegranate-tree. Believe me, love, it was the nightingale.

Juliet tries to misidentify the sound that requires Romeo to leave. Berlioz saw Harriet Smithson as Juliet in 1827. His 1839 dramatic symphony transfers the lovers’ dialogue to the orchestra, asking instruments to distinguish appeal, interruption and consent without words. The play’s dependence on heard signals makes that transfer unusually exact.

Virgil

Aeneid · IV, 651–654

First century BCE
LatinDulces exuviae, dum fata deusque sinebat, accipite hanc animam meque his exsolvite curis. vixi et quem dederat cursum fortuna peregi, et nunc magna mei sub terras ibit imago.
Our translationDear relics, while fate and the god allowed it, receive this life and release me from these cares. I have lived, and completed the course fortune gave me; and now a great image of me will pass beneath the earth.

Dido addresses the objects Aeneas has left behind. Berlioz read this scene as a child and made her farewell the centre of the final act of Les Troyens. In his own French adaptation, private leave-taking expands into the Carthaginians’ curse and a vision of Rome.

Liszt arranged the Fantastique for piano and defended Berlioz’s stage works. Strauss revised the orchestration treatise; Mahler inherited the problem of making marches, remembered melodies and distant instruments coexist within a symphony.

Questions to take away

  1. How can a march turn a melody into an interruption?

    Berlioz places an execution march inside a symphony, with a brief recollection of the beloved’s melody near its end. What happens to the regular tread when that recollection interrupts it?

    Symphonie fantastique, Op. 14 · IV. Marche au supplice — Allegretto non troppo

  2. How does one melody become an obsession rather than a refrain?

    The beloved is a melody before she is a character: Berlioz’s idée fixe will inhabit a ballroom, disturb a pastoral scene, interrupt an execution march and return grotesquely at the witches’ sabbath. Here, a long, unsettled introduction delays its first full appearance. Listen for the difference between recognising a tune and finding a place where it can rest.

    Symphonie fantastique, Op. 14 · I. Rêveries — Passions
