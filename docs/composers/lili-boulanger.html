55.1Life

Lili Boulanger grew up in a Paris household where composition, singing and teaching were family work. Her father Ernest had won the Prix de Rome in 1835 and taught singing at the Conservatoire; her mother Raïssa had been his pupil. Nadia, born in 1887, was already receiving serious musical training when Lili was small. Lili learned piano and several other instruments and accompanied her sister into Conservatoire classes. Recurrent illness interrupted her education from childhood. The resulting timetable was irregular, but the musical environment was exceptionally well supplied with teachers, performers and scores.

Nadia was an early mentor and practical support; Lili’s formal composition teacher at the Conservatoire was Paul Vidal. Their training included fugue, harmony and the cantata, extending well beyond the short piano pieces through which women composers were often permitted a public identity. In 1912 illness forced Lili to withdraw from the final stage of the Prix de Rome. She returned in 1913 and won the Premier Grand Prix with Faust et Hélène, becoming the first woman to receive that composition prize. She was nineteen. Nadia had won a second prize in 1908.

The prize cantataTechnique under public examination

Faust et Hélène sets the prescribed competition text by Eugène Adenis, after Goethe. Faust, Hélène and Méphistophélès require three solo voices and an orchestra in sustained dramatic scenes. Boulanger’s score uses the chromatic resources of French late Romanticism: a voice can be held above a changing bass, and orchestral figures carry material between vocal statements. Her separate operatic project was Maeterlinck’s La Princesse Maleine, on which she continued working without leaving a performable complete score.

A prize with a curriculumThe Prix de Rome tested composition through specified exercises and a prescribed cantata text. Winning it demonstrated command of an institutional technique.

The prize brought residence at the Villa Medici and a publishing relationship with Ricordi. She went to Rome in 1914, but war and ill health disrupted her periods there. The thirteen songs of Clairières dans le ciel, composed in 1913–14, set poems by Francis Jammes selected from Tristesses. Their speaker remembers a young woman through particular gestures and objects. Recurrent musical ideas connect the separate poems, so a later song can reactivate the memory of an earlier one. The piano carries that continuity across the thirteen lyrics.

The cycle’s long final song, Demain fera un an, recalls earlier material and makes recollection itself the formal subject. Shorter songs can turn on a small interval or a repeated accompaniment. Her technique shares something with Fauré’s song cycles and Debussy’s treatment of register and resonance, but she permits heavier harmonic accumulations and more explicit climaxes. The difference is particularly audible when the same concentrated use of repeated material moves from a piano accompaniment into the large choral works.

War and psalmsThe low register as a foundation

During the war Lili and Nadia helped organise a Franco-American committee supporting Conservatoire musicians serving in the armed forces. Correspondence and practical assistance accompanied composition. Lili’s psalm settings belong to these years and differ sharply in character. Psalm 24 uses ceremonial brass and choral proclamation; Psalm 129 sets oppression and defiance. Psalm 130, Du fond de l’abîme, completed in 1917 after work begun in 1914, has a much larger span, with contralto and tenor soloists, chorus, organ and orchestra. Its text is French.

In Psalm 130 the low instruments establish a register from which the chorus and solo voices must emerge. Repeated bass material, chromatic friction and the alternation of collective with individual utterance organise the span. A harsh sonority can remain in place while orchestration and dynamics change its weight. Vieille prière bouddhique gives tenor soloist and chorus a different religious text, a French version of a Buddhist prayer for the happiness of all living beings.

Two numberingsThe same biblical prayer may carry different psalm numbers in Hebrew and Greek/Latin traditions. Du fond de l’abîme is normally catalogued as Psalm 130, corresponding to Psalm 129 in the Vulgate numbering.

D’un matin de printemps and D’un soir triste, dating from 1917–18, survive in chamber and orchestral forms. The first uses quick articulation and repeated rhythmic activity; the second allows a related musical vocabulary to move under a much heavier pulse. In both, the bass and recurring motives give shape to the descriptive title. In her final months, too ill to write consistently, Boulanger dictated the Pie Jesu to Nadia. Its solo voice, string quartet, harp and organ make a deliberately restricted ensemble. She died at Mézy-sur-Seine in March 1918, aged twenty-four.

Nadia BoulangerTeaching after Lili

Nadia lived until 1979 and made the preservation and performance of Lili’s music a continuing responsibility. Her own compositions included songs, organ and chamber music and the opera La Ville morte, written with Raoul Pugno; she largely ceased composing after Lili’s death. Alongside teaching, she was a pianist, organist and conductor, involved in early-music performance as well as contemporary work. Her editions, performances and advocacy shaped which of Lili’s scores became available to later listeners.

She taught at the American Conservatory at Fontainebleau from its opening in 1921 and also in Paris. Aaron Copland, Elliott Carter, Walter Piston, Philip Glass and Quincy Jones belonged to different generations of her pupils. The common discipline involved harmony, contrapuntal exercises, score-reading and realising music at the keyboard; Bach and older vocal polyphony were practical models. Her pupils developed strikingly different styles, from Copland’s broad tonal spacing and Carter’s rhythmic independence to Glass’s later repetitive structures.

55.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

𝄢Bass & pedal

A sustained or returning low note can establish the reference point for a long span, even as chords above it create friction. In Psalm 130, orchestral depth and persistence are structural resources: follow the low register before trying to identify a succession of conventional cadences.

𝄞Choral mass

Unison, divided parts and a solo voice have different rhetorical weights. The psalms move between collective proclamation and individual utterance; instrumental colour can strengthen or resist a choral statement. Compare Psalm 24’s brass-led public address with the contralto’s part in Du fond de l’abîme.

♭Harmony

Modal inflections coexist with dense chromatic movement. A narrow melodic figure can remain recognisable while its harmonic setting changes; dissonant notes may be held, delaying resolution. The effect depends on spacing and register as well as on which pitch classes a chord contains.

𝄋Cyclic memory

In Clairières dans le ciel, returning musical material connects poems separated by changes of tempo and character. The final song remembers earlier music. This gives the piano a role comparable to the orchestra’s memory in a larger dramatic work.

𝅘𝅥Instrumental versions

The chamber and orchestral forms of D’un matin de printemps and D’un soir triste reveal which gestures survive a change of medium. A piano attack decays while a wind instrument or string section can sustain; orchestration redistributes the same activity among these different sounds.

55.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1912–13
    Pour les funérailles d’un soldatBaritone, mixed chorus and piano or orchestra; poem by Alfred de Musset
    A pre-war setting of Musset’s military funeral poem. The distinction between the solo utterance and collective response anticipates the later large choral scores.
  2. 1913
    Faust et HélèneMezzo-soprano, tenor, baritone and orchestra
    The Prix de Rome cantata, on Eugène Adenis’s text after Goethe. A substantial dramatic score with three characters.
  3. 1913–14
    Clairières dans le cielVoice and piano; thirteen poems by Francis Jammes
    Recurring ideas and harmonic memories bind a cycle about a remembered young woman. The final song, Demain fera un an, gathers earlier material into a longer span.
  4. 1916
    Psalm 24: La terre appartient à l’ÉternelTenor, mixed chorus and ensemble including brass and organ
    A compact ceremonial setting. Its massed proclamation provides a useful contrast with the scale and low-register tension of Psalm 130.
  5. 1917
    Psalm 130: Du fond de l’abîmeContralto, tenor, mixed chorus, organ and orchestra
    Begun in 1914. A large French-language setting of the prayer from the depths; bass persistence and the alternation of solo and choral writing control its form.
  6. 1917
    Vieille prière bouddhiqueTenor, mixed chorus and orchestra
    Completed after work begun in 1914. Sets a French version of a Buddhist prayer for all living beings, with a religious vocabulary distinct from the psalms.
  7. 1917–18
    D’un soir tristeChamber versions and orchestra
    A slow instrumental work whose repeated weight and accumulated sonorities should be heard alongside D’un matin de printemps.
  8. 1917–18
    D’un matin de printempsFlute or violin and piano; piano trio; orchestra
    Quick articulations and repeated figures exchange between instruments. Compare versions to hear what changes when piano resonance becomes sustained orchestral colour.
  9. 1918
    Pie JesuSolo voice, string quartet, harp and organ
    Her last completed work, dictated to Nadia. The compact ensemble separates the vocal prayer from instrumental lines that remain individually audible.

55.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

The photograph belongs to the press coverage surrounding Lili’s Prix de Rome. It places the sisters in the same documentary frame before Nadia’s long teaching career made her the better-known name. Their musical collaboration was practical: study, performance, copying and, at the end of Lili’s life, dictation. The surviving scores distinguish two composing careers.

Francis Jammes

Elle était descendue

From Tristesses, 1906; set in 1913–14
FrenchElle était descendue au bas de la prairie, et, comme la prairie était toute fleurie de plantes dont la tige aime à pousser dans l’eau, ces plantes inondées je les avais cueillies. Bientôt, s’étant mouillée, elle gagna le haut de cette prairie-là qui était toute fleurie. Elle riait et s’ébrouait avec la grâce dégingandée qu’ont les jeunes filles trop grandes. Elle avait le regard qu’ont les fleurs de lavande.
Our translationShe had gone down to the bottom of the meadow, and, as the meadow was full of flowering plants whose stems like to grow in water, I had picked those water-covered plants. Soon, having got wet, she went to the upper part of that meadow which was full of flowers. She laughed and shook herself with the gangling grace of girls who have grown too tall. Her gaze had the look of lavender flowers.

The first song of Clairières dans le ciel begins with this precise memory: wet plants, an awkward movement, a gaze. Jammes describes the meadow twice as the speaker returns to it. Boulanger’s cycle similarly allows remembered musical material to return with altered significance. Jammes died in 1938; this is the public-domain French original with a new translation.

Lili’s legacy owes much to Nadia’s preservation and performance of her scores. Nadia’s teaching has its own extensively documented lineage: Copland, Carter, Piston, Glass and Quincy Jones were her pupils.

Questions to take away

  1. What changes when the unsettled organ writing gives way to the quiet major-key ending?

    A single high voice faces the organ’s restless harmony, with four strings and a harp rather than a full orchestra. Alain Fauqueur sings Boulanger’s last completed work; listen for how the small ensemble makes its final calm sound earned rather than automatic.

    Pie Jesu

  2. How does a psalm that begins “grave, rude” arrive at such a restrained close?

    The orchestra speaks at length before Pierre Mollet’s baritone takes up the psalm. Boulanger allowed several vocal scorings; this recording names a solo baritone, not a male chorus in his place. Follow the forceful declamation towards its unexpectedly quiet, suspended ending.
