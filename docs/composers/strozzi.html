Life

DatesParish and notarial records, many of them found by Ellen Rosand and Beth Glixon, supply the dates of Barbara’s baptism, her children’s baptisms, Giulio Strozzi’s wills and her death. Her birth date is unknown.

Barbara was baptised on 6 August 1619 in the parish of Santa Sofia, Venice, as Barbara Valle. Her mother, Isabella Garzoni, was called la Griega or la Greghetta, and the register names no father. The writer Giulio Strozzi, a prominent member of the Accademia degli Incogniti, adopted her. A will of 1628 still called her Barbara Valle; in a later one Giulio named “Barbara di Santa Sofia” as his chosen daughter, “commonly called la Strozzi”. Isabella lived with them both until her death in 1653. On 8 May 1677, a few months before Barbara died, a notarial document drawn up by her declared that she was the daughter of the late Giulio, perhaps for the first time.

1619–⁠37Giulio Strozzi’s house

Giulio, born in Venice in 1583, wrote verse, prose and opera libretti and had worked with Monteverdi. By 1634 Barbara was singing in his house, and Francesco Cavalli, one of the leading Venetian composers, taught her; in 1651 she called him her “courteous teacher” from childhood. In 1635 and 1636 Nicolò Fontei published two books of Bizzarrie poetiche for one to three voices, mostly to texts by Giulio. He dedicated the first to Giovanni Paolo Widmann, a Carinthian merchant living in Venice, and wrote that he had composed the songs chiefly to please “the most gentle and most accomplished young woman, Signora Barbara”, “almost a tenth sister” to the Muses.

The Genoese poet Gian Vincenzo Imperiale, another Incognito, noted in his travel journal for 1 November 1635 that he had heard Barbara sing at her father’s house; all esteemed her “one of the Muses of Parnassus”. He returned a few days later and wrote of the evening: “I do not know which was greater, the pleasure received from the music or that felt from the discourses.”

1637–⁠44The Unisoni and the Widmann family

In 1637 Giulio founded a formal academy, the Accademia degli Unisoni, with several Incogniti among its members. Accounts of its meetings were printed as Le veglie de’ signori Unisoni (1638) and dedicated to “Signora Barbara Strozzi”, who performed with other musicians and acted as mistress of ceremonies at some debates. She delivered the discourses written by Matteo Dandolo and Giovanni Francesco Loredan for the Contesa del canto e delle lagrime (1638), on whether song or tears did more to stir love. In the printed text she told the academicians that they would not have honoured her with their presence “if I had invited you to see me weep, not to hear me sing”. Satires aimed at the Unisoni mocked Giulio’s poetry and Barbara’s reputation as a courtesan. No record shows her on an opera stage, although the first Venetian opera houses opened in these years and Giulio was involved in them from 1639 at the latest.

Bernardo Strozzi, a Genoese painter active in Venice from the early 1630s and no relation of the family, painted a young woman musician who is generally identified as Barbara; a copy was made in 1639. He also painted Giulio and Monteverdi.

By 1640 Barbara was close to Widmann, a friend of her father who had admired her singing since 1634. Widmann was married. Their son Giulio Pietro was baptised on 6 February 1641, less than a month after the first performance of La finta pazza, a drama by Giulio Strozzi dedicated to Widmann, with music by Francesco Sacrati. Glixon considers Widmann probably the father of three more children: Isabella Aquilina, baptised on 18 March 1642; Lauretta, born on 10 December 1643; and Massimo. In December 1642 Barbara lent Widmann 2,000 ducats.

1644–⁠64Eight books

Barbara was twenty-five when her Il primo libro de’ madrigali for two to five voices was printed by Alessandro Vincenti in 1644, a few months after Lauretta’s birth. Giulio wrote every text. The book was dedicated to Vittoria della Rovere, Grand Duchess of Tuscany. In the dedication Barbara called it “the first work, which as a woman I send into the light too boldly”, and asked that it rest “under an oak of gold” safe from “the thunderbolts of readied slander”. Monteverdi had died the year before. Glixon suggests the book may have been meant as a tribute to him; Barbara knew him but never called him her teacher.

In 1650 Giulio made a will describing himself as without means. The house where he lived was rented by Barbara, and he trusted her to make the customary charitable gifts on his behalf, as she would do willingly, “remembering what I have done for her in bringing her up and setting her on the path of virtue”.

The second book, Cantate, ariette e duetti (Gardano, 1651), was dedicated to the Emperor Ferdinand III and opened with a piece for his marriage to Eleonora Gonzaga. Its dedication, dated 1 June 1651, names Cavalli. The book contained the first of her many laments, on the execution of the Marquis of Cinq-⁠Mars, a favourite of Louis XIII who had joined a plot against Richelieu. Only two of its texts were by Giulio. He died on 31 March 1652.

Her third book (1654) carried the dedication Ignotae deae, to an unknown goddess. A fourth, which according to her letter of 10 November 1655 was to be finished that year and dedicated to Carlo II, Duke of Mantua, is lost. The fifth, Sacri musicali affetti (1655), is a book of devotional pieces for solo voice dedicated to Anna de’ Medici, Archduchess of Austria; only its Salve regina sets a liturgical text, and Glixon notes the intimate tone of its Latin dedication. In 1656 Barbara’s two daughters entered the convent of the Santo Sepolcro, and her motet Quis dabit mihi, for alto, tenor and bass, was printed in Bartolomeo Marcesso’s anthology Sacra corona.

The sixth book, Ariette a voce sola (1657), was dedicated to Prince Francesco Carafa, who had already heard several of its pieces. The seventh, Diporti di Euterpe (1659), went to Nicolò Sagredo, procurator of San Marco and ambassador extraordinary to Pope Alexander VII, whom she addressed as her “tutelary god”; he was the only Venetian dedicatee of her books. Her eighth, Arie (1664), was dedicated to Sophia, Duchess of Brunswick-⁠Lüneburg, who visited Venice that year, and opens with a poem by Giuseppe Artale in praise of her.

1665–⁠77Padua

The last mention of new music is a letter of 30 May 1665 about pieces she had composed for the Duke of Mantua. Very little is known of her musical activity in her last decade. She died in Padua on 11 November 1677 after three months of illness and was buried in the church of the Eremitani.

The invention of the cantataIn his Six English Cantatas (London, 1710), Johann Christoph Pepusch credited Strozzi with inventing the mixture of recitative and aria; John Hawkins took up the claim in 1776 on the evidence of her Op. 2. Glixon notes that the chamber cantata had begun in Venice in the 1620s, when Barbara was a child.

Her printed books and manuscript copies kept her name known in England. On 4 May 1940 Luigi Dallapiccola’s orchestration of Amor dormiglione, a canzonetta from her second book, was played at the Palazzo Strozzi in Florence in a concert of Medici-era music during the sixth Maggio Musicale Fiorentino.

Dallapiccola’s scoreThe orchestration is lost. In 2004 Mario Ruffini found the printed programme in Dallapiccola’s papers in Florence and the orchestral parts in the Maggio Musicale archive, and reconstructed the score from the parts: two flutes, oboe, clarinet, bassoon, horn, trumpet, harpsichord and strings.

The sound

♮Voice and continuo

Most of her pieces are for one voice, usually a soprano, with basso continuo. The exceptions include the madrigals for two to five voices of Op. 1, the duets of Op. 2, the motet Quis dabit mihi for alto, tenor and bass, and the serenata Hor che Apollo in Op. 8, which adds two violins. On page 5 of the 1659 print, the last staff of Sino alla morte is a run of fast notes with no new syllable beneath it.

♯Forms

Strozzi named her pieces aria, cantata, arietta, soliloquio, lamento and, once, serenata. Ruffini counts more than half of the pieces in Op. 2 as strophic arias in two sections, with the same music for each stanza; the later books have longer designs, such as ABCD and ABCDA. Johan van Veen, reviewing a Harmonia Mundi disc, singled out the alternation of recitative, arioso and aria, and described L’astratto, in which the lover begins an aria several times and breaks off into recitative.

♭Laments

The piece beginning Lagrime mie is headed “Lamento” in the 1659 print. Ruffini notes that her modal framework is often minor, especially in the laments. The lament on Cinq-⁠Mars takes a political event as its subject; van Veen heard a fast repeated chord in the continuo under its closing line, about Paris trembling and the Seine growing agitated. He also found dissonance and chromaticism on words such as tormento, morte and lamenti across the disc.

𝄞Texts

Giulio Strozzi wrote every text of Op. 1 and two of those in Op. 2. Among the poets she set were authors of dramas for music, including Pietro Paolo Bissari, Aurelio Aureli, Giuseppe Artale and Gaudenzio Brunacci, and noble amateurs such as Marc’Antonio Corraro, Nicola Beregan and Pietro Dolfin. Op. 7 includes verses by the Roman poets Sebastiano Baldini and Giovanni Pietro Monesio. According to Glixon she never set verses by Guarini or Marino, though many of her contemporaries set them.

♩Virtuosity and wit

Glixon writes that Strozzi’s music is very often highly virtuosic and was certainly cut to the measure of her own voice, and that pathos alternates with the witty spirit that must have animated her. The programme for the 1940 concert described Amor dormiglione as a triple-time opening, a short recitative with vocal flourishes and a return of the opening. Its text begins “Amor, non dormir più” (Love, sleep no more) and later calls Love sluggish and slow.

Sister arts

The title page reads “Diporti di Euterpe overo cantate & ariette a voce sola di Barbara Strozzi, opera settima”, consecrated to Nicolò Sagredo, procurator of San Marco and ambassador extraordinary to Pope Alexander VII, and printed at Venice by Francesco Magni. Only two complete copies survive, in Bologna and at the Library of Congress; this is the Library of Congress copy. The table at the end lists fifteen pieces with the poet of each, and names Pietro Dolfin as the author of Lagrime mie, which begins on page 76.

Bernardo Strozzi, who painted the young woman now identified as Barbara, also painted her adoptive father Giulio and Monteverdi, according to Glixon. Giulio Strozzi had known Monteverdi since 1621 and wrote texts for him. The sitter of this portrait is identified through the engraving in the memorial volume Fiori poetici (1644).

Pietro Dolfin

Lagrime mie, opening

Set by Strozzi in Diporti di Euterpe, 1659
ItalianLagrime mie, à che vi trattenete, perche non isfogate il fier dolore che mi toglie’l respiro e opprime il core?
Our translationMy tears, why do you hold back? Why do you not let out the fierce pain that takes my breath and weighs on my heart?

The first three lines of the text of the piece that begins on page 76 of the 1659 book under the heading “Lamento”. The table at the end gives “Parole dell’Illustrissimo Sig. Pietro Dolfino”. Dolfin was one of the Venetian noble amateurs whose verses Strozzi set. The punctuation is editorial.

Lineage

Strozzi
Passed toLuigi Dallapiccola

Cavalli taught her, and Giulio Strozzi wrote the texts of her first book and brought her into the Incogniti and the Unisoni. Glixon found an affinity between some of the 1644 madrigals and Monteverdi’s last books, and R. L. Kendrick compared her motet Quis dabit mihi with a scene from his Il ritorno d’Ulisse in patria; she never called herself his pupil. Dallapiccola orchestrated Amor dormiglione for the concert of 4 May 1940.

  • Beth L. Glixon, “Strozzi, Barbara”, Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani, vol. 94 (Istituto della Enciclopedia Italiana, 2019). encyclopaedia article

    An archival biography in Italian by the scholar who found many of the records, establishing dates, documents and dedications, with a bibliography.

  • Ellen Rosand, “Barbara Strozzi, ‘virtuosissima cantatrice’: The Composer’s Voice”, Journal of the American Musicological Society 31/2 (1978), pp. 241–⁠281. journal article

    A study of her career and style that relates her life, the Unisoni and the satires written against them to her arias and cantatas. Later accounts draw on it.

  • Beth L. Glixon, “New Light on the Life and Career of Barbara Strozzi”, The Musical Quarterly 81/2 (1997), pp. 311–⁠335. journal article

    Archival findings on her children, her loans and her finances, and on the letters of 1655 and 1665 that bear on her last books.

  • Beth L. Glixon and Wendy Heller, eds., Barbara Strozzi in Context (Cambridge University Press, 2026). essay collection

    On family, sex and the position of women in seventeenth-century Venice, and how they bore on her career. The essays include chapters on Giulio Strozzi and on Barbara as daughter and mother.

  • Susan Clermont, “Barbara Strozzi at 400”, In The Muse (Library of Congress, 17 September 2019). blog essay

    A short account of the Library of Congress copy of Diporti di Euterpe, Op. 7, of its dedication to Sagredo, and of the motet Quis dabit mihi in Sacra corona.

  • Mario Ruffini, “Barbara Strozzi nel Novecento: la scoperta di una nuova composizione per il catalogo di Luigi Dallapiccola” (Centro Studi Luigi Dallapiccola, 2005), pp. 216–⁠227. article
