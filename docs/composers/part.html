Arvo Pärt

Born 11 September 1935, Paide, Estonia

He joined a moving scale to the notes of a single triad, then used that rule to rebuild his musical language.

Life

Pärt was born in Paide in 1935 and grew up in Rakvere, where he began piano lessons. Estonia’s incorporation into the Soviet Union placed his musical education within a system that could reward craftsmanship while treating some techniques and religious texts as political offences. He studied briefly with Veljo Tormis before military service, then entered the Tallinn Conservatoire in 1957 to study with Heino Eller. From 1958 to 1967 he worked as a sound engineer at Estonian Radio. Recording, editing and hearing a wide range of music were practical parts of his education.

Nekrolog (1960) introduced twelve-note writing into Estonian orchestral music; Perpetuum mobile (1963) builds a large orchestral process from accumulating activity. In Collage über B-A-C-H (1964), a borrowed Baroque language comes into conflict with modern instrumental writing. Quotation exposes the distance between different ways of organising musical time and makes that distance the work’s subject.

Credo, 1968A public declaration

Credo, for piano, chorus and orchestra, made the collision explicit. Bach’s C major Prelude from Book I of The Well-Tempered Clavier stands beside violent disintegration; a Latin declaration of Christian belief stands inside an officially atheist culture. Neeme Järvi conducted the premiere in Tallinn in 1968. The audience demanded a repeat, but the work subsequently fell under an effective performance ban. Its religious text mattered to the authorities at least as much as its musical language.

Pärt’s ensuing crisis was a problem of composition as well as censorship. He no longer trusted the available methods to tell him what a note or interval should mean. He studied Gregorian chant, early polyphony and Renaissance music, filling notebooks with exercises. The Third Symphony of 1971 belongs to this search, using modal and contrapuntal procedures that preceded the later system. Film commissions continued to provide work during the eight years between Credo and Für Alina.

Two lines, one ruleThe M-voice moves through a scale. The T-voice selects notes from a fixed triad according to a stated relation to the M-voice — above it, below it, or in alternating positions. Their relationship creates the counterpoint.

1976The invention of tintinnabuli

In 1972 Pärt married Nora and joined the Orthodox Church. In 1976 the short piano piece Für Alina introduced the method he called tintinnabuli, from the Latin word for a small bell. A scale-based line is paired with a voice restricted to the notes of a triad. The two parts are inseparable in construction: when the melodic note changes, the rule determines its triadic partner. A simple surface can therefore result from exact decisions about position, direction and the treatment of unisons.

A moving scale note can rub against a nearby pitch of the fixed triad, and a long decay gives the ear time to register that friction. The method also accommodates sharply different tempi and instrumental colours. The quick figuration and abrupt interruptions of the first movement of Tabula rasa are as much part of the new language as the quiet piano of Für Alina. Silence articulates the pattern.

Several defining works followed in 1977. Cantus in Memory of Benjamin Britten sends descending A minor scales through strings at different speeds, with a bell marking another level of time. Fratres sets a recurrent harmonic pattern against changes of register and presentation; its identity survives many authorised instrumentations. Tabula rasa, for two violins, prepared piano and strings, sets the active Ludus against the slow Silentium. Gidon Kremer and Tatjana Grindenko were its first solo violinists.

Leaving EstoniaVienna, Berlin, ECM

In January 1980 Pärt left the Soviet Union with Nora and their two sons. They first settled in Vienna, then moved to Berlin in 1981 with a DAAD scholarship. The religious basis of his work had remained a source of conflict with Soviet officials. Für Alina, Cantus, Fratres, Tabula rasa and Spiegel im Spiegel had all been composed in Estonia. Emigration changed his access to performers, commissions and sustained international recording.

Manfred Eicher’s ECM release Tabula rasa in 1984 brought several early tintinnabuli works together and launched the label’s New Series. The performers included Kremer, Grindenko, Alfred Schnittke and Keith Jarrett, in different pieces and sessions. This association helped establish a recognisable recorded presentation of Pärt’s music, embracing forms as different as a piano miniature, a violin variation sequence and a double concerto.

Text as a mechanismIn Pärt’s vocal works, syllable counts, word lengths and punctuation can determine musical lengths and melodic patterns.

Passion and prayerLarger forms

Passio (1982) sets the Passion according to St John in Latin. Different vocal and instrumental groups carry the Evangelist, Jesus, Pilate and the crowd. A restricted set of procedures regulates the declamation, allowing changes in narrative role and punctuation to acquire unusual weight. Te Deum (1984–85) uses three choirs, prepared piano, strings and a recorded wind-harp sound. The fixed background and moving voices make the relation between sustained pitch and human breath audible on a larger scale.

Pärt’s later catalogue includes the Church Slavonic Kanon pokajanen (1997), the Latin prayer Da pacem Domine (2004), and orchestral works in which an unsung text can guide composition. These pieces draw on different Christian languages and traditions. Like Reich, he restricts material and makes process audible. Pärt pairs scale and triad; Reich gradually displaces repeating patterns.

After Estonian independence, performances and recording projects re-established his ties with the country. He returned to live there permanently in 2010, when he and Nora founded the Arvo Pärt Centre at Laulasmaa to preserve his archive and support research. The centre’s purpose makes the notebooks and compositional procedures available beside the familiar recordings. Pärt turned ninety-one in September 2026; his catalogue continues to include revisions and new instrumental versions as well as separately titled compositions.

The sound

Harmony, line, rhythm, colour and form.

Tintinnabuli

A melody or M-voice moves through a scale; a T-voice uses only a fixed triad. A rule relates the two. The triadic voice may remain above, below or change sides. The resulting seconds and sevenths are part of the method.

Counterpoint

Canons, inversion and augmentation organise material that initially seems elementary. In Cantus, descending scales unfold at different speeds and in different registers. Augmentation means lengthening a line’s note values while preserving their proportions; the same descent can therefore occupy several levels of time at once.

Text

Word length, syllables and punctuation can generate melodic and rhythmic patterns. In the regular declamation of Passio, characters and narrative functions receive their own musical conditions, so a small change of pitch or duration becomes legible within a stable framework.

Resonance

A bell, a prepared piano and a sustained string note have different attacks and decays. Pärt leaves enough space for those differences to be heard. In Tabula rasa the prepared piano supplies a metallic punctuation. The resonance continues to shape a phrase after the written attack.

Form

Processes of expansion, contraction and return often replace thematic development. Spiegel im Spiegel enlarges scale-based phrases around a stable centre; Fratres revisits a recurring harmonic design. The listener can track changing phrase length or register while the pitch collection remains fixed. Repetition is a condition for hearing those changes.

Editorial comparison

Three angels sit around one table; repeated outlines and inclined heads bind distinct figures into a single composition. The icon belongs to the Orthodox tradition Pärt entered in 1972. The comparison is theological and formal: a restricted set of elements carries distinctions through placement and relation.

Documented useAnonymous Latin antiphon

Da pacem Domine

Ninth century
LatinDa pacem, Domine, in diebus nostris, quia non est alius qui pugnet pro nobis nisi tu, Deus noster.
English · Contrapunctus translationGrant peace, Lord, in our days, for there is no other who may fight on our behalf but you, our God.

Jordi Savall commissioned Pärt’s setting in 2004; Pärt began it two days after the Madrid train bombings of 11 March. The prayer’s compact clauses support a four-part texture whose sustained lines move at different rates. The old liturgical words address a particular recent event.

Later connectionsContemporary choral repertoireFilm and concert uses of tintinnabuli

Pärt studied chant intensively; his scale-and-triad system is his own invention. Reich and Gubaidulina offer useful contrasts: the first builds rhythmic processes from repetition, while the second makes religious drama through instrumental pressure, register and numerical proportions.
