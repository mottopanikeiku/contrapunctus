Life

On 16 November 1968, in Tallinn’s Estonia Concert Hall, Neeme Järvi conducted a new work that the printed programme did not explain. Pärt’s Credo set a Christian declaration for a Soviet radio choir. At the piano, Mart Lille played Bach’s C major Prelude; around it, the orchestra broke into loud, dissonant noise, then grew quiet again. The officials who should have heard the work beforehand were away in Moscow. The audience demanded that the whole work be played again. Later performances were stopped.

1935–⁠68Tallinn Conservatoire and Estonian Radio

Pärt was born in Paide in 1935 and grew up in Rakvere, where he learned the piano with Ille Martin. After a brief period of study with Veljo Tormis and two years of military service, he entered Heino Eller’s class at the Tallinn Conservatoire in 1957. He graduated in 1963. By then he had written Nekrolog, the first twelve-note orchestral work in Estonian music, and Perpetuum mobile. From 1958 to 1967 he worked as a sound engineer at Estonian Radio, where his work involved microphones and tape.

The young composer tried out the newest techniques with conspicuous success and growing dissatisfaction. In Collage über B-A-C-H (1964), Baroque music is set beside sounds that could tear it apart. Credo took this approach further. Its text set the command to take an eye for an eye against Christ’s refusal of retaliation; its orchestra built a violent heap of fifths and then dismantled it.

1968–⁠76Gregorian chant and Für Alina

The working yearsPärt continued to write for the cinema during his crisis, including Leida Laius’s Ukuaru (1973) and Grigori Kromanov’s Diamonds for the Dictatorship of the Proletariat (1975).

He began again with a single line. “I just wanted a simple musical line, internally alive and breathing,” he told Enzo Restagno in conversations published in 2010. A Tallinn church gave him a Liber usualis, the collection of Gregorian chant, and he sang and played his way through it. He studied the early polyphony of Notre Dame and Renaissance sacred music. The Third Symphony of 1971 drew on these studies in an orchestral score. In 1972 he married Nora and joined the Orthodox Church.

The girl in the titleAlina was the daughter of the Pärts’ friend Irena Veisaitė, a Lithuanian theatre scholar. The Iron Curtain separated mother and daughter. Alina lived in England and her mother in the Soviet Union.

In February 1976 he wrote Für Alina. The piano plays a melody whose phrases gain a note at a time, grow to eight notes, then shrink. Beside it runs a second line confined to the B minor triad. Every melodic note has its prescribed partner. Once, the second line steps outside the triad onto C♯, and the pedal lifts. Rein Rannap first played the piece as an unannounced encore in Tallinn on 23 March.

Pärt called the method tintinnabuli, from the Latin for a little bell. The melody can move up and down a scale while the other voice keeps returning to its three notes. A note in one part may clash with its neighbour in the other. Für Alina is written for two hands.

1977–⁠78Cantus, Fratres and Tabula rasa

When the radio announced Britten’s death in December 1976, Pärt’s hope of meeting him ended. Pärt had been struck by the tenderness he heard in Britten’s music. Cantus in Memory of Benjamin Britten (1977) begins with three bars of written silence and a tolling bell. The strings descend through A minor at different speeds, their lines lengthening as they go, until they arrive at a low chord. The bell goes on ringing after the strings have stopped.

Fratres, written the same year for the early-music players of Hortus Musicus, had no fixed instrumentation. Its recurring three-part theme, over a low drone, could be played by different instruments; in 1980 Pärt added a demanding violin part for Gidon Kremer. In Spiegel im Spiegel (1978), commissioned by Vladimir Spivakov, the violin repeatedly returns to A. Each departure takes it one note further away, while the piano keeps up its three-note accompaniment.

Kremer also asked for Tabula rasa, a double concerto for himself and Tatjana Grindenko, with prepared piano and strings. Its scales and triads baffled the players in rehearsal. The first movement, Ludus, consists of eight variations and a cadenza; in Silentium, the bass moves fastest and the first solo violin slowest. At the première on 30 September 1977, in the assembly hall of the Tallinn Polytechnic Institute, the performance came together. “Never again have I experienced the silence that took over the assembly hall after the premiere,” Nora recalled to Immo Mihkelson for Estonian radio in 2005.

1980–⁠84Emigration and the ECM New Series

A Central Committee official visited the Pärts at home in autumn 1979 and suggested they leave. “It was supposed to look like a voluntary decision,” Nora told Restagno in the conversations published in 2010. In January 1980 they left with their two sons, settling first in Vienna and moving to Berlin on a DAAD scholarship in 1981. At the railway border station at Brest, guards searching their luggage had played a recording of Cantus.

Manfred Eicher first heard Tabula rasa on a car radio, without knowing its title or composer. In 1984 he made it the first release of ECM’s New Series. Kremer and Grindenko played the concerto, with the composer Alfred Schnittke at the prepared piano; elsewhere on the album Kremer played Fratres with Keith Jarrett. Another partnership began that January on a train from London’s Victoria station to Gatwick: Paul Hillier and Pärt used the airport trip to discuss the music that the Hilliard Ensemble would soon be singing.

Passion and prayerPassio and later choral works

The EvangelistIn Passio, four singers share the narration, joined by violin, oboe, cello and bassoon. Jesus and Pilate have their own solo voices and organ accompaniment; the choir sings the crowd and the smaller roles.

Passio (1982) sets St John’s account of the arrest, trial and crucifixion in Latin. Punctuation governs the lengths of sung notes; Jesus is given slower note values than the people questioning him. The Hilliards’ performances restored Pärt’s confidence in a score he had doubted. Te Deum (1984–⁠85) is scored for three choirs, strings and prepared piano above a recorded wind harp. A sustained D and A are held beneath the singers throughout.

For Kanon pokajanen (1997), he set the Church Slavonic Canon of Repentance for unaccompanied choir. Tõnu Kaljuste and the Estonian Philharmonic Chamber Choir gave its première in Cologne Cathedral in 1998. Jordi Savall commissioned the much shorter Da pacem Domine (2004), in which the old chant runs through the alto part and three new voices surround it. At its first performance in Barcelona, Savall dedicated the concert to the victims of that March’s Madrid bombings.

Pärt’s ties with Estonia were renewed after independence in 1991, and Kaljuste and his Estonian musicians became important recording partners. In 2010 Arvo and Nora returned to live there permanently and founded the Arvo Pärt Centre at Laulasmaa. The archive holds his working papers, including sketches and diagrams.

    Gidon Kremer and Tatjana Grindenko (violins); Alfred Schnittke (prepared piano); Lithuanian Chamber Orchestra; Saulius Sondeckis (conductor) · ECM New Series · November 1977, live

    Two solo violins lead this double concerto through eight variations before the slow “Silentium”, in which the voices move at different speeds. Kremer and Grindenko, the violin soloists at the première, are joined by Alfred Schnittke on prepared piano on the album that launched ECM’s New Series, released in 1984. It is the recording to start with, and Kremer and Keith Jarrett’s Fratres opens the same album.

The Hilliards sing steadily with very little vibrato, so each interval is clear; the recording brought the work to international attention and convinced Pärt, after years of doubt, that the score worked.

The sound

♮Tintinnabuli

One voice moves by step through a scale. The other uses only the three notes of a fixed chord. Pärt set how far above or below the melody that second voice must sit. The two lines can meet in a third or a second. In Für Alina, the accompanying notes come from B minor, and one C♯ stands out against them.

𝄢Counterpoint

In Cantus, five groups of strings descend at different speeds. Their lines lengthen, overlap and end on the same low A minor chord. The piece is a prolation canon, in which the notes of one line are played at several different rhythmic values. The bell tolls above the strings and is still heard after the bows have stopped.

𝅘𝅥Text

The full stops in Passio last longer than the commas. Pärt gave each speaker a basic note length and adjusted it to the words’ position in the sentence. Jesus has the slowest, lowest part; Pilate is higher and faster. Four voices share the Evangelist’s account.

𝄐Bells and piano

The piano in Tabula rasa has screws placed between its strings. The metallic rattle they produce links it to the bell in Cantus, though the piano can play a whole arpeggio at once. In the slow Silentium, its attacks interrupt long string notes.

𝄋Returning

The violin in Spiegel im Spiegel returns repeatedly to A. Each phrase adds a step to the outward walk, and each ascending phrase has a descending answer. The piano’s three-note figures mark the increasing distance. In Fratres, a three-part theme returns in a new octave over the same low drone.

Works

A selection from the composer’s works.

  1. 1968
    CredoPiano, mixed choir and orchestra
    Bach’s C major Prelude is set against an orchestral eruption, around a text from the Creed and St Matthew. The Tallinn audience demanded an immediate repeat; further performances were stopped.
  2. 1976
    Für AlinaPiano
    The first tintinnabuli piece reached the public as Rein Rannap’s unannounced encore in Tallinn. Its companion voice leaves the B minor triad for one exposed C♯.
  3. 1977
    FratresVarious authorised instrumental versions
    First written without fixed instrumentation for Hortus Musicus. Pärt added solo variations for violin and piano in 1980 and dedicated that version to Gidon and Elena Kremer.
  4. 1977
    Cantus in Memory of Benjamin BrittenString orchestra and bell
    Pärt had hoped to meet Britten. Five string lines descend at different speeds and converge on A minor, and the bell keeps ringing after the final chord.
  5. 1977
    Tabula rasaTwo violins, prepared piano and string orchestra
    Ludus has eight variations and a cadenza; in Silentium the lines move at different speeds. Kremer and Grindenko were the first soloists, with Helju Tauk at the prepared piano.
  6. 1978
    Spiegel im SpiegelViolin and piano; later versions for other instruments
    Written for Vladimir Spivakov, who first played it with Boris Bekhterev at the Moscow Conservatory. The violin’s widening phrases always return to A.
  7. 1982
    PassioVocal soloists, mixed choir, instrumental quartet and organ
    St John’s Passion in Latin, with four singers sharing the Evangelist. Jesus is set in longer note values than the other characters; the punctuation of the text helps set the pace.
  8. 1984–⁠85; revised 1992
    Te DeumThree choirs, prepared piano, string orchestra and recorded wind harp
    A recording of a wind harp holds D and A beneath the singers. The final repetitions of Sanctus are sung above a sustained string chord.
  9. 1997
    Kanon pokajanenUnaccompanied mixed choir
    The Church Slavonic Canon of Repentance, first sung complete in Cologne Cathedral in 1998 by Kaljuste’s Estonian Philharmonic Chamber Choir.
  10. 2004
    Da pacem DomineFour vocal parts; several later arrangements
    Jordi Savall commissioned this peace prayer. The alto sings the medieval chant, surrounded by three newly composed voices.

Sister arts

Rublev’s three angels sit around a table, their heads inclined towards one another. They are the visitors received by Abraham in Genesis, understood as an image of the Trinity. The icon belongs to the Orthodox tradition Pärt entered in 1972.

Anonymous Latin antiphon

Da pacem Domine

Ninth century
LatinDa pacem, Domine, in diebus nostris, quia non est alius qui pugnet pro nobis nisi tu, Deus noster.
Our translationGrant peace, Lord, in our days, for there is no other who may fight on our behalf but you, our God.

Jordi Savall asked Pärt for this prayer for a peace concert. The alto sings the melody of the ninth-century antiphon, and Pärt wrote three new parts around it. Savall conducted the first performance in Barcelona in July 2004, at a concert dedicated to those killed in the Madrid train bombings that March.

Lineage

Drew fromJohann Sebastian BachHeino EllerGregorian chantNotre Dame polyphonyRenaissance sacred music
Pärt
Passed toContemporary choral repertoireFilm and concert uses of tintinnabuli

Heino Eller taught Pärt in Tallinn. Bach supplied the prelude at the centre of Credo. A borrowed chant book later helped Pärt arrive at his own melodic line. The canons of Cantus draw on older counterpoint; the pairing of scale and triad was his own invention. Performers took the music further, among them Kremer on the violin, Hillier with the Hilliards and Kaljuste with his Estonian choir. Film-makers have used Fratres, Cantus and Spiegel im Spiegel many times.
