44.1Life

Satie was born in the Norman port of Honfleur to a French father and a Scottish mother. After his mother died in 1872, he lived with his paternal grandparents and received lessons from a local organist. He later joined his father in Paris and entered the Conservatoire in 1879. His periods of study ended without the normal professional qualifications. His mature music would expose elementary actions — a bass note, a repeated chord, a scale fragment.

By the late 1880s he was working in Montmartre, including at the Chat Noir cabaret. The three Sarabandes of 1887 and Gymnopédies of 1888 came from this period. The latter use a slow triple metre, low bass notes and chords on the second beat. Major sevenths remain part of the sonority instead of being immediately corrected into consonances. Their regular accompaniment continues beneath a melody whose phrase-endings often avoid the sort of cadence that would make the next phrase feel inevitable.

MontmartrePlain chords and elaborate identities

In the early 1890s Satie associated with Joséphin Péladan’s Rosicrucian activities and composed music for their ceremonial and theatrical projects. The static chordal writing of Le Fils des étoiles is a different use of the keyboard from the popular songs he played for a living. He also invented an ecclesiastical identity of his own, with himself as its leading functionary. Religious conviction, theatrical costume and satire are hard to disentangle in these documents. The scores show a sustained investigation of chords that recur without developing towards a large climax.

Suzanne Valadon painted his portrait during their brief relationship in 1893. A former artists’ model, she was establishing an independent career as a painter; Satie’s letters record an attachment that continued after the relationship ended. Around this time he wrote Vexations, a small chromatic piece accompanied by an instruction referring to playing its motif 840 times. It was not performed publicly in his lifetime. The much later marathon tradition, initiated by John Cage and fellow pianists in New York in 1963, turned a private manuscript into a different kind of concert event.

The 840 repetitionsThe manuscript’s wording is a direction for preparing to play the motif 840 times. Whether Satie envisaged the modern uninterrupted public marathon remains uncertain.

He moved to Arcueil in 1898 and walked between the suburb and his Paris work. Cabaret songs such as Je te veux belong to the same composer as the austere early piano pieces. Their waltzes, repeated strains and practical accompaniments supplied procedures he could reuse or caricature. In 1905, at thirty-nine, he entered the Schola Cantorum and studied counterpoint with Albert Roussel. He earned a diploma in 1908 and continued studying. Fugues and canons subsequently appear in music that refuses the solemnity usually attached to those forms.

1911–17From the piano page to the ballet stage

Ravel played early Satie pieces at a Société musicale indépendante concert in January 1911, helping to bring music written decades earlier before a new audience. Debussy, a longstanding friend, had already orchestrated two Gymnopédies. Satie continued to innovate. The piano collections of 1912–15 use abrupt changes, literal quotation and written instructions that comment on the act of playing. Some jokes address only the performer reading the page.

Sports et divertissements, composed in 1914, pairs twenty short musical scenes and an opening chorale with drawings by Charles Martin and Satie’s own calligraphy. The work is designed to be read and looked at as well as heard. Embryons desséchés includes a parody of the funeral march from Chopin’s Second Piano Sonata and exaggerates final cadences until the habit of concluding becomes the subject. In both works, the rests, repetitions and disproportionate endings specify the comedy in the music.

The ballet Parade was first performed by Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes in 1917, with a scenario by Cocteau, designs by Picasso and choreography by Léonide Massine. Music-hall routines and ragtime enter an orchestral score that also includes conspicuous noise-making instruments. The premise concerns performers trying to attract spectators into a show, so the advertised entertainment and the entertainment actually seen are deliberately confused. Satie’s short repeated blocks suit this theatrical structure better than a symphonic development would. The collaboration made the relationship between music, scenery and everyday sound an explicit public controversy.

The last worksSpeech without acting

Furniture musicSatie’s musique d’ameublement was intended to accompany activity. His slow piano pieces, by contrast, were written to hold the listener’s attention as concert pieces.

Socrate, completed in 1918 for the patron Winnaretta Singer, Princesse Edmond de Polignac, sets French translations of Plato. Women’s voices narrate encounters with Socrates and his death without assuming conventional operatic roles. The accompaniment repeats restrained figures, holding its reserve through the most consequential sentences. His experiments in furniture music, developed with Darius Milhaud’s help, addressed another question: whether music could remain present while an audience continued talking and moving. Later ambient music borrowed that question within a different listening culture and with electronic means.

In 1924 he worked with Picabia on Relâche for the Ballets Suédois and wrote the music for René Clair’s interlude film Entr’acte. Repeated units could be extended to fit the projected images, organising musical duration around the film. He died in a Paris hospital in July 1925. His manuscripts, letters and drawings preserve several distinct practices — cabaret work, contrapuntal study, private notational experiments and commissioned theatre — which the familiar recordings of the three Gymnopédies only partly represent.

44.3The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Harmony

Sevenths, ninths and added notes can be stable components of a chord rather than promises of resolution. The opening of the First Gymnopédie alternates G major seventh and D major seventh: the sonorities establish a recurring space. Other works use chromatic sequences whose awkward spellings resist simple tonal labels.

𝄆Repetition

A repeated unit can preserve its intensity, volume and orchestration. In furniture music and the film score Cinéma, repetition has a practical relation to duration. In the early piano pieces, return often takes the place of thematic development.

𝄾Notation & language

The unbarred notation of the first published Gnossiennes removes visual metrical boundaries without removing rhythm. Instructions and prose comments can address the pianist privately. Sports et divertissements combines words, drawing and notation on the page; a recording necessarily leaves part of the object out.

𝄋Genre & quotation

Waltz, ragtime, chorale and fugue are recognisable working materials. In Embryons desséchés, a quotation and an absurdly prolonged close make inherited musical behaviour audible as behaviour. The parody depends on accurate rhythm and proportion.

𝄞The speaking voice

Socrate places syllabic narration above recurring figures, withholding the normal operatic equation between emotional crisis and a vocal climax. The female singers report a male philosopher’s life through translated prose. The scoring and text establish that distance.

44.4Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1887
    Trois SarabandesPiano
    Slow triple-time pieces with extended chords. Their dates are important to the later debate about Satie’s relationship to Debussy and Ravel.
  2. 1888
    Trois GymnopédiesPiano
    Bass on the first beat, chord on the second, melody crossing the repeated pattern. Debussy later orchestrated Nos. 1 and 3.
  3. c. 1890
    Trois GnossiennesPiano
    The first published group uses unbarred notation and verbal directions. Additional pieces received the title posthumously, forming the familiar six-piece set.
  4. 1893
    VexationsKeyboard
    A short chromatic motif and harmonisations, with the instruction mentioning 840 repetitions. Modern marathon performances began long after Satie’s death.
  5. 1913
    Embryons desséchésPiano
    Three pieces whose annotations, quotations and inflated cadences parody musical description and the etiquette of a grand ending.
  6. 1914
    Sports et divertissementsPiano, with written texts and drawings by Charles Martin
    Twenty miniature scenes and a prefatory chorale; a combined visual, verbal and musical publication.
  7. 1916–17
    ParadeOrchestra with additional noise-making instruments; ballet
    Cocteau, Picasso and Massine collaborated on the Ballets Russes production. Repeated popular-style blocks match the parade of competing attractions.
  8. 1917–18
    SocrateFemale voices and chamber orchestra; version with piano
    Three movements on Plato in Victor Cousin’s French translation, ending with Socrates’s death. Narration takes the place of operatic impersonation.
  9. 1924
    Relâche, with Cinéma for Entr’acteOrchestra; ballet and film
    Picabia’s Ballets Suédois production incorporates René Clair’s film. The score for the film uses repeatable units to coordinate music and projected action.

44.6Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Valadon painted Satie during their brief relationship in Montmartre. Her outline fixes the head and narrow torso against a plain background, without a piano or other professional attribute. The picture records a relationship between two working artists. She died in 1938, and the painting is in the public domain.

Satie admired Puvis de Chavannes; the painter’s restricted colour, flattened space and restrained gestures provide an artistic context for his preference for reduced means. The link is to the painter’s work generally; this canvas is not named in the score of Socrate. Compare the separation of its figures and their restrained gestures with the work’s detached narration of Socrates’s death.

Satie
Passed toClaude DebussyMaurice RavelFrancis PoulencJohn CageDarius Milhaud

Debussy’s orchestrations and Ravel’s 1911 concert made Satie’s early music public precedents, but Satie continued to work in directions neither composer followed. Cage’s advocacy of Vexations belongs to the later experimental reception. Poulenc learned from the directness of Satie’s popular-style materials without adopting his withdrawal from harmonic development.

Questions to take away

  1. How does a plucked string carry Satie’s held melody across the changing accompaniment?

    Michael Laucke places Satie’s piano texture on six guitar strings, in a different key. Follow how each plucked bass, chord and melodic note carries into the space before the next attack; the recording is licensed CC BY-SA 4.0, not a public-domain sound recording.

    Trois Gymnopédies · I. Lent et douloureux — guitar arrangement

  2. What changes when the same melody reaches its second ending?

    Gmaj7 and Dmaj7 alternate beneath a melody that holds notes across the accompaniment; the repeated span leads to a different final cadence. The score is aligned automatically, with provisional timings not checked by ear.

    Trois Gymnopédies · I. Lent et douloureux
