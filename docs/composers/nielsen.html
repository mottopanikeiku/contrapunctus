Composers / IV. Fin de siècle

CarlNielsen

Born 9 June 1865, Sortelung, Nørre Lyndelse, Funen, DenmarkDied 3 October 1931, Copenhagen, Denmark

Danish composer, violinist and conductor, born on Funen and based in Copenhagen, who wrote six symphonies, the opera Maskarade, a wind quintet and a large body of songs and hymn tunes.

Life

Carl August Nielsen was born on 9 June 1865 at Sortelung, a cottage in the parish of Nørre Lyndelse on Funen. He was the seventh of twelve children. His father, Niels Jørgensen, was a house painter who played the fiddle at dances; he was known as Painter-⁠Niels, and the children took Nielsen as their surname. At six, when he had measles, Carl was handed a three-quarter-size violin that hung on the wall, taught himself a few melodies and played them for his father. Emil Petersen, the assistant teacher at the village school, taught him to play from notes. Petersen later told how he had once passed Painter-⁠Niels’s house and found a boy of seven or eight hammering on blocks of wood laid out as a keyboard.

1879–⁠91Odense, Copenhagen and Paris

Confirmed at fourteen, Nielsen was apprenticed to a shopkeeper whose business failed, and on 1 November 1879 he joined the band of the Sixteenth Battalion in Odense. In 1883 he showed a string quartet to Niels W. Gade in Copenhagen, and in January 1884 he entered the Conservatory on a scholarship, studying the violin and, with Orla Rosenhoff, theory and counterpoint. He left at the end of 1886. His Suite for Strings, Op. 1, was played at Tivoli on 8 September 1888, and in 1889 he joined the second violins of the Royal Orchestra, where he stayed for sixteen years.

A grant from the Ancker legacy took him abroad in 1890. In Dresden he heard Wagner’s Ring and wrote in his diary on 10 September, after Das Rheingold: “The musician who does not think Wagner great is himself very small.” In Paris in the spring of 1891 he met the sculptor Anne Marie Brodersen, and they were married in the English church in Florence on 10 May.

1894–⁠1906First symphony, first operas

The Royal Orchestra under Johan Svendsen gave the First Symphony, in G minor, on 14 March 1894. That autumn Nielsen travelled to promote it, and in Vienna on 7 November Dr Eduard Schiff took him to Brahms. In the diary extract that Nielsen copied to Rosenhoff, Brahms ignored him, then asked in a sharp tone how things stood with Thorvaldsen’s Museum, an allusion to a story about Brahms’s visit to Copenhagen in 1868. Nielsen answered: “Well, it is still standing there, Herr Doctor.” Brahms asked to keep the symphony and the F minor String Quartet.

Hymnus Amoris was sung at the Music Society on 27 April 1897. According to the Carl Nielsen Edition, the idea came in 1891, on his honeymoon, when he and his wife saw a painting by Titian of a jealous husband killing his wife. The opera Saul and David followed at the Royal Theatre on 28 November 1902, and the Second Symphony, The Four Temperaments, three days later. Maskarade, a comic opera to a libretto by Vilhelm Andersen after Ludvig Holberg’s comedy Mascarade, was first performed at the Royal Theatre on 11 November 1906 with Nielsen conducting. According to the Dansk Biografisk Leksikon, everyone at the theatre expected a failure. The opera was given twenty times that season.

1908–⁠16The Fourth Symphony

Two sets of timpaniThe score of the Fourth Symphony places the second pair of timpani opposite the first, at the edge of the orchestra nearest the audience.

Nielsen was conductor at the Royal Theatre from 1908 to 1914. On 3 May 1914 he wrote to his wife about a new work. She was then in Celle, modelling the horse for her equestrian statue of Christian IX. The work would have “no programme” but would express “what we understand by the urge to life or manifestations of life”, “only life and movement, though different, very different, yet in a connection”. In April 1915 he told his friend Julius Röntgen that the symphony rested on the idea that “the most elemental being of music is sound, life and movement that cut the silence to pieces”.

Nielsen finished the score on 14 January 1916 and conducted the first performance at the Music Society in Copenhagen on 1 February. After the repeat performance on 14 April the critic Charles Kjerulf, who had not liked his earlier music, wrote an enthusiastic review in Politiken. Nielsen replied in a letter of 15 April: “Oh, if only I could tell you how rich I feel today, because I have won your deep interest completely.”

1920–⁠22The Fifth Symphony and the Wind Quintet

The side drumA note in the score of the Fifth Symphony says that in one passage of the first part the side drummer plays in his own tempo, “as though he meant at any price to disturb the music”, and later improvises freely.

Nielsen began the Fifth Symphony in the autumn of 1920, and the fair copy is dated 15 January 1922. It has two parts in place of four movements. Nielsen conducted the first performance at the Music Society on 24 January 1922, after five rehearsals instead of the usual three. Politiken printed an interview with him that day: “I roll a stone up a hill and use the strength I have in me to bring it to a high point. There the stone lies quite still. The forces are bound in it, until I give it a kick, and the same forces are released and the stone rolls down again.” The composer Victor Bendix wrote to him the next day that he had come away from the concert abusing “this unclean trench music” and added, “I hate it, but it holds me captive.”

The finale’s themeThe variations that end the Wind Quintet are on Nielsen’s own hymn tune Min Jesus, lad mit Hjerte faa, written in 1914–⁠15 and printed in 1919. The oboist changes to cor anglais for the Prelude.

In the autumn of 1921 Nielsen heard four members of the Copenhagen Wind Quintet rehearsing a Mozart piece with the pianist Christian Christiansen, and told the oboist Svend Christian Felumb that he would like to write a quintet for them. He had begun it before he left for Gothenburg on 1 February 1922 to conduct there, and on 2 March he wrote to his wife that he had got a little further with it. On 16 April he wrote to her: “I have worked hard at my new quintet, and in a couple of days it will actually be finished, for it amuses me greatly.” It was first played in private in Gothenburg on 30 April. The Copenhagen Wind Quintet (Paul Hagemann, flute; Svend Christian Felumb, oboe; Aage Oxenvad, clarinet; Hans Sørensen, horn; Knud Lassen, bassoon) gave it in public at the New Music Society on 9 October 1922. In a programme note Nielsen wrote that the instruments “talk over one another” at one moment and are “quite alone” at the next.

1922–⁠31Last years

A heart attack in July 1922 did not end his conducting, and on 22 June 1923 he led the Fourth Symphony with the London Symphony Orchestra at Queen’s Hall. The Sixth Symphony was first played in public on 11 December 1925, and concertos for two players of the Wind Quintet followed, for flute in 1926 and for clarinet in 1928. From 1914 he also wrote simple strophic songs with the organist Thomas Laub, and he supplied fifty melodies or arrangements to the Folk High School Melody Book of 1922. Jens Vejmand, a setting of Jeppe Aakjær from 1907, sold in large numbers.

His memoir of boyhood, Min fynske Barndom, was published in 1927, and on 1 January 1931 he became director of the Royal Danish Academy of Music. He went into the National Hospital in Copenhagen on 1 October and died there ten minutes after midnight on 3 October. The funeral service at Vor Frue Kirke on 9 October used only his own music, and at Vestre Kirkegård the Royal Orchestra’s wind quintet played the closing chorale of the Wind Quintet.

Nielsen in the Web of Influence

The sound

♮Intervals

Nielsen once said that the task was to “say a pure fifth in such a way that no one thinks he has heard that interval before”. The Dansk Biografisk Leksikon calls the sentence the programme of his art and describes a melodic style built on feeling for the value of each interval and strict economy in using them.

♯Tonality

The First Symphony is in G minor but begins and ends on a C major chord, which the Dansk Biografisk Leksikon describes as unheard of in 1894. Robert Simpson called the tonal plans of Nielsen’s later symphonies “progressive tonality” or “emergent tonality”.

𝄞Counterpoint

Orla Rosenhoff taught him counterpoint at the Conservatory and gave him private lessons afterwards. The Dansk Biografisk Leksikon describes the choruses of Hymnus Amoris as the first in Denmark since Buxtehude to be written in large-scale polyphony, and says that in the symphonies Nielsen developed a contrapuntal combination of themes and a derivation and variation of motifs.

𝄾Percussion

The Fourth Symphony uses two pairs of timpani, the second placed opposite the first at the edge of the orchestra. In the Fifth the side drum plays against the orchestra in its own tempo, set by a metronome, and then improvises.

𝄢Songs

The songs to poems by J. P. Jacobsen of 1891 are mostly through-composed, with independent piano parts; the Ludvig Holstein songs of 1894–⁠96 are mostly strophic. From 1914 he wrote strophic songs for singing in common, and his melodies fill the Folk High School Melody Book.

𝄋Wind instruments

The Wind Quintet is for flute, oboe (changing to cor anglais in the Prelude), clarinet in A, horn in F and bassoon. Nielsen planned a concerto for each of the five players and finished two, for flute and clarinet.

Sister arts

Titian painted the fresco in 1511. On their honeymoon in 1891 Nielsen and Anne Marie Brodersen saw a painting by Titian of a jealous husband killing his wife, and the Carl Nielsen Edition names this fresco as the likely picture. Both resolved to make a work about love in all its forms. Nielsen’s was the cantata Hymnus Amoris of 1896. Anne Marie never made the frieze she had planned, but she drew the title page of the printed score.

Anne Marie Carl-⁠Nielsen won the competition for the statue in 1908, and it was unveiled on 15 November 1927. On 3 May 1914 she was in Celle modelling the horse. Her husband wrote to her that day about the new symphony and added that he would like to see her turn the great stallion for him.

Jeppe Aakjær

Jens Vejmand (“Hvem sidder der bag Skjærmen”)

1905
DanishHvem sidder der bag Skjærmen med Klude om sin Haand, med Læderlap for Øjet og om sin Sko et Baand? Det er saamænd Jens Vejmand, der af sin sure Nød med Ham’ren maa forvandle de haarde Sten til Brød.
Our translationWho sits there behind the screen, with rags around his hand, a leather patch before his eye and a strap about his shoe? Why, that is Jens the roadman, who from his bitter need must with his hammer change the hard stones into bread.

The first of six stanzas, in the spelling of Aakjær’s collection Rugens Sange (1906). The poem is dated Jebjerg, 19 June 1905, and was first printed in Politiken on 26 June 1905. Nielsen set it in 1907 as No. 3 of the first volume of Strophic Songs, Op. 21, dedicated to Bodil Neergaard; the autograph is dated 25 June 1907. The song was published separately and sold very widely.

Nielsen
Passed toMogens WöldikeNancy DalbergKnud Jeppesen

Nielsen heard Wagner’s Ring at Dresden in September 1890, on his first stay in Germany. In Vienna in 1894 he gave his First Symphony to Brahms, who later let him know through the critic William Behrend that he thought it good. According to the Dansk Biografisk Leksikon, Mozart was the one of the great classics who stood nearest to him, and the Wind Quintet began in a rehearsal of a Mozart piece. Knud Ketting writes that the idea of a symphony in one span is said to have come from Liszt’s B minor Sonata, which his friend Henrik Knudsen had played to him. Niels W. Gade and Johan Svendsen were the older composers of his student years in Copenhagen, and Svendsen conducted the First Symphony. Orla Rosenhoff taught him counterpoint. Mogens Wöldike, Nancy Dalberg and Knud Jeppesen were pupils of his.
