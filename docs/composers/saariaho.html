Born 14 October 1952, Helsinki, FinlandDied 2 June 2023, Paris, France

68.1Life

Kaija Saariaho was born Kaija Anneli Laakkonen in Helsinki in 1952. Before entering the composition class at the Sibelius Academy she studied visual art, an education still audible in titles concerned with light, translucency and changes of surface. In Lichtbogen, the northern lights suggested a kind of continuous transformation; the notes themselves came partly from computer analysis of a changing cello sound. Her mature technique joins these two kinds of observation: how something changes before the senses, and how a musician can construct that change.

At the Sibelius Academy, where she studied with Paavo Heininen from 1976, Saariaho entered a Finnish new-music community that included Magnus Lindberg and Esa-Pekka Salonen. She helped establish the Korvat auki! — Open Your Ears! — group in 1977. Its concerts and discussions challenged the restricted place of contemporary music in Finnish institutions. Further study with Klaus Huber and Brian Ferneyhough in Freiburg sharpened her command of notation and instrumental technique. Her music increasingly brought gradual changes within sustained sounds to the foreground, diverging from Ferneyhough’s density of simultaneous rhythmic information.

Helsinki to ParisInside the sustained note

SpectrumA musical tone contains partials at different frequencies and strengths. Their distribution helps distinguish a cello from a flute playing the same fundamental. Spectral composition can turn those internal frequencies into notes for an ensemble.

Hearing Gérard Grisey and Tristan Murail at Darmstadt in 1980 offered an alternative to organising music principally through ordered pitch classes. Spectral music treated the constitution of a sound as compositional evidence: the strength of its partials, its attack, its instability, its decay. Saariaho also valued Ligeti’s ability to make many individual lines behave as one changing texture. These influences opened new ways of thinking about harmony: a chord could be understood as an enlarged timbre, and a change of timbre could perform some of the work previously assigned to a chord progression.

She moved to Paris in 1982 to work at IRCAM, the research institute founded by Boulez. There she met the composer and researcher Jean-Baptiste Barrière, who became her husband and a continuing collaborator. Computers allowed her to analyse instrumental recordings, calculate transformations and extend acoustic sounds electronically. In Lichtbogen (1985–86), a cello harmonic subjected to increasing bow pressure supplied a model for harmony. What begins as a relatively clear pitch becomes unstable and noisy; the ensemble magnifies stages of that change.

The instrumental experiments depended on particular players. With flautist Camilla Hoitenga, Saariaho explored the passage from breath through speech to fully sounded notes; with cellist Anssi Karttunen, the changing proportions of fundamental, harmonics and friction in a bowed string. NoaNoa and Près, both from 1992, place those instruments beside electronics that preserve, transform or prolong their sounds. A listener can lose track of where the live note ends. Saariaho composes this uncertainty by enlarging the player’s physical gesture through the electronic part.

Orchestra and voiceFrom a spectrum to a character

Pitch, noise and loudnessA quiet breath sound may contain little stable pitch; a very loud trumpet note may remain clearly harmonic. Saariaho’s pitch–noise axis and her soft–loud axis are related but independent compositional controls.

Du cristal (1989) and …à la fumée (1990) transfer these procedures to the orchestra. The first concentrates on the orchestral body; the second draws out solo alto flute and cello, electronically treated, from that body. The diptych’s titles — from crystal to smoke — describe different states of related material. Harmonic density, register and the degree of instrumental fusion carry the argument. Individual lines can emerge from a crowded chord and then become indistinguishable from it, a reversal of the usual concerto relationship between soloist and accompaniment.

The violin concerto Graal théâtre, completed in 1994 and premiered by Gidon Kremer with Salonen in 1995, dispenses with electronics. Its title comes from Jacques Roubaud’s writing on the Grail legends. The exposed violin brings a more continuous, recognisably soloistic line into contact with her orchestral transformations. Two movements, Delicato and Impetuoso, organise sharply different conditions of motion. The expressive range of this concerto helps explain the move towards opera.

L’Amour de loin received its premiere at Salzburg in 2000, conducted by Kent Nagano and staged by Peter Sellars. Amin Maalouf’s libretto adapts the medieval legend of Jaufre Rudel, a troubadour who loves a countess he has never seen. The three principal voices — baritone Jaufré, soprano Clémence and the mezzo-soprano Pilgrim — inhabit different relations to distance. The Pilgrim alone travels between the two shores until Jaufré attempts the crossing. Orchestral and electronic transitions give the sea a duration that cannot be compressed into stage action; the lovers’ meeting arrives only at the end, as Jaufré dies.

A biography made from songsRudel’s medieval vida offers a legendary life. Its story appears to turn the poems’ repeated phrase amor de lonh, love from afar, into a literal voyage and deathbed meeting.

Maalouf and Sellars remained collaborators in Adriana Mater, premiered in Paris in 2006, and La Passion de Simone, first heard in Vienna that year. The former places pregnancy after rape and a son’s prospective revenge at the centre of an opera; the latter approaches Simone Weil through fifteen sections for soprano, chorus, orchestra and electronics. In Émilie, premiered in Lyon in 2010, a single soprano embodies the mathematician and physicist Émilie du Châtelet. These works attach the finely differentiated vocal colours of the earlier music to decisions, memories and arguments carried by individual women.

The last theatreDifferent ways of telling

Innocence, completed in 2018 and premiered at Aix-en-Provence in 2021, changes the scale of that theatre. Sofi Oksanen’s Finnish libretto, adapted into several languages by Aleksi Barrière, connects a wedding with the aftermath of a fictional school shooting. Operatic singing, spoken testimony and the Finnish folk-singing of Markéta, a murdered pupil, distinguish intersecting times and communities. The orchestra can connect these accounts without making their speakers agree. Where L’Amour de loin delays a meeting, Innocence progressively discloses connections that its characters have tried to conceal.

Saariaho received a brain-cancer diagnosis in 2021 and continued composing. Her final completed work, HUSH, is a trumpet concerto for Verneri Pohjola: an instrument capable of both sharply defined attacks and air-filled, unstable sounds returns to the problem that occupied the early flute and cello pieces. She died at home in Paris on 2 June 2023. Pohjola gave the concerto’s first performance in Helsinki that August, with Susanna Mälkki conducting the Finnish Radio Symphony Orchestra. Its four movements take their titles from Aleksi Barrière’s text Not a Knight; the third movement’s mechanical rhythms derive from the MRI scans she underwent during her illness.

68.2The Sound

How the music is built — harmony, line, rhythm, colour, form.

♮Harmony

A chord can be derived from the internal frequencies of a recorded sound, then filtered, compressed or spread across registers. The harmonic series is one resource among several. In Lichtbogen, the transition from a cello harmonic towards a noisy multiphonic gives harmony its direction.

𝄞Timbre

Bow position and pressure, flute breath, harmonics and whispered consonants are structural variables. A sound can move towards greater pitch clarity while remaining on approximately the same note. Saariaho distinguishes this pitch–noise continuum from loudness: an abrasive texture can be quiet, and a pure one loud.

𝄐Electronics

Reverberation, delay and transformation extend the acoustic instrument’s duration and spectrum. In Près, the cello is both a live source and material for its electronic surroundings. Graal théâtre shows that the same thinking can operate without electronics, through orchestral register, doubling and articulation alone.

𝄋Time & form

Slow processes may contain very active surfaces: tremolos, trills and minute changes of density. Du cristal and …à la fumée make the relationship between a concentrated orchestral mass and dispersing solo lines into a two-part form. Local activity and harmonic change can proceed at different speeds.

𝅘𝅥Voice & theatre

Speech, breath and sustained singing belong to a continuous vocal vocabulary, with characters drawing on different parts of it. The mixed modes of delivery in Innocence make differences of language, age and remembered experience audible. In L’Amour de loin, long vocal lines are supported by an orchestra whose changes can be slower than a sentence.

68.3Works

A listening path, not a complete catalogue.

  1. 1982
    Laconisme de l’aileFlute, with optional electronics
    The player moves between spoken words from Saint-John Perse’s Oiseaux, breath and flute tone. A compact introduction to the border between articulation and pitch.
  2. 1985–86
    LichtbogenNine instruments and live electronics
    Flute, percussion, piano, harp and five strings. Analysis of changing cello sounds informs the harmony; the title refers to the northern lights.
  3. 1987
    NymphéaString quartet and live electronics
    Written for the Kronos Quartet. Harmonics, friction and electronically extended resonance complicate the distinction between a sustained chord and a sound with an interior motion.
  4. 1989–90
    Du cristal …à la fuméeOrchestra; alto flute, cello and electronics in the second work
    Two linked works, also performable separately. …à la fumée extracts solo voices from the orchestral material of Du cristal.
  5. 1994
    Graal théâtreViolin and orchestra; chamber-orchestra version 1997
    Two movements, Delicato and Impetuoso. Premiered at the 1995 Proms by Gidon Kremer, the BBC Symphony Orchestra and Esa-Pekka Salonen; no electronics.
  6. 1999–2000
    L’Amour de loinOpera in five acts; three soloists, chorus, orchestra and electronics
    Maalouf’s libretto turns the Rudel legend into a drama of imagined love and physical separation. The Pilgrim’s mezzo-soprano connects Jaufré’s baritone and Clémence’s soprano.
  7. 2002
    OrionOrchestra
    The three movements are Memento mori, Winter Sky and Hunter. Slow registral spaces give way to the final movement’s much faster instrumental motion.
  8. 2008
    Laterna magicaOrchestra
    Named after Ingmar Bergman’s autobiography. The changing speed of images in a magic lantern suggested the relation between rhythmic activity and the perception of continuity; premiered in Berlin in 2009.
  9. 2018
    InnocenceMultilingual opera; singers, actors and orchestra
    A wedding opens onto the concealed aftermath of a school shooting. Oksanen’s text and Aleksi Barrière’s multilingual dramaturgy separate present conversation from remembered testimony; premiered at Aix in 2021.
  10. 2023
    HUSHTrumpet and orchestra
    The final completed work, written for Verneri Pohjola and premiered after Saariaho’s death. Four movements examine the trumpet’s capacity for breath, muted colour and sustained melodic line.

68.5Sister Arts

Paintings and poems that share a world with this music.

Gallen-Kallela paints the Finnish lake as bands of reflection and disturbed water. The link is a shared physical environment, with no documented connection to a composition: Saariaho repeatedly discussed the bodily effect of Finland’s extreme seasonal changes of light. In this painting, small changes of surface divide a continuous field; in Lichtbogen, changes inside sustained sounds articulate a form. The painting predates her birth by forty-seven years.

Jaufre Rudel

Lanquan li jorn son lonc e may — first stanza

Twelfth century
OccitanLanquan li jorn son lonc e may M’es belhs dous chans d’auzelhs de lonh, E quan mi suy partitz de lay, Remembra’m d’un’ amor de lonh. Vau de talan embroncx e clis Si que chans ni flors d’albespis No-m valon plus que l’yverns gelatz.
Our translationWhen the days are long in May, I delight in the sweet song of distant birds; and when I have departed from there, I remember a love far away. I go bowed down and bent with longing, so that neither song nor hawthorn flowers are worth more to me than frozen winter.

The repeated de lonh is a formal device before it is a plot. Saariaho returned to Rudel in Lonh for soprano and electronics (1996), then in L’Amour de loin. This medieval lyric precedes Maalouf’s modern libretto; its manuscript tradition contains variant spellings and readings. The Occitan text supplies the stanza translated above.

Anonymous

Vida of Jaufre Rudel — opening

Thirteenth-century biographical tradition
OccitanJaufres Rudels de Blaia si fo mout gentils hom, princes de Blaia. Et enamoret se de la comtessa de Tripol, ses vezer, per lo ben qu’el n’auzi dire als pelerins que venguen d’Antiocha. E fez de leis mains vers ab bons sons, ab paubres motz.
Our translationJaufre Rudel of Blaye was a very noble man, prince of Blaye. He fell in love with the countess of Tripoli without seeing her, because of the good he heard spoken of her by pilgrims returning from Antioch. He made many verses about her, with good melodies and simple words.

The medieval prose biography continues with Rudel’s voyage, illness and death in the countess’s arms. Its author is separated from the troubadour by generations; the love story belongs to legend. Maalouf and Saariaho adopt this narrative as theatre, giving the unnamed countess the name Clémence and the travelling Pilgrim a central mediating role.

68.7Lineage

Saariaho
Passed toElectroacoustic chamber musicContemporary multilingual operaFinnish composition after spectralism

Saariaho learned independently from the spectral composers’ analysis of sound. Her collaboration with instrumentalists made the physical production of a sound as important as its computer representation. The sustained orchestral fields connect with Debussy and Ligeti; the later operas give those processes the timescale of speech, waiting and recollection.
