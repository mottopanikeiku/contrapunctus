Rachmaninoff in exile

The music he carried, the performances he left

Rachmaninoff left Russia in December 1917 and reached New York the following November. Follow the earlier music he carried into his performing life, through his own Edison and Victor records, and distinguish the dates of the works from the dates of the performances.

Begin when you are ready. The stations follow the music; pause whenever you want to read.
Station 01 / 09

Moscow, before the record

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

Three heavy notes, A–G♯–C♯, receive a quiet chordal answer. This is Rachmaninoff playing his own C♯ minor Prelude for Edison in 1919, not a recording from his student years. The work belongs to 1892, the year he completed his composition studies at the Moscow Conservatory. He had come to Moscow in 1885 to study with Nikolai Zverev; later teachers included Alexander Siloti, Sergei Taneyev and Anton Arensky. Tchaikovsky admired the Prelude when Rachmaninoff presented his Op. 3 pieces to him in 1893. Keep the two dates apart: a young composer’s score, heard through the hands of the emigrant pianist.

Station 02 / 09

The Prelude travels first

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

The middle section exchanges the opening’s separated chords for moving triplets. When the opening material returns, the score needs four staves to accommodate its spread across the keyboard. The Prelude travelled internationally before Rachmaninoff’s permanent departure from Russia: Siloti played it on his 1898 tour of Europe, England and America. By 1900 it had helped establish its composer’s reputation abroad. Here, however, the expanding chordal return does not provide the final gesture. Let it give way to the quieter coda. The Edison disc preserves that withdrawal as well as the passages that made the piece a conspicuous concert item.

Station 03 / 09

Ivanovka: a song becomes piano music

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Lilacs, Op. 21 No. 5 (composer’s piano transcription)

Ivanovka was the Satin family’s estate near Tambov, where Rachmaninoff first spent the summer in 1890 and returned repeatedly before 1917. Lilacs began there as a song in 1902, setting words by Ekaterina Beketova; the piano transcription followed in 1913. Rachmaninoff is playing it here in a Victor recording from 27 December 1923. The melody is threaded through broken chords and triplets rather than carried by a singer. The transcription also adds pianistic decoration and a coda: it is not simply the song with its words removed. Hear the rising activity, the held trill and the subsequent retreat before the last flourish subsides.

Station 04 / 09

Beyond a single famous Prelude

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in B minor, Op. 32 No. 10
Lento

This B minor Prelude was composed in 1910, seven years before Rachmaninoff left Russia. The pianist now is La Pianista, recorded in 2010, not Rachmaninoff. Its thirteen-piece set, Op. 32, completed his coverage of all twenty-four major and minor keys alongside the ten preludes of Op. 23 and the single C♯ minor Prelude. The opening combines small groups of moving notes with sustained chords. Listen for the inner lines as well as the melody, then for the heavier, pesante writing that enlarges the texture. The catalogue has moved beyond one celebrated early piece without abandoning the contrast between quiet voicing and chordal weight.

Station 05 / 09

1917: a border, not a cadence

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in B minor, Op. 32 No. 10
Lento

Rachmaninoff left Russia with his wife and two daughters in December 1917, travelling through Finland to Sweden; the family then lived in Copenhagen before sailing for New York in November 1918. He did not return to Russia. The map records an itinerary, not a programme for the music: this Prelude had already existed for seven years. Its return of the opening material must not be made into a prediction of exile. Listen instead to what the score actually does after its central weight: moving decoration surrounds held notes, earlier gestures become recognisable, and the closing bass notes leave longer spaces before the sound disappears.

Station 06 / 09

America: Edison, then Victor

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

In America, concert playing became Rachmaninoff’s principal occupation. He made the Edison performance that opened this journey in 1919 and signed with Victor in 1920. Now the same Prelude returns in Victor’s electrical recording of 4 April 1928, made in New York: matrix BVE-25650, take 23. The comparison panel places these performances beside one another; neither the pianist’s identity nor the unchanged notes make them the same performance. Attend to the quiet opening answers, the change into central motion and the breadth of the later chords. The late withdrawal remains part of the design, but here we are hearing another occasion, another take and another recording process.

Station 07 / 09

The Concerto fits a disc side

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Piano Concerto No. 2, Op. 18
I. Moderato — opening 78-rpm side only

The Second Piano Concerto was completed in 1901 and dedicated to Nikolai Dahl, whose treatment had helped Rachmaninoff after the failure of his First Symphony. Its complete première took place in Moscow, with the composer at the piano and Siloti conducting. This is a later encounter: Rachmaninoff with Leopold Stokowski and the Philadelphia Orchestra, recorded at Philadelphia’s Academy of Music on 10 April 1929. We hear only the opening 78-rpm side of the first movement, not the whole Moderato. Follow the broad rise and the quieter continuation, but do not mistake the transfer’s stopping point for a composed ending. The work continues on another side.

Station 08 / 09

Senar: composing abroad

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Piano Concerto No. 2, Op. 18
II. Adagio sostenuto — opening 78-rpm side only

Exile did not end composition. Villa Senar, beside Lake Lucerne, was Rachmaninoff’s summer home from 1933 to 1939; there he wrote the Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini in 1934. Its twenty-four variations use Paganini’s Caprice No. 24, and Variation VII introduces the Dies irae chant in the piano against fragments of Paganini’s theme. The influence panel documents that meeting. It is not what is playing: our contextual music is the opening disc side of the Second Concerto’s Adagio, again recorded by Rachmaninoff, Stokowski and the Philadelphia Orchestra in 1929. Follow its long lines through the smaller moving notes; this side, too, stops before the movement does.

A thread of influence
documented · 1934

The death chant interrupts Paganini

Variation VII of the Paganini Rhapsody brings Dies irae into a work nominally based on a violin caprice. The piano’s chant meets fragments of Paganini’s theme in bassoons and pizzicato cellos, letting the listener hear two inherited materials interacting rather than treating the rhapsody as twenty-four independent decorations.

Station 09 / 09

One performance, not a lost country

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Lilacs, Op. 21 No. 5 (composer’s piano transcription)

Rachmaninoff died at his Beverly Hills home in March 1943, without returning to Russia. To close, we return deliberately to the complete 1923 Lilacs performance, rather than leaving the Concerto suspended at a side change. Victor’s register lists twelve takes of this piece across sessions from 1920 to 1923; the recording here is take 9, made in Camden on 27 December 1923. That specificity matters: a record preserves one performance, not every choice its player ever made. The Ivanovka photograph, the pre-war transcription and the American recording belong to different moments. Hear the melody carried through its accompaniment once more, then let the final flourish and quiet chord finish the piece.
