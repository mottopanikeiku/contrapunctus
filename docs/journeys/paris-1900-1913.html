Paris, 1900–1913

Quiet piano music, paintings of water and dance

Satie set a melody over two quiet piano chords; twenty-five years later, Stravinsky wrote music in which the orchestra stamps. Between them came Debussy’s sea and clouds, Ravel’s birds and plucked strings, and the dancers who brought the Ballets Russes to Paris.

Station 01 / 09

Satie and the unresolved note

Erik Satie · Trois Gymnopédies
I. Lent et douloureux

Satie played the piano at the Chat Noir cabaret, then at the Auberge du Clou, where he met Debussy. His first Gymnopédie, written in 1888, opens with a bass note on the first beat and a soft chord on the second. G and D alternate below. The melody enters after four bars, and its long F♯ is held over the changing chords. Later, a low E is held while the harmony moves beneath it. The first ending closes on D major before the music starts again.

Station 02 / 09

Gongs in Paris; bells at the piano

Claude Debussy · Préludes, Book I
X. La cathédrale engloutie

At the 1889 Paris Exposition, Debussy heard Javanese gamelan, with gongs sounding beneath quicker instrumental patterns. In the Revue musicale S.I.M. in 1913, he wrote that its counterpoint made Palestrina’s seem like “child’s play”. His Pagodes of 1903 had already brought gong-like bass notes to the piano. Here Marcelle Meyer plays La cathédrale engloutie, published in 1910. Bare fifths rise slowly, with long spaces between them in which the notes ring. The left hand then begins rolling figures beneath the ascending chords, which represent the cathedral of Breton legend rising from the sea.

Influence
documented · 1889

Debussy and the gamelan in Paris

At the Paris Exposition of 1889, Debussy heard a Javanese gamelan. Features of it appear in the piano piece Pagodes, which has a gong-like bass beneath a pentatonic tune, with faster notes above. He later wrote that Javanese music “obeys laws of counterpoint which make Palestrina seem like child’s play”.

Station 03 / 09

Flute, harp and orange sun

Claude Debussy · Nocturnes
I. Nuages — Modéré

Paris first heard Nuages in December 1900. In the middle of the piece, flute and harp share a five-note melody over soft held strings. The cor anglais then returns with its earlier narrow phrase. Monet painted Impression, soleil levant from a hotel window in Le Havre in 1872. Dark boats are placed close to the viewer; the orange sun and its reflection stand out against the mist. Farther back, cranes and masts are barely visible in the morning haze.

Station 04 / 09

Ravel’s writing for strings

Maurice Ravel · String Quartet in F major
II. Assez vif — Très rythmé

Ravel finished his String Quartet in April 1903 and dedicated it to his teacher, Fauré. The scherzo opens pizzicato, with both violins, viola and cello plucking their notes. A bowed melody then enters in a long phrase over the continuing pizzicato. Debussy took an interest in how the four parts sat together. Writing to Ravel before the première in March 1904, he suggested a small change to the playing, that the viola could be a little quieter.

Station 05 / 09

Hokusai’s wave on the cover of Debussy’s score

Claude Debussy · La Mer
I. De l’aube à midi sur la mer

The United States Air Force Band plays the opening of La Mer in Lawrence Odom’s concert-band transcription. A low B is held beneath the opening; short phrases enter above it, one at a time. Later, groups of two and three notes cross one another. When Durand published the score in 1905, its cover carried an adapted detail of Hokusai’s wave. In the complete print, narrow boats lie under the curling crest, and Mount Fuji stands far behind them, beyond the reach of the water.

Station 06 / 09

Birds in summer heat

Maurice Ravel · Miroirs
II. Oiseaux tristes — Très lent

Ravel described the birds of Oiseaux tristes as lost in a dark forest during the hottest hours of summer. The piano begins high and alone, with a repeated B♭ and a short flourish. Beneath the next calls, the left hand moves in threes while the right hand divides its beat in two. A louder call is followed by a quiet answer. Ravel dedicated the piece to Ricardo Viñes, who first played Miroirs at the Salle Érard on 6 January 1906. When the opening call returns in D minor, held chords sound beneath it.

Station 07 / 09

Karsavina as the bird

Igor Stravinsky · The Firebird
Infernal Dance of Kashchei

On 25 June 1910, Tamara Karsavina danced The Firebird at the Paris Opéra, with choreography by Michel Fokine for Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes. In the Infernal Dance, the bird forces Kashchei and his creatures to dance. The United States Marine Band plays Thomas Knox’s arrangement for concert band. Short figures cut across the pulse, and the accents are displaced. Bakst’s costume design shows the bird on paper, before a dancer wore it. A longer melody then extends across the beat, while Kashchei remains under her spell.

Station 08 / 09

Bassoon and orchestra

Igor Stravinsky · The Rite of Spring
Part I. Adoration of the Earth

On 29 May 1913, Pierre Monteux conducted the première of The Rite of Spring at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées. Nijinsky had choreographed the ballet for Diaghilev’s company. The music begins with a bassoon high in its range, playing a melody drawn from a Lithuanian folk-song collection. Other wind instruments enter around it, each with a separate line. In this recording Stravinsky conducts the Columbia Symphony Orchestra, in January 1960. Near the end of the introduction the wind parts thin out and the opening bassoon tune returns, briefly exposed, before the stamping begins.

Station 09 / 09

Stamping strings and horn strikes

Igor Stravinsky · The Rite of Spring
Part I. Adoration of the Earth

The strings repeat the same chord at the opening of Augurs of Spring, F♭ major against an E♭ dominant seventh. Horns play selected attacks harder. The opening bars keep a steady 2/4 while the accents shift, with unaccented strokes between them. In a letter to Nicholas Roerich on 26 September 1911, Stravinsky described an old woman in squirrel fur running in front of the dancers, stopping and interrupting their rhythm. He had her in mind while composing this dance. Around the repeated chord, shorter phrases pass across the orchestra, and the horns enter again.
