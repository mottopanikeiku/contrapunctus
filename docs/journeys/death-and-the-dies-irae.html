Death and the Dies irae

One chant, different kinds of musical inheritance

Berlioz makes a funeral chant dance; Liszt and Saint-Saëns transform it, while Verdi sets its words without borrowing its tune. Follow that distinction into Rachmaninoff’s inherited material: unavailable quotation recordings are discussed through sourced influence panels, with contextual music identified explicitly.

Begin when you are ready. The stations follow the music; pause whenever you want to read.
Station 01 / 08

First distinguish the chant

Gregorian chant · Pange lingua gloriosi corporis mysterium
Traditional plainchant hymn

The recording is Pange lingua, not Dies irae: Gareth Hughes sings the Eucharistic hymn unaccompanied. Use its single melodic line to hear what chant can do without a separate bass or chords. The different melody we will follow appears in the Dies irae thread: its opening eight notes are F–E–F–D–E–C–D–D in the Vatican Graduale version. Retain the down–up–lower contour, not an imagined regular beat; the thread’s equal note lengths are a display aid. Bruegel’s painted army of death supplies a visual comparison only. Neither this hymn nor this painting is presented as a source for Berlioz’s quotation.

Station 02 / 08

Berlioz brings the funeral indoors

Hector Berlioz · Symphonie fantastique, Op. 14
V. Songe d’une nuit du Sabbat

Now the actual Dies irae quotation approaches. In the finale of the 1830 Symphonie fantastique, the artist dreams of a gathering for his own funeral. Berlioz’s programme names a funeral knell and a parody of the chant. Bells frame the broad low statement, written from bar 127 for four bassoons and two ophicleides, the keyed brass instruments specified in the score. Their historical scoring does not identify the instruments used in this recording. Listen for the contour retained across slower and quicker note values: the melody’s recognisability makes it possible to change its ceremonial character without replacing it.

A thread of influence
inferred · 1849

From Berlioz’s Witches’ Sabbath to Liszt’s Totentanz

In the finale of the Symphonie fantastique, Berlioz gives the Dies irae chant to four bassoons and two ophicleides in C minor from bar 127, a funeral rite turned into a parody at the witches’ sabbath. Liszt met Berlioz the day before the premiere on 5 December 1830 and in 1833 made the piano transcription of the symphony. His Totentanz, planned in 1838, completed in 1849 and revised in 1853 and 1859, is a set of variations for piano and orchestra on the same chant. A second recorded source is the Triumph of Death fresco in the Campo Santo at Pisa, which Liszt saw in 1838.

Both works are documented; no letter or statement has been found in which Liszt names the Fantastique as the source of the Dies irae idea. He knew the symphony intimately, but the Pisa fresco is the other recorded stimulus for the Totentanz.Follow this connection →

Station 03 / 08

Learn the second melody separately

Hector Berlioz · Symphonie fantastique, Op. 14
V. Songe d’une nuit du Sabbat

The witches’ round is not another name for the chant. It introduces a different tune, developed through imitating entries: one line continues while another takes up the material. Berlioz’s programme eventually asks for the round and Dies irae together, so hearing their identities separately prepares the later collision. Follow an entry through the accumulating texture instead of treating the orchestra as a single block. The quotation is doing more than labelling a scene as frightening. It gives Berlioz a slow, recognisable shape that can remain audible against faster material; counterpoint makes two kinds of musical time coexist.

Station 04 / 08

Liszt knows the score

Franz Liszt · Piano Sonata in B minor, S. 178

This is the opening of Liszt’s B-minor Sonata, not Totentanz; no chant quotation is claimed in the passage. Its contrasting scales, leaps and repeated notes let us hear Liszt establishing material for transformation. His knowledge of Berlioz was concrete: he attended the Fantastique premiere and transcribed the symphony in 1833. His Totentanz makes variations for piano and orchestra from Dies irae. The influence panel carefully marks a direct Berlioz-to-Totentanz stimulus as inferred, not proved by a letter; the Pisa Triumph of Death fresco is another reported stimulus. Knowing the shared chant does not settle the exact route by which an idea arrived.

Station 05 / 08

Saint-Saëns makes death dance too

Hector Berlioz · Symphonie fantastique, Op. 14
V. Songe d’une nuit du Sabbat

Return to Berlioz’s finale for the promised combination; the recording remains Berlioz, not Saint-Saëns. The broad chant and quicker round now occupy the same orchestral space. Saint-Saëns’s Danse macabre, composed in 1874 from an earlier song on Henri Cazalis’s poem, offers a related but distinct transformation: its woodwinds quote Dies irae, while a solo violin represents Death’s fiddle and the xylophone evokes bones. Those details belong to that score, not to the sound beneath this paragraph. Here, listen instead for Berlioz’s slower borrowed contour against the active dance, then the dry string colour of col legno, bow wood striking strings.

Station 06 / 08

Verdi inherits words, not this tune

Giuseppe Verdi · Messa da Requiem
Dies irae — opening number of the sequence

The title Dies irae does not always announce the melody we heard in Berlioz. Verdi sets the medieval Latin words to his own music. Four separated orchestral blows precede the choral attack; later, repeated quiet syllables on ‘Quantus tremor’ — ‘how great the trembling’ — make the collective voice vulnerable. This is inheritance through a text rather than quotation of the familiar plainchant tune. The distinction can be heard, not merely catalogued: try recalling Berlioz’s broad contour against Verdi’s declamation. This recording contains only the opening number of the sequence and stops before Tuba mirum; its endpoint is not the Requiem’s conclusion.

Station 07 / 08

Rachmaninoff’s picture and borrowed notes

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

Rachmaninoff’s Isle of the Dead brings together two different inheritances: a black-and-white reproduction of Böcklin’s painting, seen in 1907, and fragments and fuller statements of Dies irae. The picture supplies a visual stimulus; the chant supplies notes. The music playing now is explicitly contextual: Rachmaninoff’s own 1919 recording of the C♯-minor Prelude, not the symphonic poem and not a demonstrated quotation of the chant. Its opening A–G♯–C♯ signal belongs to the Prelude’s own design. Keep it distinct from F–E–F–D as the middle section gathers motion; a sombre association alone is not evidence of borrowing.

Station 08 / 08

Two inheritances meet Paganini

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

In variation VII of the 1934 Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini, Rachmaninoff puts Dies irae in the piano against fragments of Paganini’s theme in bassoons and pizzicato cellos. Like Berlioz’s combination, this is an interaction between identifiable materials, not simply a title about death. The influence panel documents that passage; we remain with the Prelude as contextual listening, not an unheard Rhapsody excerpt. Its own opening now returns across four staves before the quieter coda. Distinguish this enlargement of a composer’s material from a borrowed chant, just as Verdi’s inherited words differed from Berlioz’s inherited melody.
