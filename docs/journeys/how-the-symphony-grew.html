How the symphony grew

Haydn’s sudden chord to Mahler’s last few strings

At the Hanover Square Rooms in 1792, Haydn startled London with a single chord. A little over a century later, the last page of Mahler’s Ninth needs only strings, played softly.

Station 01 / 08

Haydn’s surprise

Joseph Haydn · Symphony No. 94 “Surprise”
II. Andante

London, 23 March 1792. At Salomon’s concert in the Hanover Square Rooms, Haydn’s new symphony had the strings play a short, plain tune. They repeat it more softly. Then the whole orchestra plays one fortissimo chord, with timpani. Haydn wrote the symphony during his first London visit. The slow movement sets its C-major tune as a series of variations. In the first variation the second violins and violas keep the melody while the first violins play semiquavers around it.

Station 02 / 08

Mozart’s return by a different route

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart · Symphony No. 40, K. 550
I. Molto allegro

Three years before Haydn’s London visit, Mozart wrote his G-minor Symphony, K. 550. It opens with a repeated figure in the violins. In the development, fragments of it pass between strings and winds, and each repetition moves into a less stable key. Mozart then brings the opening back in G minor. The recapitulation is longer than the exposition because the bridge between the themes modulates further. The second theme, first heard in B♭ major, returns in G minor, with semitone clashes in the woodwind and strings.

Station 03 / 08

Mozart: five themes in combination

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart · Symphony No. 41 in C major, K. 551 “Jupiter”
IV. Finale: Molto allegro

Mozart wrote the Jupiter Symphony in the summer of 1788, the same summer as the G-minor. In the finale the four-note tune C–D–F–E keeps its long notes while quicker figures pass it, and one part can begin before another has finished. The return to C major is followed by further development. In the coda five themes are combined, each keeping its own rhythm as they pass between the instruments. Trumpets and timpani join the final C-major chords.

Station 04 / 08

Beethoven: a pause for an oboe

Ludwig van Beethoven · Symphony No. 5, Op. 67
I. Allegro con brio

The Fifth’s opening rhythm continues to work long after its first statement. In the development it passes between instruments and through changing keys, and the exchanges eventually shrink to isolated chords, with silence between the replies. When the opening returns at bar 248, winds and brass reinforce it. At bar 268 the orchestra stops, leaving an oboe alone in an Adagio. Later, bassoons take the call previously played by horns, and the second theme returns in C major, a minor third below its earlier E♭ major.

Station 05 / 08

Brahms’s greeting from the mountains

Johannes Brahms · Symphony No. 1 in C minor, Op. 68
IV. Adagio — Più andante — Allegro non troppo, ma con brio — Più allegro

In 1868 Brahms sent Clara Schumann a melody from the Bernese Alps, with the words: “High on the mountain, deep in the valley, I greet you many thousand times.” Eight years later a horn plays a version of that melody in the finale of his First Symphony, over tremolo strings; flutes then take it over. Trombones and bassoons follow with a short chorale. All this comes before the long string theme of the main Allegro. The chorale returns, played loud, in the coda.

Brahms’s finale theme and Beethoven’s Ode to Joy

The strings’ broad C-major tune in the finale of Brahms’s First Symphony recalls the Ode to Joy from Beethoven’s Ninth. Felix Otto Dessoff conducted the première at Karlsruhe on 4 November 1876. The following year Hans von Bülow called it “Beethoven’s Tenth”.

Station 06 / 08

Bruckner and the string writing

Anton Bruckner · Symphony No. 7 in E major, WAB 107
III. Scherzo — Sehr schnell; Trio — Etwas langsamer

A short repeated figure in the strings opens the scherzo of the Seventh, in A minor. Over it a trumpet plays a call and the clarinet answers; this exchange supplies much of the material for a movement built on large orchestral crescendos. Bruckner returns repeatedly to the same short figures, moves them between instruments and builds them into long crescendos. Between the loud passages the brass play quietly: trombones and tuba join in a soft chorale while the strings continue to move. Another crescendo begins before the excerpt ends. After it comes the slower trio in F major, in which quiet timpani introduce a melody for the violins.

Station 07 / 08

Tchaikovsky’s fading basses

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky · Symphony No. 6 in B minor “Pathétique”, Op. 74
IV. Finale — Adagio lamentoso

In Klin on 23 February 1893, Tchaikovsky told his nephew Vladimir Davydov that the Sixth would end with “the most drawn-out Adagio”. He was delighted with the new symphony. Here, after the low-brass chorale, a melody first heard in D major returns in B minor and rises to one last climax. The orchestra then drops out until only the low strings remain, and the double basses repeat B as the texture thins. Their pulse grows softer until it is barely audible.

Station 08 / 08

Mahler and silence in the symphony

Gustav Mahler · Symphony No. 9
IV. Adagio — Sehr langsam und noch zurückhaltend

The last page of Mahler’s Ninth begins with muted second violins. The marking is Adagissimo, slower still. Only strings remain, and the long melodies of the finale have broken into phrases of a few notes, separated by rests. One note is held after the others stop, and another, played softly, follows it. A viola plays a small upward turn inside the last D♭-major chord.
