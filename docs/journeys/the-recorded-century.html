The recorded century

Great performers at the horn and the microphone

In Milan in 1902, Caruso and a pianist sent <i>Vesti la giubba</i> into a recording horn. In the 1920s, microphones were catching Rachmaninoff’s piano and Chaliapin’s bass voice, while the records preserved every lingering phrase and sudden rush.

Station 01 / 09

Caruso and Cottone, Milan

Ruggero Leoncavallo · Pagliacci
Vesti la giubba — piano-accompanied side

Milan, 30 November 1902: Enrico Caruso sings Vesti la giubba with Salvatore Cottone at the piano. The two men have a horn to satisfy. Sound entering it vibrates a diaphragm, which moves the stylus cutting the groove; their voices and instruments supply the energy themselves. In acoustic studios, distance from the horn can decide whether an accompaniment survives or overwhelms the singer. Here Cottone’s piano makes separate, percussive sounds beneath Caruso’s sustained tenor. Leoncavallo’s clown Canio has to sing while his heart is breaking. The piano supplies his entire theatre.

Station 02 / 09

An orchestra behind the clown

Ruggero Leoncavallo · Pagliacci
Recitar!… Vesti la giubba

Now an orchestra surrounds Caruso. On this Victor side the instruments can sustain a phrase beneath his voice, where Cottone’s piano had to strike each note and let it fade. Canio begins with the recitative’s stunned outburst, then orders himself into his costume. In Leoncavallo’s Pagliacci, the audience has paid and expects to laugh; the performer must turn his own tears into the clown’s grimaces. Caruso’s long vowels stretch that impossible command into song. The orchestra holds the scene open around him as the melody broadens.

Station 03 / 09

Beethoven meets the side limit

Ludwig van Beethoven · Symphony No. 5 in C minor, Op. 67
I. Allegro con brio

On 10 November 1913, Nikisch and the Berlin Philharmonic recorded Beethoven’s Fifth. Getting an orchestra into a groove required ingenuity. Stroh string instruments, fitted with aluminium horns, reinforced the ordinary strings and aimed their sound towards the recording horn. Nikisch also had to fit the music on the disc. At his broad pace, the first movement’s exposition repeat would have overrun the first side, so he left it out. Beethoven’s opening argument passes once, then presses onwards. The conductor can linger over a phrase; the groove is running out all the same.

Station 04 / 09

Paderewski’s hands take their time

Frédéric Chopin · Nocturne in F♯ major, Op. 15 No. 2
Larghetto

Paderewski recorded Chopin’s F♯ major Nocturne in New York on 14 May 1917. His hands often arrive separately: the accompaniment sounds, then the melody seems to lean into it. Chords can unfold from bottom to top. In his essay for Henry T. Finck’s Success in Music and How It Is Won (1909), Paderewski rejected, for soloists, the rule that time borrowed in one phrase must be paid back in another: “What is lost is lost.” Chopin’s doppio movimento brings a larger change, doubling the pace for the middle section.

Station 05 / 09

Rachmaninoff for Edison

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

Rachmaninoff sat down for Edison in 1919 and recorded the Prelude that had followed him from Russia. The resulting Diamond Disc reached buyers in December 1920. We enter at the Agitato, where triplets carry the tune towards the huge return of the opening chords. The score spreads that return across four staves, though only two hands are playing. It is an extravagant amount of piano to fit on a small disc. Rachmaninoff then lets the chords subside into the quiet coda, leaving the last sounds widely spaced.

Station 06 / 09

A second sitting for the Prelude

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

Two years later, Rachmaninoff made the Prelude again for Victor. The studio still recorded through a horn. His quick central passage arrives after the quiet opening and drives towards the familiar chords, now caught on another day by another company. He can press the triplets forwards and still give the returning chords ample room. In this performance the great return is already close when our excerpt begins. Beyond it, the coda draws the hands apart into quiet.

Station 07 / 09

The microphone takes over

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

By the time Rachmaninoff returned to the Prelude in New York on 4 April 1928, Victor had changed its machinery. A microphone turned the piano’s sound into an electrical signal; amplification supplied the energy to drive the cutting stylus. Victor and Columbia had introduced the process commercially in 1925, capturing a wider range of sound. Rachmaninoff’s issued performance was take 23. The restless triplets and massive chords belong to a piece he had written thirty-six years earlier. At the end, the new apparatus catches the old Prelude withdrawing into its quiet coda.

Station 08 / 09

Chaliapin’s prophet at the horn

Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov · The Prophet, Op. 49 No. 2

Hayes, 10 October 1921: Chaliapin records Rimsky-Korsakov’s The Prophet, with Percy Pitt conducting the orchestra. Pushkin’s 1826 poem begins with a thirsty wanderer in a dark desert. A six-winged angel opens his eyes and ears, tears out his tongue and replaces his heart with a burning coal. Chaliapin’s bass has to tell this terrible story before it can deliver God’s command. The opening is measured, almost spoken; the accompaniment starts to flow around the voice. At the end the score demands con forza e maestoso, forcefully and majestically. The wanderer has become a prophet.

Station 09 / 09

The prophet returns to the studio

Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov · The Prophet, Op. 49 No. 2

On 20 October 1927 Chaliapin recorded The Prophet in London with Albert Coates. Six years separate this electrical performance from the acoustic side with Percy Pitt. The same bass voice tells Pushkin’s story again, taking more time over the whole span. Quiet orchestral passages leave room between the great vocal declarations. When God finally speaks, the song turns outwards: the remade man must cross seas and lands, setting human hearts alight with his words. Chaliapin carries that command above the busy accompaniment. After his last syllable, the instruments keep running towards the final chords.
