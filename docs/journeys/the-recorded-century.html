The recorded century

What the horn demanded, what the microphone changed

Recording changed the work of performing: acoustic studios organised players around a horn, while electrical recording replaced direct mechanical capture with a microphone and amplification. These nine stations follow Caruso, Nikisch, Paderewski, Rachmaninoff and Chaliapin across that transition, using the published comparison pages to revisit corresponding phrases. Different accompaniments, omitted repeats, uncertain takes and modern transfers remain part of the evidence—not defects to conceal when comparing performances.

Begin when you are ready. The stations follow the music; pause whenever you want to read.
Station 01 / 09

Caruso: a voice meets the horn

Ruggero Leoncavallo · Pagliacci
Vesti la giubba — piano-accompanied side

In acoustic recording, sound entering a horn vibrated a diaphragm attached to the cutting stylus: the performers’ sound energy made the groove, without a microphone. Balance therefore depended on position and projection; singers could be directed to turn or step back during louder passages. Those are documented studio practices, not movements we can reconstruct for Caruso from this transfer. He recorded this piano-accompanied Vesti la giubba with Salvatore Cottone in Milan on 30 November 1902. Follow the sustained tenor line across the piano’s separate attacks. The complete short side is a performance for a recording apparatus, not an unmediated seat in an opera house.

The same tenor, another accompaniment

Ruggero Leoncavallo · Pagliacci
Recitar!… Vesti la giubba

The orchestral side changes more than the background colour: sustained accompaniment now frames the same tenor’s phrases. Its source attributes Victor 88061 to Caruso’s acoustic performance of 17 March 1907, but the transferred disc’s exact master has not been confirmed from a legible runout. Keep that qualification beside the date. The comparison maps only 0:05–1:54 of the piano side to 0:07–2:19 here; neither complete duration is a tempo measurement. Listen through the early relaxation and broad later crest, then use Compare within those shared bounds. A more prominent voice in one transfer would not, by itself, prove that Caruso sang more loudly.

Station 03 / 09

Nikisch: the orchestra must fit

Ludwig van Beethoven · Symphony No. 5 in C minor, Op. 67
I. Allegro con brio

Nikisch and the Berlin Philharmonic recorded Beethoven’s Fifth on 10 November 1913. Archival research places the performance within an acoustic practice that reinforced conventional strings with directional Stroh instruments; this was not the generic substitution of tubas for double basses. Space on a side also mattered: at Nikisch’s broad pace, taking the exposition repeat would have exceeded the first side’s capacity. He omits it. The comparison therefore matches his opening to the modern reference’s second exposition, beginning at 1:42.6. This is a concrete alteration of performed form, not merely a different recorded tone. Compare phrase breadth only after accounting for the music each performance actually includes.

Station 04 / 09

Paderewski: the hands need not coincide

Frédéric Chopin · Nocturne in F♯ major, Op. 15 No. 2
Larghetto

Paderewski’s 14 May 1917 recording of Chopin’s F♯-major Nocturne preserves a different sort of evidence: the relationship between melody and accompaniment. Jonathan Woolf describes hand dislocation and arpeggiation in these recordings—notes belonging together need not begin simultaneously. Paderewski’s own 1909 essay rejects the rule that every expressive delay must be mechanically repaid within the bar. Listen for independent melodic timing, but keep it distinct from Chopin’s written doppio movimento in the middle. Compare links this performance with Paderewski in 1937 and other pianists. The record lets us revisit an interpretive practice; its noise and equalisation are not themselves evidence of his touch.

Station 05 / 09

1919: the composer is not a metronome

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

Rachmaninoff’s 1919 Edison performance was issued on Diamond Disc 82187-R in December 1920. It is a sound recording of the pianist, not a reproducing-piano roll. We enter the Prelude at the agitated middle, then hear its broad chordal return and quiet coda. Remember the time taken by those three functions, not just the grain of the piano sound. This restored copy, credited to Adam Cuerden, serves as the comparison’s reference for the composer’s 1921 and 1928 remakes. Repeated recordings preserve different performances of the same score; ‘played by the composer’ identifies a performer, not a single immutable set of timings.

Station 06 / 09

1921: a remake without one secure take

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

The Victor remake is still acoustic, but it is not a duplicate of the Edison performance. Our map gives the Edison reference’s 1:46–2:18 central span a corresponding duration of about 28.8 seconds here. That provisional transfer measurement is a useful comparison of pacing, not a recovered studio metronome mark: historical playback speed remains uncertain. The disc identity has a second limit. DAHR lists two issued 1921 takes under Victor 814, and this transfer does not establish which it contains. Listen from the central change through the broader chords and final retreat. The catalogue narrows the event; it does not license an invented exact session date.

Station 07 / 09

1928: the microphone changes the capture

Sergei Rachmaninoff · Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

Columbia and Victor introduced electrical recording commercially in 1925, extending the tonal range captured by records. A microphone and amplification replaced the acoustic horn’s direct dependence on performers’ sound pressure to move the cutter. Rachmaninoff’s electrical Prelude is securely identified as take 23 of 4 April 1928. The same reference central span occupies about 31.2 seconds in its provisional comparison map, against 28.8 in the 1921 transfer. That difference concerns the reproduced performance, not a demonstrated effect of the microphone on his tempo. This digital copy has also been equalised, filtered and de-noised. Hear the later distribution of weight, without treating its apparent brightness as an untouched record of his piano.

Station 09 / 09

The microphone is not the only variable

Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov · The Prophet, Op. 49 No. 2

Chaliapin returned to The Prophet with Albert Coates on 20 October 1927, now in an electrical recording. The same song and singer give a comparison across the change of apparatus, but six years and another conductor separate the performances. This mapped musical span lasts 255 seconds against the earlier side’s 220; the complete file lengths also contain sound outside the comparison. Follow the broader crests and quieter intervals rather than explaining every difference by the microphone. Recording altered the conditions under which performers worked and preserved their choices for repeated scrutiny. It did not remove interpretation, recording balance or later transfer decisions from what we hear.
