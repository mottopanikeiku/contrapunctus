78 rpm

Also: 78s; 78 rpm disc; shellac disc

A 78 rpm record is a disc designed to turn at roughly seventy-eight revolutions per minute.

These discs were usually made from a shellac compound, and each side held only a few minutes. A symphony occupied several records, which had to be turned and changed as the music progressed. The Hall’s Koussevitzky recording of Haydn’s “Surprise” Andante originally filled two sides in Victor set M-55. Its digital transfer joins them into one movement.

recording

Acoustic recording

Also: acoustic recordings; electrical recording; electrical recordings

Acoustic recording uses a horn and diaphragm to move a cutting stylus directly, without microphones.

A singer faced the recording horn, and instrumentalists gathered close enough for their sound to reach it. The sound shook a diaphragm connected to a stylus, which cut the vibrations into a wax master. The system registered only a narrow band, roughly 350 to 3,000 Hz, and the need to crowd around one horn limited the size of ensembles. In the spring of 1925 Victor and Columbia began issuing records made with microphones; the range widened to about 100–⁠5,000 Hz, and orchestral recording grew rapidly. Edison’s first electrically recorded Diamond Discs went on sale in 1927. Rachmaninoff recorded his C♯ minor Prelude acoustically for Edison in 1919. Koussevitzky’s recording of the Andante from Haydn’s “Surprise”, made for Victor on 22 April 1929, is electrical.

The composer at the keyboard in 1919, in an acoustic recording on an Edison Diamond Disc. Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

An electrical recording: Koussevitzky and the Boston Symphony for Victor, 22 April 1929. Symphony No. 94 “Surprise”

Appoggiatura

Also: appoggiaturas

An appoggiatura is a non-chord note that delays a chord note, usually on a strong beat, and then resolves by step.

The Italian appoggiare means ‘to lean’. The melody moves to a note outside the chord and stresses it before moving by step to a chord note, which delays the point of rest inside a phrase. An F held over C–E–G and resolving to E is an example. A suspension is tied over from the previous chord; the leaning note is not. The long string phrases of the Adagietto of Mahler’s Fifth use such delayed resolutions.

The melody stresses notes outside the chord, then resolves to chord notes by step. Symphony No. 5

form

Aria

Also: arias

An aria is a substantial solo song in an opera, oratorio or cantata.

An aria gives a singer room to set out what a character feels or wants. Recitative carries the plot forward, and the aria stops it. A Baroque da capo aria returns to its opening section after a contrasting middle section, with scope for new ornaments. Puccini’s Sì, mi chiamano Mimì grows more freely out of conversation. In Act I of La bohème (Turin, 1 February 1896), Mimì answers Rodolfo’s account of his life as a poet with an account of her own. “Sola mi fo il pranzo da me stessa”, ‘I make my own dinner’, is set in a light 2/4 Allegretto moderato that halts on a held note at “in cielo”. At “ma quando vien lo sgelo”, ‘but when the thaw comes’, she stands, and the music broadens into common time and Andante molto sostenuto. “Il primo bacio dell’aprile”, the first kiss of April, rises to a D major fortissimo marked con grande espansione.

Mimì begins her answer. Short phrases close to speech sit beside sustained singing within one conversation. La bohème

Atonality

Also: atonal

Atonality is music without an established home key or the usual pull of dominant chords towards a tonic.

In atonal music, a phrase can be held together by a recurring interval or rhythm while chords move freely; dissonance is not expected to resolve. Schoenberg’s Second String Quartet, Op. 10, lies on the boundary between tonal and atonal writing. The first movement is in F♯ minor, the third, Litanei, is loosely in E♭ minor, and the finale, Entrückung, opens with an instrumental introduction that establishes no home key before the soprano enters with Stefan George’s “Ich fühle Luft von anderem Planeten”, ‘I feel the air of another planet’. The quartet ends on an F♯ major triad. At the first performance, in the Bösendorfer-⁠Saal in Vienna on 21 December 1908, with Marie Gutheil-⁠Schoder and the Rosé Quartet, one critic heard “a convocation of all the neighbourhood cats”; Schoenberg later recalled “riots which surpassed every previous and subsequent happenings of this kind”.

The introduction has no clear home key at first. Its chromatic lines do not cadence at once in the tonic. String Quartet No. 2, Op. 10

The harmony closes on an F♯ major triad, with A♯ and C♯ above F♯. The quartet began in F♯ minor. String Quartet No. 2, Op. 10

Attacca

Also: attacca subito

Attacca tells performers to continue straight into the next movement or section without a break.

The instruction means that the next movement follows without a pause. Mendelssohn joins the movements of his E minor Violin Concerto. A bassoon holds B after the first movement’s closing chord, then moves to C as the Andante begins. A short passage also links the slow movement to the finale.

Augmented sixth chord

Also: augmented sixth; German sixth; Italian sixth; French sixth

An augmented sixth chord contains two notes that usually move outwards by a semitone to an octave on the dominant.

In C major or minor, the defining notes are A♭ below and F♯ above. A♭ falls to G and F♯ rises to G, so the augmented sixth opens into an octave on the dominant. The Italian sixth adds C between them. The French sixth also adds D; the German sixth adds E♭. These inner notes change the sound of the chord, and the two outer notes still resolve to G. In Beethoven’s Bagatelle, Op. 119 No. 1, an Italian sixth falls on the third beat of bar 3, immediately before the dominant. A French sixth precedes the cadence at bar 14 of the finale of Mozart’s “Dissonance” Quartet, K. 465. A German sixth falls on the last beat of bar 7 in the third movement of Beethoven’s Op. 109 and leads to the half cadence at bar 8.

Bar

Also: bars; bar number; bar numbers

A bar is a span of music between bar lines, normally containing the number of beats shown by the time signature.

American musicians call it a measure. Bar numbers give players a shared address in the score. Repeats mean that one address can be visited twice. instrument

Basset clarinet

Also: basset clarinets

A basset clarinet extends the clarinet’s range downwards to written low C.

The extra length and keys add a major third below the ordinary clarinet’s lowest note, written E, down to written C. Mozart wrote his Clarinet Concerto for Anton Stadler’s extended instrument. When the concerto was published after his death, the low passages were transposed up an octave for an ordinary clarinet. Stadler lost the autograph, so it is not known exactly which notes were changed, and modern editors reconstruct the extended line. In the Hall’s Adagio recording, William McColl plays a basset clarinet he built himself.

Basso continuo

Also: continuo; figured bass; thoroughbass

Basso continuo is a bass line with chords supplied by a keyboard or plucked-string player, often joined by a bass instrument.

A harpsichordist, organist or lutenist builds chords above the written bass. Figures beneath the notes can specify the intervals: a 6 calls for a sixth above the bass, for instance. A cello, viol or bassoon may reinforce the line, and the keyboard’s realisation differs at each performance. Couperin’s La Piémontaise, from Les Nations (1726), gives two treble instruments this support; the Hall’s performance pairs violin and flute above viol and keyboard. In Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto the harpsichord has a written-out solo part as well as its continuo role, and Bach’s scoring calls it an obbligato harpsichord.

Violin and flute carry the upper parts; viol and keyboard share the bass. Les Nations

form

Binary form

Also: rounded binary

Binary form divides a piece into two main sections, usually both repeated, with the second section returning to the home key.

Binary form has two sections, each usually repeated. The first often ends away from the home key; in a major-key piece that is commonly the dominant. The second begins with contrasting material, moves through other keys and returns to the home key. In rounded binary form, the opening tune also returns within the second half. Scarlatti’s Sonata in D major, K. 119, reaches A major at the end of its first half. When the repeat is taken, the music goes directly from that A major close back to D. (The K. numbers are Ralph Kirkpatrick’s catalogue of 1953.)

The first-half repeat is taken. The music jumps from the A major cadence back to D major material, with no bridge written for it. Sonata in D major, K. 119

harmony

Bitonality

Also: polytonality; bitonal

Bitonality is the use of two keys at the same time in the same passage.

One part of the music is in one key while another part is in a different key. The best-known example built from chords is in Stravinsky’s Petrushka, where C–E–G, a C major triad, sounds against F♯–A♯–C♯, an F♯ major triad. The two chords are a tritone apart, the furthest-removed pair possible. The passage belongs to the unhappy puppet’s music in the ballet’s second scene, ‘Petrushka’s Room’.

Cadence

Also: cadences

A cadence is a melodic and harmonic arrival that marks the end of a phrase or section.

A cadence ends a phrase. A perfect cadence, dominant to tonic, is a full close; a half cadence stops on the dominant; a deceptive cadence moves to another chord; a plagal cadence, subdominant to tonic, is the ‘Amen’ close. Cadences also mark the divisions of a form, such as the end of an exposition or the start of a coda. The final cadence of Bach’s B minor fugue reaches B major. Its D♯ replaces the D♮ of the minor key. This is called a Picardy third, and it changes the mode without changing the tonic. The last chord is held under a fermata.

The opening ritornello closes in D major, and its pulse carries on into the solo episode. Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050

Cadenza

Also: cadenzas

A cadenza is a solo display, usually unaccompanied, within a concerto, aria or other work.

Cadenza is Italian for ‘cadence’. The custom probably began with singers, who used the pause before the final close of an aria to display their flexibility and range. A concerto cadenza may be improvised or written out, and may turn the movement’s themes into scales, trills and other virtuoso passages. Bach gave the harpsichord a long solo near the end of the first movement of the Fifth Brandenburg Concerto, bars 154–⁠218, before the tutti returns at bar 219. Schumann wrote a long cadenza into his Piano Concerto. Beethoven used the device for the solo oboe Adagio at bar 268 of his Fifth Symphony.

texture

Canon

A canon is a melody imitated by another voice at a fixed distance in time.

In a canon one voice leads and another follows with the same melody. In a round such as Frère Jacques, each group sings the same notes, entering later and continuing while the next group begins. More elaborate canons start the following voice at another pitch, turn the melody upside down or stretch its note values. Bach’s Goldberg Variations include nine canons, with the interval between their entering voices increasing from the unison to the ninth. The third movement of Mahler’s First Symphony is a funeral march on Frère Jacques in the minor. A solo double bass plays the subject first, followed by bassoon, tuba and eventually the whole orchestra, with an oboe counter-melody above.

Catalogue number

Also: catalogue numbers; opus number; opus numbers; BWV

A catalogue number identifies a particular work within a composer’s output.

An opus number often reflects publication order. Other numbers come from catalogues, and the compiler’s initials became part of each work’s name: BWV for Bach (Wolfgang Schmieder, 1950), K. for Mozart (Ludwig von Köchel, 1862), D. for Schubert (Otto Erich Deutsch, 1951) and Wq for C. P. E. Bach (Alfred Wotquenne). The same letter can belong to different catalogues depending on the composer. Mozart’s K. numbers refer to Köchel’s catalogue; Scarlatti’s refer to Ralph Kirkpatrick’s, of 1953. Mozart’s Symphony No. 40 is therefore K. 550, and Scarlatti’s D major keyboard sonata heard in the Hall is K. 119.

Chaconne

A chaconne is a set of variations built on a recurring bass or chord pattern, usually in triple time.

The chaconne began in Spain in the late sixteenth century, reputedly brought from the New World, as a fast dance-song with suggestive movements and mocking texts. By the early eighteenth century it had become a slow instrumental piece in triple time, built on the same short foundation repeated throughout while the upper parts are varied. Bach’s D minor Chaconne fills the last movement of his Second Partita for solo violin, BWV 1004. His Cantata No. 150, Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich, ends with another chaconne. In 1880 Brahms played friends its bass line and asked: “What would you think of a symphonic movement written on this theme someday?” He adapted it for the finale of his Fourth Symphony, stretching the pattern to eight bars and adding a chromatic note.

In this passage the recurring pattern taken from Bach’s cantata continues beneath the variations. Symphony No. 4 in E minor, Op. 98

Chromatic slide

A chromatic slide moves a note or chord by a semitone into a new harmonic setting.

harmony

Chromaticism

Also: chromatic

Chromaticism uses notes outside the prevailing scale, often bringing semitone movement into melodies and chords.

A chromatic note belongs to the twelve-note scale but not to the seven-note scale of the current key. In C major, F♯ is a chromatic note; a melody rising F–F♯–G passes through it between two notes of the scale. A small amount of chromaticism colours a diatonic melody; a large amount loosens a key’s hold, as in Wagner’s Tristan and the early works of Schoenberg. Chromatic lines can still be tonal. The subject of Bach’s B minor fugue contains all twelve notes, joining semitone pairs with wide leaps, and the fugue remains in B minor as its home key.

Clavichord

A clavichord is a quiet, touch-sensitive keyboard instrument whose metal tangents strike the strings and remain against them.

The tangent stays in contact with the string for as long as the key is held, so the player can control the volume through key velocity and rock the key to produce Bebung, a small vibrato. The instrument is very quiet. In Hamburg in 1772 Charles Burney persuaded C. P. E. Bach to play after dinner; Bach continued, Burney wrote, “with little intermission, till near eleven o’clock at night”, and “looked like one inspired”. Joan Benson plays the F♯ minor Fantasia, Wq 67, on clavichord in the Hall.

form

Coda

A coda is a closing passage added after a piece’s main sections, ranging from a few chords to a substantial final section.

Coda is Italian for ‘tail’. It follows the last main section of a movement, or the last variation of a set. A coda may settle the key, visit a new one or revisit earlier material. In Beethoven’s Fifth, the first movement’s coda occupies bars 374–⁠502 and is longer than the exposition. It starts on the cadence that closes the recapitulation, with no pause. The violins hold a high C while the bass moves beneath them through C, A♭, E and F, so the held note no longer sounds over settled harmony. The four-note motto continues to be developed in the coda. Chopin’s First Ballade ends with a fast coda marked Presto con fuoco, in cut time.

Col legno

Col legno tells string players to use the wooden stick of the bow against the strings.

Col legno battuto means tapping the strings with the stick of the bow, which makes a dry, pitched rattle. Col legno tratto means drawing the wood across them, which produces a faint, rough sound. Berlioz asked for the tapping kind in the Witches’ Sabbath finale of Symphonie fantastique, where the strings play the dance with the wood of their bows.

Common-tone modulation

Also: common tone modulation

Common-tone modulation connects two keys through a note that belongs to both the old and the new harmony.

A held note stays in place while its surroundings change. C, for example, is the root of C major and the third of A♭ major. Fauré used that shared pitch in the Agnus Dei of his Requiem. The sopranos hold C alone; when the other voices enter beneath them, the note belongs to A♭ major and Lux aeterna has begun.

tuning

Concert pitch

Also: A=440; A = 440; A440; A=415; A = 415; pitch standard

Concert pitch is the agreed reference pitch to which musicians tune their instruments.

The reference is usually A above middle C. At A=440, that note vibrates 440 times a second, and ISO 16 sets this as the international standard. An international conference in London recommended 440 Hz in 1939, and the International Organization for Standardization took it up in 1955 and confirmed it in 1975. Before that, A=435 had been fixed in Paris in 1859 as the diapason normal and confirmed at a Vienna conference in 1885. Ensembles can choose another pitch, especially when playing historical instruments. Early-music players often use A=415, about a semitone below modern pitch, as a convenient Baroque standard. Tuning pitch sets how high the music sounds. Temperament sets the sizes of the intervals within it.

Violin, flute and continuo share a tuning reference as they exchange phrases. Les Nations

Concerto

Also: concertos

A concerto brings one or more soloists into musical partnership and competition with an orchestra.

The soloist may lead, answer the orchestra or play alone in a cadenza. In the Baroque concerto a solo group is set against the full ensemble, or tutti, which opens the movement and returns between the soloists’ episodes. Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto has three solo instruments: flute, violin and an obbligato harpsichord whose part is written out in full. Later in the movement the harpsichord plays a long unaccompanied passage. In Schumann’s Piano Concerto the piano enters immediately, descending in a rapid figure after the orchestra’s opening chord. Mozart wrote his Clarinet Concerto, K. 622, for Anton Stadler’s extended-range basset clarinet; it was first played in Prague on 16 October 1791.

Also: contrapuntal

Counterpoint combines melodic lines that keep their own shape and rhythm while sounding together.

Each part has a melodic line that can be heard on its own, and each affects the others. One may hold a note while another moves, or answer a rising phrase with a falling one. Countersubject

A countersubject is a recurring melody written to accompany a fugue’s subject or answer.

When the first voice of a fugue finishes the subject, it may begin a second melody against the next voice’s answer. If this companion returns with later entries, it is a countersubject. It usually contrasts in rhythm with the subject and is written to work above or below it, because the two change places. In Bach’s B minor fugue, descending crotchets in the alto accompany the tenor’s answer. At bar 9 the tenor carries that companion above the bass’s subject while the alto adds a quicker line, and three different rhythms sound at once.

A crescendo build is a passage that grows gradually louder towards a climax.

Cyclic form

Also: cyclic; idée fixe

Cyclic form links the movements of a work by bringing back themes heard in earlier movements.

A familiar theme returns in a new setting. In Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique, the melody representing the beloved appears in all five movements. It is heard at the ball and becomes grotesque at the Witches’ Sabbath. In the March to the Scaffold, a solo clarinet plays it just before the full orchestra cuts it off.

transition

Deceptive cadence

Also: interrupted cadence

A deceptive cadence moves from the dominant to a chord other than the tonic, usually the chord on the sixth degree, delaying the close.

In C major, G–B–D normally resolves to C–E–G. In a deceptive cadence an A minor chord follows instead: A–C–E, which shares two notes with the tonic chord but is not the tonic. The melody can still reach C, but the bass has moved to A, and the phrase continues. In a minor key, the usual sixth-degree chord is major. British musicians also call this an interrupted cadence. In the first movement of Brahms’s Fourth Symphony, Kelly Dean Hansen identifies an expected arrival on B at bar 73, and again at bar 184, thwarted by “a deceptive motion to G”, after which the fanfares return.

Decrescendo

Also: diminuendo; decrescendi

A decrescendo is a gradual decrease in loudness.

Diminuendo usually means the same thing; a closing hairpin is the common sign. A player can soften a phrase without changing its pace. In the development of Beethoven’s Fifth, the wind and string replies become quieter as they shrink from short phrases to isolated chords. In Elgar’s Nimrod the long melody descends from the climax and grows quiet.

Development section

Also: development section

The development is the middle section of a sonata-form movement, where earlier themes are altered and taken through other keys.

A development works on music already heard. A tune may be cut down to its opening notes, and two instruments may exchange the same fragment. Composers repeat the fragments in sequence, pass them between instruments and move the harmony through keys far from the home key. In Beethoven’s Fifth, bars 125–⁠247 return again and again to the four-note motto. The horns restart it, and wind and strings exchange pairs of chords from bar 196 and single chords from bar 209, until very little of the tune remains. The full opening returns at bar 248. In the development of Mozart’s G minor Symphony, only the first few notes of the opening tune are left, repeated on different instruments. A development usually ends by preparing the return, often by settling on the dominant.

Diminished seventh

Also: diminished seventh chord; diminished chord

A diminished seventh chord consists of four notes a minor third apart. It is a tense chord with several possible resolutions.

B–D–F–A♭ divides the octave into four equal minor thirds, so only three different diminished seventh chords exist. In C minor, B rises to C and the chord can resolve to the tonic; respelled, its notes can lead to other keys, which makes it a staple of retransitions and sudden modulations. Equal temperament

Also: equal-tempered

Twelve-note equal temperament divides the octave into twelve equal semitones, giving every key the same pattern of intervals.

Each semitone is the same size, a frequency ratio of the twelfth root of 2. In this tuning a melody moved from C major to D♭ major keeps exactly the same interval sizes. The compromise is small but audible in chords. A fifth is about two cents narrower than a pure fifth, and a major third, at 400 cents, is about fourteen cents wider than a pure 5:4 third. Through the nineteenth century keyboard tuning moved steadily closer to equal temperament, and it is the usual starting point for modern piano tuning. The Hall’s recordings of Rameau’s Gavotte and Scarlatti’s K. 119 are played on a piano, although both composers wrote for harpsichord, which was tuned in other ways.

Metre

Also: meter; time signature

Metre groups musical beats into recurring patterns, such as the three beats in a waltz.

A time signature shows how the beats fit into a bar. Accents can conflict with the pattern. The third movement of Brahms’s Third Symphony is in 3/8, but its middle section is metrically displaced throughout. In the finale of Brahms’s Fourth, a slower variation changes 3/4 into 3/2, doubling the length of each bar while the note values keep the same speed.

C major and C minor share the tonic but differ in their third, sixth and seventh. A composer can move between them without changing the home note. C major can borrow F–A♭–C from C minor, and this F minor chord changes the sound of the key. Switching a whole theme from major to minor is a larger version of the same contrast. Haydn did this in the Andante of the “Surprise” Symphony, where the second variation restarts the C major theme in C minor. C and G remain, E♭ replaces E♮, and loud unison phrases make the switch conspicuous. Ending a minor piece on a major triad, as Bach’s B minor fugue does, is called a Picardy third.

Modes

Also: modal; Dorian; Phrygian

Modes are ways of arranging a scale around a home note, each with its own pattern of tones and semitones.

D Dorian uses the white piano keys from D to D: D–E–F–G–A–B–C. Its B♮ distinguishes it from D natural minor, which has B♭; Dorian is a minor scale with a raised sixth. E Phrygian uses the white keys from E to E, with a semitone immediately above its home note, so it is minor with a lowered second. F Lydian begins on F and has B♮, so it is major with a raised fourth. Major and natural minor correspond to the Ionian and Aeolian modes. In Satie’s first Gymnopédie, a later phrase brings C♮, F♮ and B♮ above a D in the bass, which gives D Dorian colour. Modulation

Also: modulate

Modulation is a change from one established key to another within a piece.

A modulation is a change of key, after which another note serves as the tonic. The new key may be reached through a chord both keys share (a pivot chord) or a note held across the change (a common-tone modulation), or it may come suddenly (a direct modulation). Large forms depend on it. A sonata exposition moves from the home key to a second key, and the development moves further. In the first movement of Beethoven’s Fifth, the exposition leaves C minor for its relative major, E♭. Also: motive; motifs

A motif is a short, recognisable musical idea that can be repeated, altered and combined with other ideas.

A motif is smaller than a theme and more adaptable. It can be turned upside down, stretched, shrunk, passed between instruments or placed in a different harmony without losing its identity. Also: Neapolitan chord

A Neapolitan sixth is a major chord on the flattened second note of a key, with its third in the bass.

In C minor, the Neapolitan chord is D♭–F–A♭, a major triad on the flattened second degree, a semitone above the tonic. Parody

Also: parodied

Musical parody can mean reworking an existing composition or imitating music for comic effect.

Pedal point

Also: pedal note; organ point

A pedal point is a note held or repeated while the chords around it change.

Rondo

Also: rondo form

A rondo brings its main tune back between contrasting episodes, often in the pattern A–B–A–C–A.

The returning tune is called the refrain; the passages between its appearances are episodes. The refrain is always heard in the home key, though its accompaniment or scoring may change; the episodes change key, tune and character. In a symphony or sonata a rondo is usually a fast finale or a slow second movement. Ravel’s Pavane pour une infante défunte follows the pattern A–B–A–C–A: its G major tune returns after each of two contrasting episodes. In the orchestral version a solo horn plays the first statement. Strauss gave Till Eulenspiegels lustige Streiche the subtitle in Rondeauform; the horn tune and clarinet motif that identify Till return between his pranks.

rhythm

Rubato

Also: tempo rubato

Rubato is flexibility of timing in performance, with some notes held longer and others played ahead of the beat.

Italian rubato means ‘stolen’: time taken in one place is given back in another. A singer or player can delay a note, then let the phrase catch up. Sometimes the melody moves freely over a steady accompaniment; sometimes all the parts slow and quicken together. Writing to his father from Augsburg on 23 October 1777, Mozart complained that other pianists let the left hand follow every delay in the right: “When they play the left hand always follows.” His own left hand kept strict time.

form

Scherzo

A scherzo is a lively piece or movement, often in triple time with a contrasting middle section called a trio.

Scherzo means ‘joke’, though the music can be fierce. Haydn’s Op. 33 string quartets (1781) are the first in which a dance movement is called a scherzo, in place of the minuet. Beethoven made it fast and driving, and later composers kept the plan of an outer section, a trio and a return. Bruckner’s Seventh Symphony has an A minor scherzo marked Sehr schnell, ‘very fast’, in which a trumpet plays a call above a repeated string figure. The F major trio is slower. The scherzo of Ravel’s String Quartet (Assez vif — Très rythmé) opens with a pizzicato theme, and Prokofiev’s Second Piano Sonata has a short Scherzo, Allegro marcato, as its second movement.

Sequence

Also: sequences

A sequence repeats a melodic or harmonic pattern at successively higher or lower pitches.

A short phrase is repeated, each time a step higher or lower. A tonal sequence adjusts some intervals to stay within the scale; a real sequence keeps the exact intervals. In Beethoven’s Fifth, bars 410–⁠414, a violin figure steps down: E♭–D–F–E♭, then D–C–E♭–D, then C–B♭–D–C. In bars 19–⁠20 of Bach’s B minor fugue, the bass plays a four-note shape, F♯–A–B–D, then repeats it a tone lower as E–G–A–C♯. The upper voices continue their lines while the sequence takes the episode downwards.

form

Sonata form

Also: sonata-allegro form; sonata-allegro

Sonata form presents themes in different keys, develops them, then brings them back in the home key.

A sonata-form movement usually has three main sections: exposition, development and recapitulation. The exposition sets a first theme in the home key against a second in a new one, the dominant in a major-key movement and the relative major in a minor-key one. In the exposition of Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony, a first theme in C minor is followed by a second in E♭ major (bars 1–⁠124, then repeated). The development opens at bar 125 and breaks up the four-note motto, taking it through other keys. The recapitulation begins at bar 248. When the second group returns at bar 303 it is in C major, on the same home note as the first theme. A long coda brings the movement back to C minor.

The name itself is nineteenth-century. Haydn, Mozart and early Beethoven very probably never used it; the critic A. B. Marx endorsed it in 1838 and 1845. Many of their movements depart from the plan it describes.

form

Stretto

In stretto, entries of a fugue subject overlap: the next voice starts before the previous statement has finished.

Stretto is Italian for ‘narrow’. In a stretto the entries of the subject overlap, with a second entry beginning while the first is still sounding. It usually comes late in a fugue to raise tension, and it is possible only if the subject was written to fit against itself at a short distance. In Bach’s B minor fugue, the alto starts a shortened subject in F♯ minor at bar 34 and the soprano follows one bar later in B minor. From bar 41, soprano (B minor), alto (E minor, bar 42), bass (A major, bar 43) and tenor (D major, bar 44) enter in successive bars, each taking up the subject’s opening before the preceding voice has finished.

harmony

Suspension

Also: suspensions

A suspension holds a note from one chord into the next, where it clashes before resolving down by step.

In a suspension, a note from one chord is held over into the next. A suspension has three stages. The note is prepared in a consonance, then held while the chord beneath it changes, so that it becomes a dissonance, and then resolves down by step. For example, an F in the melody can be held over a new C major chord and then fall to E. In Bach’s B minor fugue, the soprano’s E is tied across bars 19–⁠20 over D in the bass and resolves down to D. Near the close, at bar 75, the soprano holds B over the dominant F♯, then steps down to A♯ before the final chord.

The soprano’s E, tied over the bass’s D, resolves down to D. The Well-⁠Tempered Clavier, Book I · BWV 869Bar 19 · timed automatically

form

Symphonic poem

Also: tone poem

A symphonic poem is a usually single-movement orchestral work inspired by a story, poem, picture or idea.

A symphonic poem is an orchestral work with a subject outside the music. Liszt began his series with Ce qu’on entend sur la montagne, composed in 1847–⁠48 on a poem by Victor Hugo and first performed at Weimar in February 1850; the last, Von der Wiege bis zum Grabe (1881–⁠82), was based on a drawing by Mihály Zichy. Themes can stand for characters and are varied as the characters’ fortunes change. Richard Strauss later combined the colours of a large orchestra with an exact narrative in Don Juan and Till Eulenspiegel. Till Eulenspiegels lustige Streiche, Op. 28, was first performed in Cologne on 5 November 1895, conducted by Franz Wüllner. A winding horn tune and a clarinet figure introduce the prankster, and his adventures end in a trial and execution. The Hall presents the piece in Herb Weber’s transcription for concert band.

A tempo change alters the speed of the musical beat.

Ternary form

Also: A–B–A form; ABA form

Ternary form has an opening section, a contrasting middle and a return of the opening, often with changes.

The outline is A–B–A. The middle section can change key or introduce another tune; the return may be written out with new details. The plan underlies the da capo aria, the minuet and trio and many short piano pieces. Da capo, ‘from the head’, is the instruction to go back and play the first section again. In Schubert’s G♭ major Impromptu, D. 899 No. 3, a piano melody surrounds an E♭ minor middle section that begins at bar 25, with accented B♭s over broken chords. The accompaniment continues underneath both. Bar 54 holds a D♭ dominant seventh under a fermata before the opening melody returns at bar 55, very softly.

Texture change

Also: change of texture

A texture change alters the number of musical lines or the way they fit together.

A single tune can be shared between voices, and separate moving voices can join in one chord. Handel’s Hallelujah chorus shifts repeatedly between singers moving together and parts entering one after another. Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto thins its opening ensemble to a trio of flute, solo violin and harpsichord, and the quick pulse continues across the change.

Theme and variations

Also: variation form; variations

Theme and variations presents a tune followed by altered versions that keep part of its melody, bass or chords.

The theme is usually short and clearly phrased, so that it can be followed through its changes. A variation can ornament a tune, put it in another voice, turn it from major to minor, thin or thicken the orchestra or alter the metre. The Andante of Haydn’s “Surprise” Symphony has a simple C major theme in the manner of a nursery tune, followed by four variations; the second is in C minor. In Rameau’s Gavotte with Six Doubles, each double is another variation, with faster notes running through the theme’s chords. At the end of the finale of Beethoven’s Op. 109 the trills stop and the theme returns without ornament.

form

Toccata

A toccata is an instrumental showpiece, usually for keyboard, written to display the player’s touch and dexterity.

The name comes from the Italian toccare, ‘to touch’. The earliest, by Merulo and Giovanni Gabrieli, were showy keyboard pieces in flowing notes of equal length, written in the manner of an improvisation. Bach’s D minor Toccata, with its fugue, begins and ends with rapid cadenza-like passages divided between the two hands. Other toccatas keep the fingers moving with few breaks. Prokofiev’s Toccata, Op. 11, is of the second type. He composed it in the spring of 1912 for his fellow student Nikolay Shtember, and Jurgenson published it in Moscow in 1913. Repeated Ds pass between the hands, and chromatic neighbour notes and hard-edged chords are then added to them. The piece has few rests for the pianist. Ravel ends Le Tombeau de Couperin with a toccata, dedicated to the memory of Captain Joseph de Marliave.

Repeated Ds pass between the hands; neighbouring notes gradually join them. Toccata in D minor, Op. 11

Tonic

Also: home key

The tonic is a key’s home note, and the tonic chord is the chord built on that note.

C is the tonic of both C major and C minor. The major chord is C–E–G; the minor chord lowers E to E♭. Every other note and chord is heard in relation to it. Other chords can lead the music away, and a cadence confirms the tonic. A long piece can postpone the tonic or leave it by modulation. At the end of Chopin’s D♭ major Nocturne, Op. 27 No. 2, the left hand repeats D♭ beneath the right hand’s final duet.

The last expansion settles on a D♭ in the bass, which the coda repeats as a tonic pedal under the final duet of the melody. Nocturne in D♭ major, Op. 27 No. 2

Tonic pedal

A tonic pedal holds or repeats the home note while the chords and melodies around it move.

The bass can remain on C while the upper parts move through C major, F major and other chords. Some of these chords may clash with the held note. A dominant pedal creates tension and a tonic pedal gives stability; a tonic pedal often marks a coda or an introduction. Chopin closes his D♭ major Nocturne, Op. 27 No. 2, over repeated D♭s in the bass, while the right hand continues its duet. In the first birdsong episode of Vivaldi’s Spring, bars 13–⁠27, the violins hold tonic and dominant pedals on E and B under the trills.

Transfer

Also: transfers

A transfer copies sound from one medium to another, for example from an old disc to a digital file.

A transfer begins with playback. For a disc, the engineer chooses a stylus that fits the groove and a speed that restores the music’s pitch and pace; at the Library of Congress the monaural transfer is made in two channels, to capture both walls of the groove, before the file is summed. Equalisation adjusts the balance of low and high sounds; restoration may remove clicks and reduce surface noise. Each choice changes the sound, so a transfer credit belongs to a recording’s history. The Hall’s copy of Rachmaninoff playing his Prelude comes from an Edison disc and was cleaned and restored by Adam Cuerden.

Tremolo is the rapid repetition of a note or rapid alternation between notes.

String players make a bowed tremolo with quick, short strokes; pianists can alternate two notes or chords. A measured tremolo has a specified rhythm. harmony

Tristan chord

The Tristan chord is the chord F–B–D♯–G♯, heard near the opening of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde.

In the Prelude to Tristan und Isolde, a short cello phrase leads into F–B–D♯–G♯. The notes move by small steps into another dissonant chord, and then the phrase stops. Wagner repeats the pattern at a higher pitch. The harmony does not resolve until Isolde’s Liebestod, in the last act. The chord’s spelling and the movement of its individual notes allow several harmonic readings. The notes themselves were not new. H. A. Harding pointed out a nearly identical chord in bar 36 of the first movement of Beethoven’s Piano Sonata in E♭, Op. 31 No. 3. The chord became the emblem of the opera. Debussy quoted it in Golliwogg’s Cake-walk and Berg in the Lyric Suite, and Britten used it in Albert Herring while the hero hiccups after drinking spiked lemonade.

harmony

Twelve-note row

Also: twelve-tone row; tone row; twelve-tone; serialism

A twelve-note row places the twelve notes of the chromatic scale in an order that supplies a piece’s melodies and chords.

A row uses each of the twelve notes once, treating notes in different octaves as the same pitch class. A composer can play it forwards, reverse its order, turn its intervals upside down and transpose it to any of twelve pitches, so there are forty-eight forms to work with. Notes from the row can sound together as chords. Schoenberg’s Piano Suite, Op. 25, builds a prelude and old dance forms from one row; its minuet’s trio works the row into a canon. A theme that happens to contain all twelve notes is not a row. The subject of Bach’s B minor fugue contains all twelve, but it has no system of inversion and retrograde behind it, and it is heard against a home key.

Bach’s subject contains all twelve notes and remains in B minor. The Well-⁠Tempered Clavier, Book I · BWV 869Bar 1 · timed automatically

tuning

Well temperament

Also: well-tempered

Well temperament is a family of unequal keyboard tunings that make every key usable while giving different keys different interval sizes.

The tuner distributes small adjustments unevenly among the notes. Some keys have purer thirds than others. Earlier systems, such as meantone, made some keys pure and others unusable; the tuning Andreas Werckmeister described in 1691 spreads the compromise unevenly so that every key can be used. Each book of Bach’s The Well-⁠Tempered Clavier contains pieces in all twenty-four major and minor keys, from the opening C major prelude to the final B minor fugue. Its title does not say which tuning Bach had in mind, and keyboard players still argue the question.

Whole-tone scale

Also: whole-tone

A whole-tone scale has six notes, with a whole tone between every neighbouring pair.

C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯ is one whole-tone scale. Starting a semitone higher gives the other; every further transposition repeats one of those two collections. The scale contains no semitones, so it has no leading note, and no two of its notes lie a perfect fifth apart. A chord made from alternate notes of the scale, such as C–E–G♯, is an augmented triad. Glinka used the scale in Ruslan and Lyudmila, first performed in St Petersburg on 9 December 1842, to depict the evil sorcerer Chernomor.
