78 rpm

Also: 78s; 78 rpm disc; shellac disc

The nominal speed of shellac discs from the early 1900s to the 1950s; a 12-inch side held about four and a half minutes.

In the acoustic era disc speeds varied between makers; the standard settled at roughly 78 revolutions a minute. A symphony needed several discs, issued as a numbered set whose sides were played in turn. Edison’s Diamond Discs were different: they ran at 80 rpm and were cut vertically (“hill and dale”), so they cannot be played on a machine built for the others.

The Hall’s recording of the “Surprise” Andante is two sides, Andante, part 3 and Andante concluded, part 4, of Victor set M-55 (the Andante on disc 7059). The Scheherazade movement is Bob Varney’s transfer from the original Victor 78 rpm set DM-920, made by Pierre Monteux and the San Francisco Symphony in March 1942.

recording

Acoustic recording

Also: acoustic recordings; electrical recording; electrical recordings

Recording before microphones, when performers played into a horn that drove the cutting stylus directly; electrical recording replaced it in 1925–27.

Before the mid-1920s performers crowded around a horn, and the sound waves moved a diaphragm that cut the groove directly. The system registered only a narrow band of frequencies — roughly 350 to 3,000 Hz, according to the Discography of American Historical Recordings — and the need to gather round one horn limited the size of ensembles. In spring 1925 Victor and Columbia began issuing records made with microphones and amplifiers; the range widened to about 100–5,000 Hz, and orchestral recording grew rapidly. Edison, the last major label to change, recorded its Diamond Discs acoustically until 1927.

Rachmaninoff’s own recording of his C♯ minor Prelude was made in 1919 for Edison (Diamond Disc 82187-R, issued in December 1920), so it is an acoustic recording. Haydn’s “Surprise” Andante, with Koussevitzky and the Boston Symphony in Symphony Hall on 22 April 1929 for Victor, is electrical.

Appoggiatura

Also: appoggiaturas

A dissonant “leaning” note that arrives on a strong beat, unprepared, and moves by step to a chord note.

From the Italian appoggiare, to lean. An appoggiatura delays the point of rest inside a phrase: the melody sounds a note that clashes with the harmony, leans on it, and then releases it. It differs from a suspension in that the note is not tied over from the previous chord. Analysts have read the G♯ of the Tristan chord as an appoggiatura.

Mahler’s Adagietto from the Fifth Symphony is full of them. The Hall’s notes ask you to listen for notes that clash with the harmony and then move by step to a chord tone.

Aria

Also: arias

A self-contained song for one voice in an opera, oratorio or cantata, where the action pauses for a character’s feeling.

Recitative carries the plot; the aria stops it to express a feeling at length. The Baroque da capo aria is in A–B–A form: the singer ornaments the A section when it returns. Later composers dissolved the boundary, so that an aria can grow out of conversation and return to it.

Mimì’s “Sì, mi chiamano Mimì” in Puccini’s La bohème (Act I, Turin, February 1896) answers Rodolfo’s account of his poet’s life with her own. It moves from short speech-like phrases to sustained singing: at a held pause the light 2/4 account of solitary life gives way to common time and Andante molto sostenuto, with a melody about spring that climbs to D major.

Atonality

Also: atonal

Music with no tonic: no single note or chord acts as home, so keys and cadences no longer organise it.

Atonal music does not use the key as its organising principle. Chords are heard by their sound rather than their function, and dissonance is not expected to resolve. The step towards it came gradually through the chromatic harmony of late Romanticism.

Schoenberg’s String Quartet No. 2, Op. 10 (1907–08), shows the transition. Its first two movements carry key signatures; the last two add a soprano singing poems by Stefan George and are almost fully atonal. The finale, Entrückung, begins “Ich fühle Luft von anderem Planeten” (“I feel air from another planet”), has no key signature, and ends on an F♯-major triad, the key in which the quartet began in minor. The first performance, in Vienna on 21 December 1908, had the soprano Marie Gutheil-Schoder and the Rosé Quartet.

transition

Attacca

Also: attacca subito

Italian “attack”: an instruction to go straight on to the next movement without a pause.

Movements are normally separated by a pause in which the performers settle and the audience breathes. Attacca removes it, so the end of one movement leads straight into the start of the next and the work feels continuous. Mendelssohn links the movements of his Violin Concerto in E minor, Op. 64, in this way, without a break.

harmony

Augmented sixth chord

Also: augmented sixth; German sixth; Italian sixth; French sixth

A chord containing an augmented sixth between its outer notes, which expand outward by step to an octave on the dominant.

In C major or minor the interval is A♭ up to F♯. On the dominant chord the two notes resolve outward by step, A♭ to G and F♯ to G, an octave apart. Three standard versions add different inner notes: the Italian sixth (A♭–C–F♯), the French (adding D) and the German (adding E♭). Sounding identical to a dominant seventh on A♭, the German sixth can be respelled and reinterpreted to modulate.

Beethoven’s “Pathétique” Sonata, Op. 13, has a French sixth in the finale at bars 44–47, as set out in Fundamentals, Function, and Form; the final bars of Schubert’s String Quintet in C major, D. 956, use another, as Nicole Biamonte describes in Music Theory Online.

Bar

Also: bars; bar number; bar numbers

A unit of music between two bar lines, holding a set number of beats; scores number the bars so passages can be found.

American usage says measure. Bars are counted from the first complete bar; pick-up notes before it are not numbered. A passage inside a repeat is performed twice, and the Hall therefore records which pass is meant: “bar 1, occurrence 2” is the second time bar 1 is played.

Beethoven’s Fifth, first movement, shows the effect. The exposition (bars 1–124) is repeated, so every bar from 1 to 124 has two timings in the recording. Bar numbers on the Hall’s pages come from public-domain scores, and their timings are produced by software and marked provisional until a listener has checked them.

instrument

Basset clarinet

Also: basset clarinets

A clarinet extended downwards by a few notes, for which Mozart wrote his Clarinet Concerto, K. 622, for Anton Stadler.

Anton Stadler’s extended-range clarinet could play lower than the clarinets most players use. Mozart wrote the concerto for it, and the autograph is lost; the early published edition adapted the low passages for an ordinary clarinet, so modern basset-clarinet versions reconstruct the extended line from surviving evidence rather than a complete original score.

In the Hall’s recording of the Adagio, William McColl plays a basset clarinet he constructed himself, not a surviving instrument from Stadler’s time.

performance

Basso continuo

Also: continuo; figured bass; thoroughbass

The accompanying bass line of Baroque music, played by a low melody instrument and a chord player who fills in the harmony from numbers.

The bass line is written with small figures under the notes (figured bass) giving the intervals above it: 6 means a first-inversion chord, 7 a seventh, and so on. A keyboard player — harpsichord, organ or lute — improvises the chords from the figures, while a cello, viola da gamba or bassoon doubles the bass. Together they are the continuo; the keyboard’s realisation is different at each performance.

Couperin’s La Piémontaise from Les Nations (1726) is written for two treble instruments and continuo; in the Hall’s recording violin and flute take the upper parts, and viola da gamba and keyboard share the bass. In Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto the harpsichord has a written-out solo part rather than only accompanying chords; the Hall lists it as an obbligato part, a written part the composer treats as essential.

form

Binary form

Also: rounded binary

A form in two repeated halves: the first leaves the home key, the second returns. Baroque dances and Scarlatti’s sonatas use it.

Each half is normally repeated. The first half moves from the tonic to a second key — the dominant in a major-key piece — and ends with a cadence there. The second half begins in that key, wanders, and comes back to the tonic. In rounded binary the opening material also returns near the end of the second half, which foreshadows sonata form.

Domenico Scarlatti’s Sonata in D major, K. 119, shows the layout: its first half travels from D major to a cadence in A major, and when the repeat is taken the music springs from that A-major close straight back to the opening D-major gestures. (K. numbers are Ralph Kirkpatrick’s catalogue of 1953.)

harmony

Bitonality

Also: polytonality; bitonal

Two keys sounding at once, as in Stravinsky’s Petrushka chord of C major against F♯ major.

Bitonal music layers two keys, one in each hand or each section, without resolving the clash. The two triads of the Petrushka chord are a tritone apart, the furthest-removed pair possible, and the effect is of two incompatible worlds side by side. In Petrushka (1911) the chord is the puppet’s music: in the second tableau, as the puppet pulls himself together, clarinets play arpeggios of C major and F♯ major together.

Cadence

Also: cadences

A harmonic formula that closes a phrase, like punctuation: perfect, half, deceptive and plagal cadences are the four standard kinds.

A cadence is two or more chords that end a phrase with more or less finality. The perfect cadence (dominant to tonic) is a full stop; the half cadence stops on the dominant and asks a question; the deceptive cadence swerves from the dominant to another chord; the plagal cadence (subdominant to tonic) is the “Amen” close. Cadences also mark the joins in a form: the end of an exposition, the arrival of a coda.

The last chord of Bach’s B minor fugue is B major, not B minor. The raised third (D♯ replacing D♮) is called a Picardy third; it changes the mode without changing the tonic.

Cadenza

Also: cadenzas

A passage for the soloist alone in a concerto or aria, displaying technique over a pause on the dominant; originally improvised.

Italian for “cadence”. In a concerto the orchestra stops, usually on a dominant chord, and the soloist plays alone, traditionally improvising on the movement’s themes. Composers from Beethoven on often wrote them out.

Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto has a harpsichord cadenza written out in full, beginning at bar 154 and prolonging the dominant until bar 219, with one player supplying the whole texture. Schumann writes out the cadenza of his Piano Concerto, and the pianist continues alone after the orchestra stops. In Beethoven’s Fifth the oboe’s written Adagio solo at bar 268 acts like a short cadenza within the recapitulation.

texture

Canon

Strict imitation: a melody followed note for note by other voices entering at a fixed distance; a round is a canon that can repeat endlessly.

Every voice sings or plays the same melody, each starting later. The simplest canon is the round: Frère Jacques sung in four staggered voices. In a canon at the unison the voices sing the same pitches; others enter at a different interval, higher or lower, or in inversion. In Bach’s Goldberg Variations every third variation is a canon, at a different interval each time.

In the slow movement of Mahler’s First Symphony (1888), a funeral march is built on Frère Jacques in the minor, introduced by a muted solo double bass.

Catalogue number

Also: catalogue numbers; opus number; opus numbers; BWV

The number that identifies a work: Op. for the composer’s or publisher’s opus order, or a letter code from a later scholar’s catalogue.

Opus numbers were often assigned by a publisher at the time of publication, so they do not always follow the order of composition. For composers who left no reliable list, scholars compiled catalogues, and the initials of the compiler became part of each work’s name: BWV (Bach-Werke-Verzeichnis, Wolfgang Schmieder, 1950), K. or KV for Mozart (Ludwig von Köchel, 1862), D. for Schubert (Otto Erich Deutsch, 1951), RV for Vivaldi (Peter Ryom, 1973), Wq for C. P. E. Bach (Alfred Wotquenne, 1905).

Beware that one letter can mean different catalogues: Scarlatti’s K. numbers are Ralph Kirkpatrick’s (1953), so Mozart’s Symphony No. 40 is K. 550 and Scarlatti’s Sonata in D major is K. 119.

form

Chaconne

Variations over a short repeating bass or chord pattern in triple time, close kin to the passacaglia; a Spanish dance made serious.

The chaconne began in Spain in the late sixteenth century, reputedly brought from the New World, as a fast, lively dance-song; by the seventeenth century it had become a stately set of variations. Bach’s Chaconne in D minor, the last movement of his Partita No. 2 for solo violin (BWV 1004), is the most famous example; the closing chorus of his Cantata No. 150, Nach dir, Herr, verlanget mich, is also a chaconne.

Brahms took the chaconne bass of that cantata chorus for the finale of his Fourth Symphony, enlarging it to eight bars and adding a chromatic note. The Boston Symphony programme note documents the Bach source, and Kelly Dean Hansen’s listening guide names Cantata No. 150.

Chromatic slide

Moving a note or chord by a semitone to reach new harmonic ground, without a conventional modulation.

A semitone step in the bass or an inner part can shift the whole harmony. Because the step is small, it is heard as a slide rather than a leap.

In Debussy’s La cathédrale engloutie the rising fifths return over C in the bass; Debussy lowers the bass to B and establishes B major, while left-hand triplets begin beneath chords that keep the rising pentatonic shape. In the first movement of Rachmaninoff’s Second Piano Concerto, a chromatic descent leaves the horn alone before the quieter restatement of the second subject.

Chromaticism

Also: chromatic

The use of notes outside the key’s seven-note scale, so that melodies and chords move by semitones.

A chromatic note belongs to the twelve-note scale but not to the seven-note scale of the current key. A little chromaticism colours a diatonic melody; a lot loosens the grip of the key, as in Wagner’s Tristan, Mahler and the early works of Schoenberg. Chromatic lines can also be tonal: the subject of Bach’s B minor fugue uses all twelve pitch classes, yet its move towards F♯ gives the line a clear tonal direction.

The opening of Schoenberg’s Second String Quartet, last movement, suspends the usual harmonic bearings with chromatic lines; Chopin’s D♭ nocturne uses chromatic movement in inner notes to loosen a temporary foothold in C♯ minor.

Clavichord

A small keyboard instrument in which brass tangents strike the strings and stay in contact while the key is held; quiet, but sensitive to touch.

When a clavichord key is pressed, a brass tangent hits the string and stays against it, so the player’s finger pressure still affects the sounding note. A trained player can add a gentle vibrato (Bebung), and the instrument can sustain and swell very slightly. It is too quiet for any room larger than a small one, and was made for the player’s own ears.

C. P. E. Bach wrote his F♯ minor Fantasia, Wq 67 (1787), for it. Charles Burney described Bach playing the clavichord in 1772 until nearly eleven at night. The Hall’s recording has Joan Benson at the clavichord.

Coda

Italian “tail”: a closing section after the main structure has ended, sometimes only a few chords, sometimes a second development.

A coda confirms the ending. After the recapitulation of a sonata-form movement, or the last variation of a set, composers add a passage that settles the key and often adds a last thought. In Beethoven it can be long enough to count as a second development.

The coda of Beethoven’s Fifth, first movement, begins at bar 374 and runs to bar 502. It starts on the cadence that closes the recapitulation, with no pause, and holds a high C in the violins while the bass moves under it through C, A♭, E and F, so the repeated note no longer means settled harmony. Chopin’s First Ballade ends with a fast coda, Presto con fuoco, in cut time.

texture

Col legno

Italian “with the wood”: string players strike or draw the stick of the bow, not its hair, for a dry, clattering sound.

Hitting the strings with the stick gives a short, percussive click with little pitch; drawing the stick across them (col legno tratto) gives a faint, hollow sound. A composer asks for it to evoke bones, rattling or something ghostly. It is cancelled by arco or col crini (“with the hair”).

Berlioz uses it in the finale of the Symphonie fantastique (1830), the Witches’ Sabbath, where it is among the effects that depict the gathering of witches. The Hall’s recording is the fourth movement, the March to the Scaffold, which comes before it.

Common-tone modulation

Also: common tone modulation

Moving to a distant key by holding one note shared by both harmonies, which is heard afresh as a different member of the new chord.

The held note acts as a bridge. Because it belongs to both the old chord and the new one, the ear accepts the change even when the other notes move far.

In the Agnus Dei of Fauré’s Requiem, the tenors’ prayer settles towards C major and the sopranos hold C alone while the moving accompaniment falls away. That same pitch becomes the third of A♭ major as the other voices enter beneath it, and the Lux aeterna begins in a six-part choral texture. The join connects distant harmonic surroundings without a conspicuous melodic leap.

tuning

Concert pitch

Also: A=440; A = 440; A440; A=415; A = 415; pitch standard

The frequency to which an orchestra tunes: A=440 Hz today, but Baroque ensembles often tune near A=415, about a semitone lower.

The note A above middle C is the reference. A 1939 conference at Broadcasting House in London recommended 440 Hz, and the standard was adopted by the International Organization for Standardization in 1955 and finalised as ISO 16 in 1975. Before it, a′ = 435 Hz had been fixed in Paris in 1859 (diapason normal) and confirmed at a Vienna conference in 1885. Historical pitch varied from city to city and decade to decade.

Early-music ensembles now commonly use A=415 for the Baroque, about a semitone lower than modern pitch, and around A=430 for the Classical period. The Hall’s Couperin recording appears to sit near A=415 by estimate; the performers do not document a pitch standard.

Concerto

Also: concertos

A work in which one or more soloists contrast and cooperate with an orchestra, usually in three movements.

The Baroque concerto sets a solo group against the full ensemble, or tutti, in ritornello form. The Classical concerto absorbs sonata form, with the orchestra stating the themes first and the soloist developing them, and ends the first movement with a cadenza. Romantic composers lengthened both the display and the argument, and sometimes cut the orchestral introduction.

In Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto the solo group of flute, violin and harpsichord converses with the strings; the harpsichord’s part is written out in full and swells into a long cadenza. Schumann’s Piano Concerto in A minor begins with one orchestral attack and a descending piano flourish, with no long orchestral introduction. Mozart’s Clarinet Concerto, K. 622, was composed for Anton Stadler’s extended-range instrument.

Counterpoint

Also: contrapuntal

Music made by combining independent melodic lines that fit together, as in fugue and canon.

From the Latin punctus contra punctum, “note against note”. In counterpoint each line has its own rhythm and shape, yet the combination stays consonant at the points that matter. The opposite is a single melody with accompaniment. Baroque counterpoint reached its height in Bach, and later composers returned to it when they wanted density: in the development of Mozart’s G minor Symphony, or in the middle variations of Beethoven’s Op. 109 finale.

In the Op. 109 variations the Hall’s notes ask you to hear the lower part as a melody rather than an accompaniment: two hands, independent lines, not always sharing a rhythm. In Bach’s B minor fugue at bar 9 three voices have three distinct rhythms at once.

Countersubject

A companion melody that accompanies each statement of a fugue subject, so the two lines can be heard together.

The countersubject first appears against the answer, in the voice that has just finished the subject. It usually contrasts in rhythm — slow against fast, or smooth against leaping — and is written so it works above or below the subject, because it will change places with it. The Bach fugue lets you hear this: whenever a voice has the subject, one of the others tends to carry the countersubject.

In the B minor fugue the countersubject is a line of descending crotchets that breaks into semiquavers, set against the subject’s quavers. At bar 9 the tenor carries it above the bass’s subject while the alto adds a quicker companion, so three rhythms are distinct at once.

Crescendo build

Also: crescendo; crescendos

A long, gradual increase in force that carries the music towards a climax, by adding instruments, notes, register or speed.

A build is not only “getting louder”: composers also thicken the texture, raise the register, quicken the rhythm or move the bass. The score may say crescendo, or may rely on the writing alone.

In Lawrence Odom’s band transcription of Debussy’s La Mer, quiet preparation gives way to broad chordal motion in D♭ major, with the bass joining the expanding progression. Ravel’s Boléro is a single build: the growing orchestra supplies most of the large-scale change while a two-bar rhythm and a repeated bass stay fixed.

Cyclic form

Also: cyclic; idée fixe

A work in several movements that brings back the same theme or themes in more than one of them.

Cyclic form unifies a multi-movement work by recall. A theme from one movement reappears, transformed, in another, and the finale may gather several. Deceptive cadence

Also: interrupted cadence

A dominant chord that resolves to a chord other than the tonic, usually the minor chord on the sixth degree (V–vi), so the expected close is denied.

In C major the listener hears G and expects C; the composer supplies A minor (A–C–E), which shares two notes with the tonic chord and so sounds close, but is not the arrival. The phrase has to continue, usually to a further and stronger cadence. The British name is interrupted cadence.

Equal temperament

Also: equal-tempered

The tuning that divides the octave into twelve equal semitones, so every key sounds alike and no interval except the octave is perfectly pure.

Each semitone is a frequency ratio of the twelfth root of 2, about 1.0595. Fifths come out about two cents narrower than pure, and major thirds about fourteen cents wider. The advantage is that a piece can be played in any key, and modulate to any other, without a bad-sounding interval turning up. It has been the normal tuning of the piano since the nineteenth century.

Homage

Also: tombeau; tombeaux; hommage

A piece made in honour of another composer or person, often in their style or forms; a tombeau is an homage in memory of the dead.

The tombeau began in seventeenth-century France as a lute piece, then a harpsichord piece, written on the death of a friend or patron; Johann Jacob Froberger, Louis Couperin and D’Anglebert all wrote them. The genre lapsed in the eighteenth century and was revived in the early twentieth.

Ravel’s Le Tombeau de Couperin (1914–17) takes Baroque dance forms — Prélude, Fugue, Forlane, Rigaudon, Menuet, Toccata — and dedicates each movement to the memory of friends killed in the First World War. Debussy’s Hommage à Rameau, from the first series of Images (1905), honours Rameau in a slow, chordal piece.

Lament bass

Also: lamento bass; descending tetrachord

A bass descending by step from the tonic to the dominant, a pattern that has signalled grief since the early seventeenth century.

In A minor the line runs A–G–F–E, sometimes with the chromatic steps between filled in. Metre

Also: meter; time signature

The regular grouping of beats into bars: 3/4 is three crotchet beats to the bar, 6/8 two groups of three quavers.

Metre is the pattern of strong and weak beats under the notes. Simple metre divides each beat into two (2/4, 3/4, 4/4); compound metre divides it into three (6/8, 9/8). Modelling

Also: modelled on; modelled

Taking another work’s plan — its form, its bass, its procedure — as the pattern for a new piece, without quoting its notes.

A composer who models a piece on another keeps the scaffold but writes new music on it. The model may be a form (a fugue modelled on one in the Well-Tempered Clavier), a bass (a set of variations on a known ground) or a procedure. It is the most structural kind of borrowing and the one most easily proved, since the underlying pattern can be set beside the original.

The finale of Brahms’s Fourth Symphony is modelled on the chaconne bass of the closing chorus of Bach’s Cantata No. 150. Brahms enlarged the pattern to eight bars and added a chromatic note, and the result is thirty continuous variations. The Boston Symphony programme note documents the source.

Motif

Also: motive; motifs

A short musical idea — a few notes with a recognisable rhythm or contour — that a composer repeats and transforms.

A motif is smaller than a theme and more adaptable: it can be turned upside down, stretched, shrunk, passed between instruments or placed in a different harmony without losing its identity. Beethoven built whole movements from a few of them.

Parody

Also: parodied

Two senses: in Renaissance and Baroque practice, reworking existing music into a new piece; today, imitation that mocks.

The older sense is not comic. A set of continuous variations over a repeating bass or harmonic pattern, usually in slow triple time.

The word comes from the Spanish pasacalle, a short instrumental vamp played between guitar songs in the early seventeenth century. Rubato

Also: tempo rubato

Italian “stolen”: a flexible tempo in which the performer lingers on some notes and hurries others, giving back the time within the phrase.

Rubato is rarely notated; it belongs to the player. In one form the melody moves freely while the accompaniment keeps time. Mozart described this to his father in October 1777: in a rubato in an Adagio the left hand should go on playing in strict time while the right hand yields. In a later sense, the whole texture speeds and slows together.

Because it varies between players, rubato is one reason two recordings of the same piece differ in length and in the timing of each bar.

form

Toccata

Italian “touched”: a keyboard showpiece in fast, even running notes that tests the player’s hands.

The word comes from toccare, to touch. Early toccatas were improvisatory organ and harpsichord pieces; later ones, such as Bach’s organ toccatas, alternate flourishes with fugal sections. The twentieth-century piano toccata often keeps only the motion: a stream of rapid notes in even rhythm.

Prokofiev’s Toccata in D minor, Op. 11, was written in spring 1912, dedicated to his fellow student Nikolay Shtember, and published by Jurgenson in July 1913 for seventy-five roubles. It opens with a single repeated note shared by both hands. Ravel ends Le Tombeau de Couperin with a toccata, dedicated to the memory of Joseph de Marliave.

harmony

Tonic

Also: home key

The home note of a key and the chord built on it: the point of rest that a piece leaves and returns to.

In C major the tonic is C and the tonic chord is C–E–G. Every other note and chord is heard in relation to it: the dominant pulls towards it, the subdominant moves away from it, and a cadence is the moment the tonic is confirmed. A long piece can postpone the tonic, or leave it by modulation, but its return is what most listeners hear as arrival.

At the end of Chopin’s Nocturne in D♭ major the coda settles on a D♭ bass that the left hand keeps renewing, so the home note is prolonged rather than left. Beethoven’s Fifth ends its coda with the tonic C minor confirmed only after repeated dominant tension.

transition

Tonic pedal

A held or repeated tonic note in the bass: the harmony moves above it, but the music stays at home.

Where a dominant pedal creates tension, a tonic pedal gives stability. It prolongs a place rather than leading to a new one, and often marks a coda or an introduction.

Chopin’s D♭ Nocturne ends over a D♭ bass that the coda keeps renewing, while the right hand continues its duet. In the first episode of Vivaldi’s Spring, tonic and dominant pedals on E and B hold the birdsong in one harmonic field.

influence

Transcription

Also: transcriptions

Rewriting a piece for different instruments, from keyboard to orchestra or the reverse; a transcription stays close to the original’s notes.

Liszt transcribed all nine Beethoven symphonies for solo piano between 1837 and 1864 (published in 1865), and in 1833 made a piano version of Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique, which he published in 1834. Ravel’s Pavane pour une infante défunte went the other way: Ricardo Viñes gave the piano premiere on 5 April 1902, and Ravel orchestrated it in 1910. A transcription differs from a recomposition: Rimsky-Korsakov’s 1886 concert piece Night on Bald Mountain draws on Mussorgsky’s materials but selects and recomposes them.

Several recordings in the Hall are concert-band transcriptions: Debussy’s La Mer (Lawrence Odom), Strauss’s Till Eulenspiegel (Herb Weber), the Firebird Infernal Dance (Thomas Knox) and the Waltz of the Flowers (Odom). Sibelius’s Valse triste is heard in a cello and piano arrangement.

recording

Transfer

Also: transfers

Copying a disc or tape into a digital file; the stylus, playback speed, equalisation and noise reduction chosen all shape what you hear.

An old recording reaches a listener through a transfer engineer. A disc must be played with a stylus of the right shape, at the right speed, through an equalisation curve that matches the one used when the master was cut; clicks and noise may then be reduced. Each choice changes the sound, so a transfer credit is part of a recording’s provenance. The Hall’s dossiers record who made each transfer and what is documented about its processing.

Bob Varney is credited with transfers from original Victor 78 rpm sets (Haydn’s “Surprise” Andante, Rimsky-Korsakov’s Scheherazade); stylus, playback curve, side-join locations and noise treatment are not documented. Adam Cuerden cleaned and restored the copy of Rachmaninoff’s 1919 Edison disc from audio published by the United States National Park Service; no speed-correction log is given.

texture

Tremolo

Also: tremolos

Rapid repetition of one note, on strings by very short bow strokes, to make a shimmering, unsettled sustained sound.

The bowed tremolo is the commonest: the player moves the bow back and forth as fast as possible on one note. Pianists produce a tremolo by rapidly alternating two notes, and timpanists by a roll. Tremolo gives sustained sound a tension that a held note lacks, and is often used under a climax or as a background to something more exposed.

In the coda of Beethoven’s Fifth the high C in the violins alternates between held notes and tremolo while the bass changes beneath it. The Hall’s band transcription of Debussy’s La Mer builds its closing chorale from wind and brass without the string tremolos of the orchestral original.

harmony

Tristan chord

The F–B–D♯–G♯ chord of the Prelude to Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde, whose ambiguous function made it a symbol of strained tonality.

The chord sounds in the second bar of the Prelude, in the opening phrase associated with Tristan, and is not resolved in the expected way: it moves on to another dissonance, and the music does not reach a full close until the end of the opera. Wagner completed Tristan und Isolde in 1859 and it was first performed in Munich in 1865. The notes themselves are unremarkable — respelled, F–A♭–C♭–E♭ is an ordinary half-diminished seventh — and similar chords occur earlier in Beethoven and Chopin. What is new is the context: analysts still disagree about its function.

The chord became a quotation. Berg cites it in the Lyric Suite, and Debussy jokingly quotes the opening bars of the opera in “Golliwogg’s Cakewalk” from Children’s Corner.

harmony

Twelve-note row

Also: twelve-tone row; tone row; twelve-tone; serialism

A fixed ordering of all twelve pitch classes, used as the source of a piece’s melodies and harmonies and varied by inversion and reversal.

In a twelve-note (or twelve-tone) piece, every note is drawn from a row in which each of the twelve pitch classes appears once. The row can be played forwards (prime), upside down (inversion), backwards (retrograde) and both, and transposed to any of twelve pitches, so a composer has forty-eight forms to work with. Schoenberg’s first twelve-tone movement was the Waltz of his Five Piano Pieces, Op. 23, sketched on 26 July 1921; his Suite for Piano, Op. 25 (1921–23), was the first large work unified by one row.

A theme that happens to contain all twelve notes is not a row. The subject of Bach’s B minor fugue does, but it has no system of inversion and retrograde behind it, and it is heard against a home key.

tuning

Well temperament

Also: well-tempered

A family of historical tunings in which every key is usable but each has a slightly different colour; not the same as equal temperament.

Earlier systems, such as meantone, made some keys beautifully pure and others unusable. Well temperaments, such as those described by Andreas Werckmeister in 1691, spread the compromise unevenly: keys with few sharps or flats keep purer thirds, remote keys are tenser. Bach’s Well-Tempered Clavier, Book I (1722), has a prelude and fugue in all 24 major and minor keys, which needs a tuning in which every key is usable.

The title does not say which tuning Bach used, and scholars disagree; “well-tempered” is not a synonym for “equal-tempered”. The Hall’s recording of the B minor fugue is a piano performance.

harmony

Whole-tone scale

Also: whole-tone

A six-note scale made only of whole steps, with no leading note and so no clear tonic.

Starting on C the scale is C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯. Only two different whole-tone scales exist, because every note is two semitones from the next. With no semitones and no perfect fifths, it sounds weightless, and chords built from it are augmented triads. Glinka used it in Ruslan and Lyudmila (1842) to depict the sorcerer Chernomor; Debussy made it a signature, with whole-tone runs in the clarinets and flutes of the Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune (1894).
