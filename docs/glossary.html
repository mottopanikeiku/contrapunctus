78 rpm

Also: 78s; 78 rpm disc; shellac disc

A 78 rpm record is a disc designed to turn at roughly seventy-eight revolutions per minute.

These discs were usually made from a shellac compound, and each side held only a few minutes. A symphony occupied several records, which had to be turned and changed as the music progressed. The Hall’s Koussevitzky recording of Haydn’s ‘Surprise’ Andante originally filled two sides in Victor set M-55. Its digital transfer joins them into one movement.

recording

Acoustic recording

Also: acoustic recordings; electrical recording; electrical recordings

Acoustic recording uses a horn and diaphragm to move a cutting stylus directly, without microphones.

A singer faces the recording horn; instrumentalists gather close enough for their sound to reach it. The sound shakes a diaphragm connected to a stylus, which cuts the vibrations into a wax master. Rachmaninoff recorded his C♯-minor Prelude this way for Edison in 1919. Electrical recording brought microphones and amplifiers into the process during the 1920s, allowing engineers much greater freedom in placing performers.

The composer at the keyboard in 1919: an acoustic recording on an Edison Diamond Disc. Prelude in C♯ minor, Op. 3 No. 2

An electrical recording: Koussevitzky and the Boston Symphony for Victor, 22 April 1929. Symphony No. 94 “Surprise”

Appoggiatura

Also: appoggiaturas

An appoggiatura is a leaning note that delays a chord note, usually on a strong beat, then resolves by step.

The Italian appoggiare means ‘to lean’. The melody lands on a note outside the chord and gives it emphasis before moving to a chord note. A held F over C–E–G, resolving to E, is a simple example. Unlike a suspension, the leaning note arrives afresh. In the Adagietto of Mahler’s Fifth, such delayed arrivals help the long string phrases ache.

The melody leans on notes outside the chord, then eases into chord notes by step. Symphony No. 5

Aria

Also: arias

An aria is a substantial solo song in an opera, oratorio or cantata.

An aria gives a singer room to dwell on what a character feels or wants. A Baroque da capo aria returns to its opening section after a contrasting middle, with scope for new ornaments. Atonality

Also: atonal

Atonality is music without an established home key or the usual pull of dominant chords towards a tonic.

In atonal music, a phrase can be held together by a recurring interval or rhythm while chords move freely. Schoenberg’s Second String Quartet stands at the threshold: the instrumental opening of its finale, Entrückung, suspends the sense of a home key before the soprano enters with Stefan George’s poem. Tonal chords still surface elsewhere in the movement.

Attacca

Also: attacca subito

Attacca tells performers to continue straight into the next movement or section without a break.

The instruction keeps an ending from becoming a stopping place. Mendelssohn joins the movements of his E-minor Violin Concerto: a bassoon holds B after the first movement’s closing chord, then moves to C as the Andante begins. A short passage also links the slow movement to the finale.

harmony

Augmented sixth chord

Also: augmented sixth; German sixth; Italian sixth; French sixth

An augmented sixth chord contains two notes that usually move outwards by a semitone to an octave on the dominant.

In C major or minor, the crucial notes are A♭ below and F♯ above. A♭ falls to G while F♯ rises to G, opening the augmented sixth into an octave. The Italian sixth adds C between them. The French version also adds D; the German version adds E♭. Those inner notes change the sound while the two outer notes keep their pull towards G.

Bar

Also: bars; bar number; bar numbers

A bar is a span of music between bar lines, normally containing the number of beats shown by the time signature.

American musicians call it a measure. Bar numbers give players a shared address in the score. Repeats mean that one address can be visited twice: the exposition of Beethoven’s Fifth runs from bars 1–124, then starts again in this recording. Basset clarinet

Also: basset clarinets

A basset clarinet extends the clarinet’s range downwards to written low C.

The extra length and keys add a major third below the ordinary clarinet’s lowest note, written E. Mozart wrote his Clarinet Concerto for Anton Stadler’s extended instrument. Its low passages can dip further beneath the melody, giving the soloist a wider range of colour. In the Hall’s Adagio recording, William McColl plays a basset clarinet he built himself.

Basso continuo

Also: continuo; figured bass; thoroughbass

Basso continuo is a bass line with chords supplied by a keyboard or plucked-string player, often joined by a bass instrument.

A harpsichordist, organist or lutenist builds chords above the written bass. Figures beneath the notes can specify the intervals: a 6 calls for a sixth above the bass, for instance. Binary form

Also: rounded binary

Binary form divides a piece into two main sections, usually repeated, with the second bringing the music home.

Binary form has two sections, commonly marked with repeat signs. The first often ends away from the home key; the second finds its way back. Scarlatti’s Sonata in D major, K. 119, reaches A major at the end of its first half. Taking the repeat jumps straight from that A-major close back to D. In rounded binary form, the opening tune also returns within the second half.

Bitonality

Also: polytonality; bitonal

Bitonality places two keys side by side in the same passage.

One part of the music asserts one key while another asserts a different one. Stravinsky’s Petrushka provides the famous chordal example: C–E–G, a C-major triad, sounds against F♯–A♯–C♯, an F♯-major triad. The two chords are a tritone apart. Their collision belongs to the unhappy puppet’s music in the ballet’s second scene.

Cadence

Also: cadences

A cadence is a melodic and harmonic arrival that marks the end of a phrase or section.

A cadence gives a phrase somewhere to land. Dominant to tonic can make a firm close; a stop on the dominant leaves the phrase waiting. The final cadence of Bach’s B minor fugue reaches B major. Its D♯ replaces the D♮ of the minor key, a brightening called a Picardy third, and the last chord rests under a fermata.

Cadenza

Also: cadenzas

A cadenza is a solo display, usually unaccompanied, within a concerto, aria or other work.

A cadenza can be improvised or written out, and may turn the movement’s themes into scales, trills and other feats of playing. Bach gives the harpsichord a long solo near the end of the Fifth Brandenburg Concerto’s first movement. Schumann writes a cadenza into his Piano Concerto. texture

Canon

A canon is a melody followed by another voice that keeps imitating it at a fixed distance in time.

One voice leads and another follows. In a round such as Frère Jacques, each group sings the same notes, entering later and continuing while the next group begins. More elaborate canons start the following voice at another pitch, turn the melody upside down or stretch its note values. Bach’s Goldberg Variations includes nine canons, with the distance between their entering voices increasing from unison to ninth.

Catalogue number

Also: catalogue numbers; opus number; opus numbers; BWV

A catalogue number identifies a particular work within a composer’s output.

An opus number often reflects publication order. Other numbers come from catalogues: BWV for Bach, K. for Mozart, D. for Schubert and Wq for C. P. E. Bach. The composer’s name matters. Mozart’s K. numbers refer to Köchel’s catalogue; Scarlatti’s refer to Kirkpatrick’s. Thus Mozart’s Symphony No. 40 is K. 550, while Scarlatti’s D-major keyboard sonata heard in the Hall is K. 119.

Chaconne

A chaconne is a set of variations built on a recurring bass or chord pattern, usually in triple time.

A chaconne keeps returning to the same short foundation while the upper parts find new things to say. Bach’s D-minor Chaconne fills the last movement of his Second Partita for solo violin. His Cantata No. 150 ends with another chaconne; Brahms adapted its bass for the finale of his Fourth Symphony, stretching the pattern to eight bars and adding a chromatic note.

Chromatic slide

A chromatic slide shifts a note or chord by a semitone into a new harmonic setting.

B major begins to emerge, with left-hand triplets under the chords. The semitone in the bass leads into a brighter, more flowing passage.

Chromaticism

Also: chromatic

Chromaticism uses notes outside the prevailing scale, often bringing semitone movement into melodies and chords.

In C major, F♯ is a chromatic note; a melody rising F–F♯–G passes through it between two notes of the scale. Chromatic notes can briefly sharpen a phrase or gradually loosen its hold on a key. Bach’s B minor fugue fits all twelve notes into its subject, joining little semitone pairs with wide leaps. The fugue still has B minor as its home.

Clavichord

A clavichord is a quiet, touch-sensitive keyboard instrument whose metal tangents strike the strings and remain against them.

The tangent stays in contact for as long as the key is held. A player can vary the attack and gently rock the key to produce Bebung, a small vibrato. In Hamburg in 1772, Charles Burney persuaded C. P. E. Bach to play after dinner; Bach continued until nearly eleven at night. Joan Benson plays the F♯-minor Fantasia, Wq 67, on clavichord in the Hall.

Coda

A coda is a closing passage added after a piece’s main sections, ranging from a few chords to a substantial final section.

Coda is Italian for ‘tail’. It follows the last main section of a movement and can do much more than round things off. In Beethoven’s Fifth, the first movement’s coda occupies bars 374–502, almost as long as the exposition. The violins hold C while the bass changes beneath them; the four-note motto still has work to do. Chopin’s First Ballade ends with a headlong coda marked Presto con fuoco.

Col legno

Col legno tells string players to use the wooden stick of the bow against the strings.

Col legno battuto means tapping the strings with the stick, which makes a dry, pitched rattle. Col legno tratto means drawing the wood across them, producing a faint, rough sound. Berlioz asks for the tapping kind in the Witches’ Sabbath finale of Symphonie fantastique. The strings join the grotesque dance with the clatter of their bows.

Common-tone modulation

Also: common tone modulation

Common-tone modulation connects keys through a note that belongs to both the departing and arriving harmonies.

A held note stays in place while its surroundings change. C, for example, is the root of C major and the third of A♭ major. Fauré uses that shared pitch in the Agnus Dei of his Requiem. The sopranos hold C alone; when the other voices enter beneath them, the note belongs to A♭ major and Lux aeterna has begun.

Concert pitch

Also: A=440; A = 440; A440; A=415; A = 415; pitch standard

Concert pitch is the agreed reference pitch to which musicians tune their instruments.

The reference is usually A above middle C. At A=440, that note vibrates 440 times a second; ISO 16 sets this international standard. Ensembles can choose another pitch, especially when playing historical instruments. Tuning pitch establishes how high the music sounds, while temperament determines the sizes of the intervals within it.

Concerto

Also: concertos

A concerto brings one or more soloists into musical partnership and competition with an orchestra.

The soloist may lead, answer the orchestra or step out alone in a cadenza. Bach’s Fifth Brandenburg Concerto has three solo instruments: flute, violin and harpsichord. Counterpoint

Also: contrapuntal

Counterpoint combines melodic lines that keep their own shape and rhythm while sounding together.

Each part has a tune worth hearing on its own, and each affects the others. One may hold a note while another moves, or answer a rising phrase with a descent. Countersubject

A countersubject is a recurring melody designed to accompany a fugue’s subject or answer.

When a fugue’s first voice finishes the subject, it may begin a second melody against the next voice’s answer. If this companion returns with later entries, it is a countersubject. A crescendo build is a passage that grows gradually louder towards a climax.

The score may mark cresc. or show a hairpin opening from left to right. A composer can strengthen the rise by bringing in more players or moving the melody higher. Cyclic form

Also: cyclic; idée fixe

Cyclic form links the movements of a work by bringing back themes heard in earlier movements.

A familiar theme returns in a new setting, carrying a memory of what came before. In Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique, the melody representing the beloved appears in all five movements. Deceptive cadence

Also: interrupted cadence

A deceptive cadence sends the dominant to an unexpected chord, usually the chord on the sixth degree, delaying the close.

In C major, G–B–D seems ready to resolve to C–E–G. An A-minor chord arrives instead: A–C–E. The melody can still reach C, but the bass has moved to A, leaving the phrase open for more music. In a minor key, the usual sixth-degree chord is major. British musicians also call this an interrupted cadence.

The dominant is the fifth note of a key, or the chord built on it, which commonly leads back to the tonic.

Enharmonic reinterpretation

Also: enharmonic

Enharmonic reinterpretation gives a note or chord a new spelling and harmonic role while keeping its sounding pitch.

D♭ and C♯ use the same piano key, yet their spellings suggest different routes through a score. A composer can make one become the other at a turning point. Episode

An episode is a passage between statements of a fugue subject or returns of a refrain.

In a fugue, an episode usually works with fragments of the subject or its companions and often changes key. Bach’s B minor fugue has a descending bass sequence in its first episode, with quicker imitations above. In a rondo, episodes contrast with the returning tune. In Vivaldi’s Spring, the first solo episode sets three violins exchanging little trills and calls: the birds of the accompanying sonnet.

tuning

Equal temperament

Also: equal-tempered

Twelve-note equal temperament divides the octave into twelve equal semitones, giving every key the same pattern of intervals.

A melody moved from C major to D♭ major keeps exactly the same interval sizes in this tuning. The compromise is small but audible in chords: a major third is wider than a pure third, and a fifth is slightly narrower than a pure fifth. Rubato is expressive flexibility of timing, with some notes lingering and others moving ahead.

A whole-tone scale has six notes, with a whole tone between every neighbouring pair.
