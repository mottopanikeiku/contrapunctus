iMelodies together

Counterpoint: room for another voice

Each tune keeps its shape while its notes meet those of another.

Counterpoint combines melodies that can each hold our attention. In Couperin’s La Piémontoise, violin and flute pass short phrases between them while the bass pursues its own line. A note belongs to two things at once: the tune moving through time and the chord sounding at that instant.

Johann Joseph Fux made a classroom out of this problem in Gradus ad Parnassum, published in Vienna in 1725. His imaginary teacher, Aloysius, represents Palestrina; the pupil, Josephus, keeps trying examples. The first exercise adds one note against each long note of a given melody, the cantus firmus. Every meeting must be consonant: a unison, fifth, octave, third or sixth. Consecutive parallel fifths and octaves are forbidden; thirds and sixths can travel together.

Five speciesFux proceeds from one note against one to two against one, then four against one. The fourth species uses tied notes; the fifth mixes the preceding rhythms. These are exercises in controlling two parts.

Rhythm makes room for a clash. In second species, a passing note on the weak half of the bar can move by step between consonances. In fourth species, a note that fitted the previous chord is held while the bass changes beneath it. It now rubs against the new harmony, then falls one step: a suspension. A held F over C can resolve to E; the fourth becomes a third.

The fourth’s special caseIn this teaching tradition, a fourth above the bass is dissonant. A fourth between two upper parts can be consonant. The lowest note makes a difference.

Invertible counterpoint lets complete lines exchange places. In the second example, E–F starts above C–D; moving C–D up an octave turns the thirds into sixths. A fifth would turn into a fourth and require care. This is the craft behind a fugue’s recurring companions: in Bach’s C minor Fugue, BWV 847, the subject and countersubjects keep meeting in new positions.

Fux’s Gradus ad Parnassum, printed in Vienna in 1725, puts an imaginary pupil and teacher into conversation. Josephus brings his attempts; Aloysius, modelled on Palestrina, corrects them. Behind this imposing Latin title page is a lesson conducted one note at a time.

iiOne tune, several voices

Fugue: the tune comes back with company

A new entrance adds a voice without silencing the ones already singing.

A fugue lets us learn a tune before surrounding it with other tunes. In bars 1–2 of Bach’s C major Fugue, BWV 846, an inner voice climbs C–D–E–F, curls round G and carries on. This is the subject. During bar 2 a higher voice starts the same tune on G. The first voice keeps moving beneath it.

Voices on a keyboard“Alto”, “soprano”, “tenor” and “bass” name the four lines, from their positions on the staff. One player supplies all four. The score example isolates the opening alto.

The second entrance is the answer, usually starting a fifth above or a fourth below the subject. A real answer preserves every interval; a tonal answer adjusts some intervals to keep the exchange close to the home key. BWV 846 has a real answer. Its opening entries arrive in the unusual order subject, answer, answer, subject: alto, soprano, tenor, bass.

A recurring tune that accompanies the subject is a countersubject. It often has to work both above and below its partner, using invertible counterpoint. BWV 846 has no regular countersubject. Bach’s C minor Fugue, BWV 847, has two; by the end of its eight-bar exposition, all three voices have entered. Bar 9 begins an episode, where fragments of the subject move through a sequence without a complete statement of the tune.

Other ways to alter a subjectAugmentation stretches its note lengths. Melodic inversion turns upward intervals downward and downward intervals upward. Neither operation requires a change of tempo.

In stretto, one voice starts the subject before another has finished it. The C major Fugue is full of these overlaps after its opening entries. The tune has to fit against a delayed copy of itself, sometimes several copies. Bach crowds the conversation without needing a new theme.

iiiThe accompanist’s shorthand

Figured bass: a chord in a few figures

Numbers above the bass leave the player room to make the accompaniment.

Figured bass tells an accompanist which notes belong above a written bass line. In the opening of Couperin’s La Piémontoise, four players share three lines: violin and flute take the upper parts, while gamba and keyboard share the bass. The keyboard player also supplies chords, choosing how widely to spread them and when to repeat them. That shared foundation is the basso continuo.

Counting upwardsA 6 over C calls for A, six letter names above C. A 6 over E calls for C. The key signature supplies the appropriate sharps or flats.

A bare bass note usually implies a third and fifth above it. The figure 6 normally means a third and sixth: over E in C major, G and C make a C-major chord with its third at the bottom. A 6/4 over G calls for C and E. At a cadence those notes can fall to B and D while G stays put. The figures 6–5 and 4–3 describe the two downward steps.

An accidental beside a figure alters that interval; an accidental on its own usually alters the third. The figures still leave the player plenty to decide. E–G–C and G–C–E fulfil the same 6 over a bass E, but their top lines lead to different places. A singer holding a dissonant note may need a spare chord beneath it; a long instrumental note may leave room for a little answering phrase.

Two kinds of shorthandThe modern symbol “C/E” names a C-major chord over E. A continuo figure 6 over E names the intervals above that bass. Both can lead to the same notes.

In the example, the right hand holds E–G–C while the bass moves from C to E. The chord keeps its name, but the figures change. At the end, B rises to C and F falls to E: the accompanist has made the individual voices lead into the final chord.

ivTuning the keyboard

Temperament: the fifths will not quite fit

A keyboard tuner has to share out a small, unavoidable discrepancy.

A perfectly tuned fifth and a perfectly tuned major third ask different things of a keyboard. Four pure fifths, C–G–D–A–E, produce an E that is noticeably higher than the E of a pure C-major third, once the octaves are brought together. A tuner must choose where to make the adjustment. That compromise is a temperament.

The arithmeticA pure fifth has the frequency ratio 3:2; a pure major third, 5:4. Four pure fifths, reduced by two octaves, give 81:64. The difference from 5:4 is the syntonic comma, 81:80, about 21.51 cents. A cent is one hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone.

The fifths also refuse to close their own circle. Twelve pure fifths overshoot seven octaves by about 23.46 cents: the Pythagorean comma, 531441:524288. Quarter-comma meantone narrows each regular fifth by about 5.38 cents, making its major thirds pure. On a conventional twelve-note keyboard, the closing interval then howls: the “wolf”. G♯ and A♭ really want separate keys; some old keyboards provide them.

Unequal well temperaments spread the adjustments so that all twenty-four major and minor keys are usable, while their thirds and fifths differ in size. Equal temperament divides the octave into twelve equal semitones, each with the ratio 21/12. Its fifth is 700 cents; its major third is 400 cents, wider than pure by about 13.69 cents. Bach’s Well-Tempered Clavier ranges through every key, from its opening C major to the closing B minor.

Bach’s tuningThe title promises access to every key. It leaves the exact temperament unspecified; proposed reconstructions of Bach’s tuning differ.

The buttons below make the compromise audible. Each pair of notes contains upper tones, called partials. When two partials nearly coincide, they produce a regular throbbing, or beating. Over the low A used here, the equal-tempered fifth beats slowly; the equal-tempered major third beats much faster. With the pure ratios, the corresponding partials coincide and that particular beating stops.

vChords finding home

Harmony: why G7 wants to reach C

A chord’s smallest movements can make an arrival feel inevitable.

Harmony gives a chord a place to go. In bars 1–4 of Bach’s C major Prelude, BWV 846, the right hand keeps rippling while the harmony moves from C major through D minor seventh and G7, then back to C. The bass slips from C to B and returns. On the way home, F falls to E. Two little steps make the return persuasive.

Tonic and dominantC–E–G is the tonic chord in C major, written I. G–B–D is the dominant, V. Adding F makes G7: its B usually rises to C while F falls to E. The interval B–F is a tritone.

A cadence closes a phrase. A dominant-to-tonic arrival, V–I, is an authentic cadence; its strongest textbook form puts both chord roots in the bass and ends with the tonic on top. A phrase ending on V makes a half cadence, leaving the door open. A deceptive cadence sends V somewhere unexpected, often to A minor in the key of C. IV–I, F major to C major, makes the plagal close.

Chords also prepare those arrivals. F major or D minor can lead into G7: the first chord is a predominant, preparing the dominant. A chain of descending fifths can extend the approach: E–A–D–G–C. Within a key, the chords take their notes from its scale, so the chain includes minor chords and sometimes a diminished fifth between roots.

From a chord to a keyA tonicisation briefly treats another chord as home. A modulation establishes a new key through a longer passage, often confirmed by a cadence. C major and G major share six scale notes; F♯ distinguishes G major.

The second example uses A minor as a hinge. It is vi in C major and ii in G major. D7 then introduces F♯, which rises to G as the chord’s C falls to B. The same movement that brought Bach’s prelude home to C now brings this little progression to G.

viDeparture and return

Sonata form: bringing the second theme home

Beethoven brings back a familiar melody in a key it has never had before.

Sonata form makes a departure from the home key matter when the music returns. In the first movement of Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony, the opening is in C minor. At bar 63 the first violins begin a quiet melody in E♭ major, marked dolce — sweetly. The same melody returns at bar 307 in C major. It has come home to C without taking on the opening’s minor third.

ExpositionThe opening section establishes the home key and a contrasting key. In a major-key movement, the new key is often the dominant; in a minor-key movement, often the relative major. C minor and E♭ major share a key signature.

The development works on what the exposition has introduced. Beethoven starts his at bar 125, after the exposition repeat. The horn call returns in altered form; strings answer with D♭ falling to C. Later, the four-note rhythm shrinks to isolated chords passed between winds and strings. A tune that once filled a phrase can be reduced to one stubborn fragment.

The recapitulation brings back the principal themes and changes the route between them. Beethoven’s second group now reaches C major, with bassoons taking the call previously played by horns. Beneath the violin melody, the opening rhythm still knocks away. The coda, the movement’s closing section, has more work to do: the Fifth’s first movement eventually ends in C minor.

A different destinationIn the Waldstein Sonata, Op. 53, Beethoven sends the C-major exposition to E major. At the return, the second group first appears in A major before reaching C.

A second tune is only part of the story. Sonata form can use the same theme in both key areas; the crucial change is where the music settles. In the Fifth, even the new violin song carries the old four-note rhythm underneath it. At bars 65–66, the cellos play three short B♭s and a longer E♭.

viiSharps and flats at work

Chromaticism: the force of a semitone

A single altered note can turn a familiar chord towards a new destination.

Chromatic notes lie outside the scale of the current key. In Chopin’s E minor Prelude, Op. 28 No. 4, the left-hand chords sink by small steps beneath a melody that scarcely moves. During bars 1–4 the bass falls G–F♯–F–E. F♮ comes from outside E minor’s ordinary scale; the semitone descent makes it sound like the next unavoidable step.

A passing noteC–C♯–D can simply fill the gap between C and D. Establishing D as a new key takes more than that one sharp.

Some chromatic chords have familiar jobs. In C minor, D♭–F–A♭ is the Neapolitan, a major chord on the lowered second scale degree. It usually appears with F in the bass and prepares the dominant, G. An augmented-sixth chord approaches that G from both sides: A♭ falls and F♯ rises, opening out into an octave.

Three augmented sixthsIn C, A♭–C–F♯ is the Italian form. Adding D makes the French; adding E♭ makes the German. The German commonly passes through C minor over a G bass before reaching G major, avoiding the parallel fifths of a direct resolution.

Spelling tells a player where the notes are going. A♭–C–E♭–F♯ can lead towards G as a German sixth in C. Written A♭–C–E♭–G♭, the same piano keys make A♭7, which can resolve to D♭. This enharmonic reinterpretation turns one sounding chord into a junction between keys.

A diminished seventh offers several such junctions. B–D–F–A♭ can lead into C minor. With A♭ rewritten as G♯, the chord becomes G♯–B–D–F and can lead into A minor instead. The two versions in the second example sound identical on an equal-tempered piano. Their endings reveal which note was the leading note: B rising to C, or G♯ rising to A.

viiiAn unfinished sentence

The Tristan chord: a resolution that keeps us waiting

Wagner reaches the dominant and leaves its promise unanswered.

The Tristan chord is the first harmony in Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde. After the cellos’ opening phrase, F–B–D♯–G♯ sounds in bar 2. The oboe’s G♯ begins to climb. By bar 3 the harmony has reached E7, the dominant of A minor, and an A-minor chord would make an entirely plausible next step. Wagner leaves a silence.

Four notesAbove the bass F, B makes an augmented fourth, D♯ an augmented sixth and the high G♯ an augmented ninth. Rewritten F–A♭–C♭–E♭, the same piano keys would form a half-diminished seventh.

One influential explanation treats G♯ as an appoggiatura, a note leaning towards its neighbour A. That A would complete F–A–B–D♯, a French augmented sixth preparing E. The familiar chord is there, just beneath the surface, but the oboe keeps climbing G♯–A–A♯–B. Its four-note phrase pulls across the changing harmony.

Two readingsThe appoggiatura reading explains the chord’s tonal job. John Rothgeb’s analysis treats the whole G♯–A–A♯–B ascent as an ornament arriving early over the F bass. Both readings depend on what the voices do across bars 2–3.

The second chord is still waiting for a resolution of its own. That is the opening’s trick: the first ache has eased, and another has taken its place. The pause lets E7 hang in the air before the phrase begins again at a new pitch.

Debussy later brings this opening into Golliwogg’s Cake-walk, from Children’s Corner. The piano suddenly adopts Wagner’s yearning manner in the middle of a dance. The quotation is recognisable because of the reaching phrase and its pause as much as the four notes of the chord.

ixThe orchestra remembers

Leitmotif: a tune with a past

A returning phrase can bring an absent person or object into the scene.

A leitmotif is a recurring musical idea with a dramatic association. In Wagner’s Ring, the sword Nothung has music that can summon it before anyone draws a blade. The same idea accompanies a weapon promised, possessed, broken and reforged across Das Rheingold, Die Walküre and Siegfried. The orchestra remembers more than the characters say.

The names came laterCommentators, especially Hans von Wolzogen, supplied many of the familiar labels for Wagner’s motives. The score can transform an idea long after its first dramatic association.

A motive survives changes because something distinctive remains: perhaps its opening leap or the spacing of its long and short notes. Transposition moves it to another pitch. Augmentation lengthens its notes in proportion. Reharmonisation puts different chords beneath the tune, so a phrase once heard over a major chord can return with a darker bass.

In Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique (1830), the beloved has a recurring melody, the idée fixe. It appears in all five movements. In the finale, a high E♭ clarinet twists it into a dance with trills: the beloved has joined the witches’ sabbath. The tune is familiar enough for the caricature to hurt.

Transformation and associationA melody can change without representing a character. Liszt’s thematic transformation also works in instrumental forms: a theme’s intervals remain recognisable as its pace or metre changes.

The invented C–E–G figure below keeps its rising outline in each version. E♭ turns its first leap into a minor third. Halved note lengths make it run twice as fast; doubled lengths stretch it across two bars. A composer could attach any of these versions to the sword’s next appearance on stage.

Mime bends over his anvil in Rackham’s 1911 illustration for Siegfried. He can make swords, but he cannot mend Nothung. Siegfried will succeed where he fails. In the opera, the sword’s returning music brings its earlier history into this smoky workshop.

xAnother note feels like home

Modes: the white keys change their meaning

The same white keys sound different with D as their resting place.

A mode gives a familiar set of notes a different centre. The piano’s white keys make C major when C feels like home. With D as the resting note, they make D Dorian: D–E–F–G–A–B–C. F gives it a minor third, while B♮ gives it a major sixth. Ordinary D natural minor has B♭.

Establishing the centreThe first note of a scale exercise can suggest a centre. In a piece, a held bass, repeated notes and phrase endings help establish it. Seven white keys alone cannot tell us which note is home.

The other modes alter different degrees. E Phrygian begins with the close semitone E–F. F Lydian has B♮, a raised fourth compared with F major. G Mixolydian has F♮, a lowered seventh compared with G major. Transposing a mode preserves these distances: C Lydian needs F♯; D Mixolydian needs F♯ and C♮.

In Debussy’s L’isle joyeuse (1904), the broad second theme begins at bar 67 around A major, with the D♯ of A Lydian lighting its harmony. A Lydian contains A–B–C♯–D♯–E–F♯–G♯. Lowering that G♯ to G produces the acoustic scale, another of Debussy’s resources. One altered note connects two scales while A remains in place.

Older meaningsMedieval church modes classify a chant by its final note, range and reciting tone as well as its pattern of steps. Modern uses of Dorian or Phrygian usually describe the interval pattern; the older system has further distinctions, including authentic and plagal forms.

The examples put two minor-third modes beside two major-third modes. Dorian’s B and Phrygian’s F sit in very different places above their centres. In the second pair, C Lydian reaches towards F♯ while G Mixolydian turns down towards F♮.

xiA scale without semitones

Whole-tone harmony: six equal steps

Six equal steps give Debussy a scale without a leading note.

The whole-tone scale has six notes, each a whole tone from the next: C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯, then C again. There are no semitones and no perfect fifths. C has the same neighbours, at the same distances, as every other note. A long bass note can still make one pitch stand out: Debussy’s Voiles keeps returning to low B♭.

Two possible collectionsA semitone transposition produces the other whole-tone collection. Another semitone returns to the original six notes, in a new order. Messiaen called the whole-tone scale his first mode of limited transposition.

Alternate notes make an augmented triad: C–E–G♯, three major thirds dividing the octave. The remaining notes make D–F♯–A♯. Both chords belong entirely to the scale. They can slide up and down together without acquiring the leading note and perfect fifth of a conventional dominant chord.

Debussy’s Voiles keeps to whole-tone writing for bars 1–41. At bar 42 the piano breaks into a pentatonic passage on the five black keys. Suddenly there are unequal gaps, including minor thirds. The whole-tone notes return at bar 48; those six pentatonic bars have been the piece’s one sustained escape.

VoilesThe French title can mean sails or veils. Debussy printed it after the final bar of the prelude.

In the example, the scale becomes two chords. C–E–G♯ and D–F♯–A♯ contain all six notes between them, without overlap. Neither chord supplies a B that can rise by a semitone into C. The bass, the rhythm and the placement of a phrase ending must do that work instead.

xiiSemitone, tone, semitone

Octatonic harmony: eight notes with several centres

Two perfectly ordinary major chords can belong to one unfamiliar scale.

An octatonic scale alternates semitones and whole tones. From C, one version runs C–D♭–E♭–E–F♯–G–A–B♭, then back to C. Its eight notes include C major’s C–E–G and F♯ major’s F♯–A♯–C♯, with A♯ and C♯ respelled as B♭ and D♭. Two chords a tritone apart can therefore share the same scale.

Three possible collectionsMoving an octatonic collection up a minor third reproduces its original notes. Only the starting position and the transpositions one and two semitones above it give distinct collections. Messiaen calls the octatonic scale his second mode of limited transposition.

The same eight notes divide into two diminished-seventh chords, each built from minor thirds. In the example they are F♯–A–C–E♭ and C♯–E–G–B♭. Moving one chord up a minor third simply rearranges its notes. Moving it a semitone produces the other chord.

Stravinsky brings C major and F♯ major together in the second tableau of Petrushka. Clarinets turn the two triads into the puppet’s awkward, protesting voice. Their six notes all fit the octatonic collection printed here. At the end of the third tableau, versions on A and E♭ add the remaining notes.

A chord within a scaleThe “Petrushka chord” contains six different notes. The full octatonic collection contains eight. The two triads remain audible as separate shapes even when their notes belong to a common scale.

Whole-tone harmony removes semitones; octatonic harmony has four of them spaced around the octave. C–D♭, E♭–E, F♯–G and A–B♭ can each make a close, urgent pair. A repeated bass or a strongly accented chord can bring one pair to the foreground while the others remain available.

xiiiBeyond the home key

Atonality: connections without a tonic

Small groups of notes can bind a piece together without a dominant-to-tonic cadence.

Atonal music leaves the familiar rule of tonic and dominant behind. At the opening of the finale of Schoenberg’s Second String Quartet, the strings move through shifting chromatic lines before a soprano joins them. The movement’s title is Entrückung, rapture. Its harmonies can lose all sense of a home key, even though tonal cadences return elsewhere in the movement.

Before the rowThe quartet dates from 1907–08; the Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11, from 1909. Schoenberg developed his twelve-tone method later. Atonal music can be composed without a twelve-note row.

A recurring group of intervals can hold such music together. The invented C–C♯–F figure below rises one semitone, then four. G–G♯–C repeats those distances a fifth higher. C–B–G turns both movements downwards. The notes have changed, but the small step followed by the larger one remains recognisable.

A composer can spread those notes across the keyboard or sound them together as a chord. Pitch-class analysis sets aside the octave differences to describe such relationships: every C belongs to one pitch class. The ear also hears how far apart the notes are, which instrument plays them and how long the gaps last. A high violin harmonic and a low cello note can belong to the same pitch class and have very different effects.

Consonant intervals remainA major third or perfect fifth can occur inside an atonal passage. An isolated C–E–G chord establishes a key only when the surrounding music supports C as tonic.

On 2 January 1911, Kandinsky heard Schoenberg’s music at a concert in Munich. Soon afterwards he painted Impression III (Concert): the piano is still a black shape, but yellow seems to pour out across the room.

Kandinsky attended a Schoenberg concert in Munich on 2 January 1911, with Franz Marc among his companions. He painted Impression III (Concert) soon afterwards. The black grand piano and the bent backs of the audience survive inside the yellow flood. The Lenbachhaus account also identifies them in the preparatory drawings.

xivTwelve notes in order

Twelve-tone writing: one row, many phrases

Schoenberg fixes the order of the pitches and leaves their rhythm free.

Twelve-tone writing starts with an ordering of all twelve chromatic pitch classes, each used once. Schoenberg’s Suite for Piano, Op. 25, uses E–F–G–D♭–G♭–E♭–A♭–D–B–C–A–B♭. That row can supply a melody, be divided between several voices or gather into chords. It fixes neither the note lengths nor the octave in which each note sounds.

Bach in the rowThe last four notes, read backwards, are B♭–A–C–B♮. In German notation, B means B♭ and H means B♮: the row can spell B–A–C–H.

The row has four standard forms. The prime gives the chosen order. Inversion reverses each interval’s direction, so E–F becomes E–E♭. Retrograde reads the order backwards; retrograde inversion reads the inverted order backwards. Each form can begin at twelve pitch levels, making forty-eight possibilities before any duplicates caused by a row’s symmetry.

Schoenberg uses only eight of those possibilities in Op. 25: the four forms and their transpositions by a tritone. The restriction leaves plenty of room for dancing. His Gavotte and Musette have different characters, and the Prelude starts driving ahead with repeated pitches in bar 3. A row’s ordering survives immediate repetitions of a note; it is no ban on repeating what has just sounded.

The examplesThe prime and inversion both begin on E. Equal crotchets make the order clear; the barlines group the notes in fours. These rhythms and octave positions are schematic.

Writing to Nicolas Slonimsky on 3 June 1937, Schoenberg recalled wanting “a uniform musical idea” that could govern both themes and their accompaniment. Op. 25, composed in 1921–23, gives that wish a practical form. The same row lies behind a calm low melody in the Intermezzo and the Gigue’s sharply accented rush.

xvWhere the accents fall

Rhythm: two beats can become three

The smallest pulse can stay steady while the larger groups pull against one another.

Rhythm is the pattern of notes and silences; metre groups them into beats and bars. The plucked opening of Ravel’s String Quartet scherzo pulls six quavers in two directions: two groups of three, as in 6/8, and three groups of two, as in 3/4. The little notes keep their speed while the larger beats disagree.

HemiolaSix equal units can be regrouped from 3+3 to 2+2+2. Across two bars of 3/4, three minims can replace the expected two groups of three crotchets. The span has the same length; the accents have moved.

Additive rhythm joins unequal groups. In Bartók’s first Dance in Bulgarian Rhythm, No. 148 of Mikrokosmos, nine quavers divide 4+2+3. The next dance uses 2+2+3. The short and long groups give each bar its particular gait, with the quaver running steadily underneath.

Messiaen’s added values disturb a pattern from within. A short note, rest or dot lengthens part of a familiar rhythm. In the example, two quavers plus two become three plus two: one extra quaver delays the next beginning. His non-retrogradable rhythms have a different property. A sequence such as 2–3–2 has the same durations forwards and backwards.

Changing barsIn the Rite of Spring’s Sacrificial Dance, Stravinsky uses changing bar lengths to make accents return at uneven distances. Elsewhere in the ballet, he shifts accents inside an unchanged metre.

Polyrhythm sounds different divisions of one span at once. In three against two, the upper part places three evenly spaced notes against two below. Dividing the span into six little units puts the upper attacks at 0, 2 and 4, and the lower ones at 0 and 3. They meet again at 6. In the second example, only the first note of each bar is attacked together.

xviChoosing the player

Orchestration: the flute hands over its tune

The notes can stay the same while a new instrument changes their character.

Orchestration chooses who plays each note. In Ravel’s Boléro, the first tune passes from solo flute to clarinet while the snare drum repeats its two-bar rhythm. The melody is familiar on its second appearance, but its voice has changed. Later, the second tune climbs into the bassoon’s high range, where every note sounds more exposed.

RegisterThe same instrument changes character across its range. A low flute can be easily covered by other players; a high flute can cut through. A melody’s octave is part of its orchestration.

Ravel also makes new voices by combining instruments. A horn carries the tune with celesta and piccolos sounding above it. The separate parts suggest the upper tones of one composite instrument. Elsewhere, a muted trumpet shares a statement with a flute, and a high trombone solo turns the line into something closer to a sliding human voice.

How a note begins matters as much as who sustains it. A plucked violin string starts sharply and dies away; a bowed note can grow after its beginning. A mute changes the sound again. Sul ponticello brings the bow close to the bridge; col legno uses its wood. Wind multiphonics use special fingerings and ways of blowing to sound several pitches together.

More players, less clarityDoubling a line can make it stronger, but another instrument’s attack may blur its edges. The composer also has to leave space around the melody: a quiet solo flute will struggle against a full brass chord.

Boléro keeps its two tunes and its repeating bass in place for almost the whole piece, so each new player is conspicuous. Eventually whole sections take the melody, and the orchestra grows enormous around that little drum rhythm. The example below reduces the experiment to two solo sounds: a flute plays a phrase, then an oboe plays the same notes.

  • A solo flute plays the first tune over the snare drum and the bass’s alternating C and G. Boléro

  • A clarinet takes the same tune while the drum and bass continue their pattern. Boléro

xviiA pattern begins to change

Minimalism: two players slip apart

A repeated figure can generate new music when its partner moves one note ahead.

In late 1966, Steve Reich made a tape loop of a short piano pattern and tried playing along with it. He had discovered phasing with tape recorders; now he wanted human hands to do it. Piano Phase (1967) begins with two pianists repeating the same figure together. One speeds up slightly until the parts settle one note apart. Their overlapping notes make patterns neither pianist plays alone.

The phase shiftThe moving player returns to the other player’s speed at each new alignment. The process continues until the two patterns meet in unison again. The first example shows two settled positions, with an abrupt jump between them.

Terry Riley gives performers a different freedom in In C (1964). Everyone has the same fifty-three patterns in the same order, but each player decides how many times to repeat one before moving on. Riley asks the players to stay within two or three patterns of one another. A repeated high C can keep the quaver pulse while neighbouring patterns interlock.

Philip Glass’s Two Pages lengthens and shortens a repeating line. This is additive process: a figure returns with an extra piece attached, or with something removed. An invented C–D–E could become C–D–E–G, then C–D–E–G–A. The notes keep their speed, but the beginning comes round later.

A different use of few notesPärt’s tintinnabuli joins a melodic line to a second voice drawn from a fixed triad. Its rules decide which triad note sits beside each melody note.

In Pärt’s Für Alina (1976), the piano pairs a free-moving melody with notes of the B-minor triad. Phrases gain a note at a time, then lose them again. The second example below invents a simpler rule in A minor: each melody note receives the nearest strictly lower A, C or E. When the melody reaches F, the partner is E, leaving a bare semitone between them.

xviiiThe notes inside a note

Spectralism: an ensemble inside a low E

The upper tones of one sound can supply the pitches of an entire chord.

An instrumental note usually combines a fundamental with higher tones. In an ideal harmonic spectrum, these upper tones vibrate twice, three times, four times as fast as the fundamental, and so on. Over a low E, the first few fall near E, B, E, G♯, B, D and E. Their changing strengths help make a bowed string sound different from a horn playing the same note.

Partials and harmonicsA partial is one component of a complex sound. A harmonic partial vibrates at a whole-number multiple of the fundamental. A bell’s partials can fall between those positions, producing an inharmonic spectrum.

Spectral composition turns those inner relationships into notes for instruments. Gérard Grisey’s Partiels (1975), for eighteen musicians, opens around a low E. Other instruments enter above it, spreading the E’s upper tones across several octaves. The timing of each entrance helps the separate notes merge into one large sound.

The sketchesFrançois-Xavier Féron’s research distinguishes the constructed spectra of Périodes and Partiels from the direct sound analyses Grisey used later in Modulations and Transitoires. His IRCAM lecture of 9 November 2022 corrects the familiar account of a recorded trombone as the early works’ model.

Tristan Murail’s Gondwana (1980) uses calculated pitches to build bell-like chords for an orchestra. These can gradually change towards more harmonic spectra. The notes shift their relationships while instruments sustain or renew them; a chord can seem to change its substance before it moves anywhere.

The piano can only approximate some of these pitches. Above E, the fifth harmonic falls about 13.69 cents below the equal-tempered G♯; the seventh falls about 31.17 cents below D. A cent is one hundredth of a semitone. String and wind players can adjust those notes, while the equal-tempered example below fixes them to the piano’s keys.

Two angels open a tent; Mary’s fingers part her dress. Grisey saw one opening inside another in Piero’s Madonna del Parto. In his programme note for L’Icône paradoxale, published at Festival Musica in 1996, he describes turning the fresco’s proportions, 3:5:8:12, into musical durations. The two singers use Piero’s names and words from his treatise on perspective. The composer’s account follows those borrowed shapes into the divided orchestra.

At the boundary

How pieces are joined

A cadence can close a section; a held bass note, a sequence or a shared motif can carry the music into the next. Four joins from the Hall, each with the passage to hear:

The flute takes F♯ minor

Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050 · A · Violin and flute in B minor → B · Flute refrain in F♯ minor

The harpsichord’s running right-hand line finishes and the flute brings back the dotted refrain in F♯ minor. The violin answers it over the keyboard bass. Bach marks the returning solo lines forte; the harpsichord returns to accompaniment.

The flute joins the conversation

Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050 · A · Strings in D major → B · Flute, violin and harpsichord

The eight-bar string refrain closes in D major. Flute and solo violin begin exchanging phrases above the harpsichord’s bass, with the keyboard’s right hand joining their runs. The pulse continues; the solo episode carries the music towards A major, where the next string refrain arrives.

Triplets turn to B minor

Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050 · A · Violin, flute, then harpsichord → B · Triplets in B minor
