9.1Independent lines

Counterpoint: two melodies, one argument

Counterpoint regulates the meeting of melodies. Each part must make sense horizontally, as a singable line, while its intervals with the other parts make sense vertically. Johann Joseph Fux’s Gradus ad Parnassum, published in Vienna in 1725, teaches that double responsibility through a dialogue between Aloysius, representing Palestrina, and the pupil Josephus. Its exercises isolate problems that a composer can practise before managing the rhythmic freedom of an actual motet.

SpeciesThe five stages are note against note; two against one; four against one; tied notes or suspensions; and a mixture of the preceding rhythms, called florid counterpoint.

In first species, a new note accompanies each note of a supplied cantus firmus, a fixed melody in long values. The vertical intervals are consonances: unisons, octaves and fifths, together with thirds and sixths. Parallel perfect fifths and octaves are excluded because they make two parts behave like one doubled line. Parallel thirds and sixths remain available. Contrary motion, in which one part rises while another falls, offers a particularly audible way to establish independence.

Dissonance becomes possible when rhythm explains it. In second species, a weak-beat passing note can bridge two consonances by step. In fourth species, a note is prepared as a consonance, held across a change in the other voice, and resolved down by step: preparation, suspension, resolution. A fourth above the bass counts as dissonant in this discipline, even though the same interval can be acceptable between upper parts in a larger texture. Consonance here is a matter of practice and position as well as acoustics.

Inversion is not reversalInvertible counterpoint exchanges the vertical positions of complete lines. Melodic inversion turns upward intervals downward; retrograde reads a sequence backwards. These are different operations.

In counterpoint invertible at the octave, thirds become sixths when the lower line is moved above the upper. Fifths become fourths: a consonance has become a dissonance, so the original fifth needs special treatment if both arrangements are to work. Bach designs subjects and accompanying lines with such exchanges in mind. The two invented examples separate the jobs: the opening example keeps every simultaneous interval consonant; the later one lets the same two lines trade places.

The title means “Steps to Parnassus”, the mountain associated with Apollo and the Muses. Fux makes a classical ascent into a practical teaching sequence: the pupil adds rhythmic freedom only after learning to control the intervals between voices. The printed page belongs to the same instructional culture as the dialogue inside it.

9.2Imitation

Fugue: an entrance changes the texture

A fugue starts by making a melody identifiable before making it compete with other melodies. One voice states the subject; another enters with an answer, normally at the dominant level. A real answer transposes every interval exactly. A tonal answer adjusts an interval, often exchanging a tonic-to-dominant gesture for dominant-to-tonic, to keep the opening within the home key. The distinction concerns exact and adjusted transposition.

One exact subjectThe example is the opening alto line of Bach’s C major Fugue, BWV 846, from Book I of The Well-Tempered Clavier (1722). The subject reaches E on beat three of bar 2; the remaining semiquavers continue the line.

The first voice continues when the second begins. If its accompanying material regularly returns against later statements of the subject, that material is a countersubject. A useful countersubject often works above or below the subject, requiring invertible counterpoint. BWV 846 proceeds without a regular countersubject; its initial answer is real, and its four opening entries arrive in the order subject, answer, answer, subject.

The opening round of entries is called the exposition. Subsequent entries may move into related keys, changing the subject’s harmonic surroundings while preserving enough of its intervals and rhythm for recognition. An episode is a passage without a complete subject statement, often made from a fragment sequenced at successive pitch levels. An episode can move the harmony towards a new entrance or thin the texture after a crowded passage. BWV 846 sustains its argument largely through entries and overlaps.

StrettoA new statement begins before the previous statement has finished. The distance between entries changes while the tempo can remain steady.

In stretto, the subject must combine contrapuntally with a displaced version of itself. Bach can shorten the interval between entrances, change their registers, or let more voices join the overlap. Those choices produce intensification without introducing a louder dynamic or a new theme. Augmentation lengthens the subject’s note values; inversion reverses the direction of its intervals. Listen for the moment of each entrance: it may interrupt an episode, complete a cadence, or turn an apparently accompanying voice into the principal speaker.

9.3A working notation

Figured bass: numbers above a moving foundation

Figured bass gives a player a written bass line and numbers specifying intervals above it. A harpsichordist, organist or theorbo player usually supplies the upper voices, choosing their spacing, rhythm and register to suit the singers or instrumentalists. In a Baroque continuo group, a cello or bassoon may reinforce the bass without playing chords. The division between bass instrument and chordal instrument answers practical needs, and the combination can vary between ensembles.

Count from the bassOver C, a 6 asks for A; over E, a 6 asks for C. The numeral names an interval above whichever bass note is sounding.

An unfigured bass commonly implies a third and fifth, adjusted to the key signature and the musical context. A 6 normally abbreviates 6/3; 6/4 asks for a fourth and sixth. Over E in C major, G and C therefore produce a first-inversion C major chord. Over G, C and E form a six-four sonority. At a cadence those upper notes often move down to B and D over the same G bass: the figures 6–5 and 4–3 describe the movement directly. A bare accidental usually alters the third; an accidental beside a numeral alters that numbered interval.

The numbers leave essential decisions open. Doubling a note can strengthen a chord, but doubling a leading note may make a clean resolution difficult. A singer’s sustained dissonance needs space in the accompaniment. A slow aria may invite an arpeggiated texture; a rapid recitative requires the player to follow the delivery of the text. Realisation is therefore both harmony and counterpoint: the player is composing several lines while accompanying an existing one.

Figures are not chord symbolsA modern “C/E” names a C chord with E in the bass. The historical figure 6 over E describes the intervals needed at that particular moment; the identity of the root is an analytical inference.

The example moves through C, C/E and D minor/F before its dominant seventh and tonic. Its upper voices are one possible realisation. The flexible notation constrains the harmony while leaving a trained player responsible for the texture and the local voice-leading.

9.4Tuning

Temperament: where to put the discrepancy

A pure fifth has a frequency ratio of 3:2. Stack twelve such fifths and reduce by seven octaves: the result is 531441:524288, about 23.46 cents above unison. This difference is the Pythagorean comma. On a keyboard with twelve notes per octave, closing the chain of fifths requires adjustment. Pythagorean tuning also produces a major third of 81:64, appreciably wider than the pure 5:4 third.

Two commasThe syntonic comma, 81:80, is the difference between the Pythagorean and pure major thirds. It is about 21.51 cents, compared with the Pythagorean comma’s 23.46. A cent is one hundredth of an equal-tempered semitone.

Quarter-comma meantone narrows the regular fifths by a quarter of the syntonic comma, about 5.38 cents. Four such fifths then produce a pure major third after octave reduction. The price is that a conventional twelve-note layout cannot offer every enharmonic spelling: G♯ and A♭ would ideally require different keys. The closing interval becomes a conspicuous “wolf”. Some historical keyboards supplied split accidentals to make more spellings available. Meantone is a family of tunings; quarter-comma is one especially important member.

Unequal well temperaments distribute the adjustments so that all major and minor keys become usable, but their thirds and fifths retain different sizes. Twelve-tone equal temperament instead gives every semitone the ratio 21/12. Its fifth is 700 cents, slightly narrow; its major third is 400 cents, about 13.69 cents wider than 5:4. Bach’s Well-Tempered Clavier demonstrates access to all twenty-four keys while leaving the temperament unspecified. Reconstructions of Bach’s preferred tuning remain disputed.

What beatsAt a 220 Hz base, compare the lower note’s third partial with the fifth’s second partial, or its fifth partial with the third’s fourth partial. Their frequency difference gives the beat rate. These near-coincident pairs are upper partials.

The laboratory makes that comparison with harmonic-rich tones. The equal-tempered fifth produces approximately 0.745 beats per second between 660 and 659.255 Hz. The equal-tempered third produces 8.73 between 1100 and 1108.731 Hz; the Pythagorean third produces 13.75 between 1100 and 1113.75 Hz. A pure interval removes the beating in the corresponding pair of partials; other interactions in a complex sound may remain. The printed example uses ordinary equal-tempered playback; the laboratory’s buttons change the frequency ratios.

9.5Tonal direction

Harmony: a chord’s job depends on where it goes

In C major, C–E–G is the tonic triad, I; G–B–D is the dominant, V; F–A–C is the subdominant, IV. Those labels locate roots in a scale. Harmonic function describes their behaviour over time: tonic establishes or prolongs a centre, dominant directs attention towards it, and a predominant prepares the dominant. IV often serves as a predominant, but so does ii, D minor. A chord’s position in a phrase matters as much as the notes it contains.

The dominant seventhG–B–D–F contains the tritone B–F. In a straightforward resolution to C major, B rises to C and F falls to E. The dissonance gives two independent voices specific destinations.

A cadence closes a phrase. V–I can make an authentic cadence; its strongest textbook form has both roots in the bass and the tonic in the top voice of the final chord. A half cadence ends on V. A deceptive cadence redirects V, often to vi, deflecting the expected tonic. IV–I is called plagal. Rhythm, melodic position and the preceding phrase determine whether these progressions close a phrase or occur in its middle.

The circle of fifths orders keys by successive fifths: C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯ and their enharmonic continuations. Adjacent major keys share six of their seven scale notes. This makes neighbouring keys convenient destinations for modulation. The same root relation can organise a sequence of chords in the opposite direction: E–A–D–G–C. In a diatonic sequence some chords are minor or diminished, and the bass can trace the circle of fifths within one key.

Tonicisation or modulationAn applied dominant briefly treats another chord as a tonic. A modulation gives the new centre enough support — often a cadence and a sustained passage — to function as a key in its own right.

To move from C major to G major, A minor can act as a pivot: vi in C becomes ii in G. D7 then introduces F♯, the new leading note, and resolves to G. The second example compresses that route into four bars; a full composition might delay its arrival with a sequence or extend the new tonic through several phrases. Rameau’s Traité de l’harmonie of 1722 helped establish analysis by chord roots and inversions. The later vocabulary of tonic, predominant and dominant describes relationships that continuo players also managed through bass motion and voice-leading.

9.6Large-scale tonality

Sonata form: the return changes the tonal route

Sonata form makes a large movement out of a tonal departure and a revised return. The exposition establishes the home key, then moves into a second key area and confirms it with cadential weight. In a major-key movement the second area is commonly the dominant; in a minor-key movement it is often the relative major. Themes make these regions recognisable. A movement can reuse one theme in both areas and still make the tonal opposition unmistakable.

Exposition is a shared wordA fugue’s exposition presents the voices in successive entries. A sonata exposition establishes a sequence of thematic functions and tonal areas.

The development normally unsettles the exposition’s relatively stable regions. A short motive may be detached from its theme, passed between instruments, sequenced through several keys or combined with another fragment. Some developments introduce new material. A retransition prepares the return, often by prolonging the home dominant. An almost motionless dominant pedal can make the coming tonic more urgent than a rapid succession of chords.

The recapitulation returns the principal material but alters the tonal route. Music previously directed towards a contrasting key must now be brought into the tonic region. That requires a rewritten transition, a transposed second group, or a less literal rearrangement. A coda can continue to develop the material after the main return. Exposition repeats matter too: hearing the original route twice establishes expectations that the changed return can subsequently contradict.

Beethoven’s FifthIn the first movement of Symphony No. 5, Op. 67, the exposition moves from C minor to E♭ major. The second group returns in C major during the recapitulation; the movement ultimately closes in C minor.

Beethoven’s Fifth resolves a tonal difference while preserving the second group’s major quality: the move from E♭ major to C major changes its tonal centre. The first movement’s short-note motto also continues under contrasting material, binding the key areas together rhythmically. In the “Waldstein” Sonata, Op. 53, Beethoven stretches the convention further: C major’s exposition reaches E major in place of the usual G. Its second group initially returns in A major before reaching the tonic. These are specific alternatives to a norm.

9.7Altered scale degrees

Chromaticism: the spelling records the pressure

A chromatic note lies outside the prevailing diatonic collection. It may decorate a line without changing the harmony, alter a chord’s function, or prepare another key. These are different events: a passing C♯ between C and D need not announce D major. In nineteenth-century music, chromatic notes often make otherwise familiar functions more insistent. The decisive evidence is their resolution. Two chords with the same piano keys can have different harmonic purposes if their notes are spelled and directed differently.

NeapolitanIn C minor, the major triad on the lowered second degree is D♭–F–A♭. It commonly appears with F in the bass, hence “Neapolitan sixth”, and usually prepares the dominant rather than establishing D♭ as a new tonic.

An augmented-sixth chord puts the lowered sixth and raised fourth degrees around the dominant. In C, A♭ below F♯ expands outward to G–G. Add C to obtain the Italian form; add C and D for the French; add C and E♭ for the German. The national names are later classifications. The German form commonly moves through a cadential six-four before the dominant, avoiding the parallel fifths that a direct resolution can produce.

Enharmonic modulation changes a chord’s spelling and destination while preserving its sounding pitches in equal temperament. A♭–C–E♭–F♯, a German augmented sixth in C, can be respelled A♭–C–E♭–G♭, the dominant seventh of D♭. Its outer notes take on new functions. Fully diminished sevenths offer another resource: B–D–F–A♭ can point towards C; respelling A♭ as G♯ lets the same keyboard collection act as G♯–B–D–F, directed towards A. The musical context establishes the new interpretation.

Equal-tempered equivalenceF♯ and G♭ share a key on a modern piano. They need not have identical frequencies in every historical tuning, or identical melodic inflections in a string quartet.

Chopin frequently embeds these resources within carefully directed inner voices: chromatic lines continue through the chord changes. His Prelude in E minor, Op. 28 No. 4, is a compact lesson in descending chromatic accompaniment beneath a restricted melody. Liszt uses enharmonic reinterpretation to connect more distant regions within extended forms, including the Sonata in B minor, S. 178. The invented examples isolate the harmonic mechanisms. 9.8A disputed sonority

The Tristan chord: four notes, several readings

Near the beginning of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde, the notes F–B–D♯–G♯ sound together. Their spelling matters. Above F, B is an augmented fourth, D♯ an augmented sixth, and G♯ an augmented ninth when placed in the upper register. The ensuing phrase reaches a dominant seventh on E, E–G♯–B–D. Within the implied A minor, one unstable sonority has moved to another. The opening repeatedly develops this condition of arrival without final settlement.

A familiar collection transformedRespell the chord F–A♭–C♭–E♭ and it becomes a half-diminished seventh. The novelty lies in the phrase’s voice-leading, rhythm, orchestration and withheld continuation.

One functional reading hears G♯ as an appoggiatura, a leaning note that moves to A. The resulting F–A–B–D♯ has the pitch content of a French augmented sixth in A minor. It belongs to the predominant region and prepares E. This reading explains the chord through a familiar tonal function; its account must also follow the actual chromatic motion. Other readings emphasise a decorated subdominant or interpret the initial notes as dissonant approaches to the dominant itself.

A linear reading follows the individual voices before assigning a root to every vertical slice. The upper line’s G♯–A–A♯–B forms a melody through the changing chords. Which notes count as structural and which as appoggiaturas depends on the span being analysed. A pitch-class description identifies the collection while leaving tonal function and the phrase’s direction open. These approaches answer different questions.

The example’s scopeThe two sustained chords in the opening example show the exact pitch collections F–B–D♯–G♯ and E–G♯–B–D. Their durations and connecting voice-leading are schematic.

The opera was first performed in Munich in 1865. Its influence on later harmony comes from the extension of these procedures across entire scenes. A dominant can remain intelligible while its expected tonic is postponed, redirected or concealed by another line. Debussy later quotes the opening in “Golliwogg’s Cakewalk” from Children’s Corner: a recognisable dramatic manner becomes material for interruption and parody. Tonal expectation makes the original chord’s deferred resolution audible.

9.9Musical memory

Leitmotif: recognition with a changed meaning

A leitmotif is a recurring musical idea associated with a dramatic person, object, action or concept. The music can recall that association independently of spoken words. In Wagner’s Ring, the orchestra can recall a promise that a character conceals, anticipate an object before it becomes visible, or combine motives whose dramatic meanings are in conflict. A short interval pattern may carry the identity; rhythm, register and instrumentation help the listener recognise it under changing harmonies.

Names as listening aidsMany familiar motive labels were circulated by commentators, notably Hans von Wolzogen. A label identifies a first association, leaving later uses open.

The sword Nothung gives a concrete example of musical memory extending beyond a single scene. Its associations pass through Das Rheingold, Die Walküre and Siegfried, even though the sword’s dramatic condition changes from promise to possession, fragments and reforging. The orchestra can connect those states before a spectator consciously identifies the notes. A change of harmony or a collision with another motive can enrich the association.

The technique has several predecessors. Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique (1830) carries an idée fixe, associated with the beloved, through five movements; in the finale its appearance on E♭ clarinet changes both register and character. Liszt’s thematic transformation similarly preserves a theme’s identity while changing its tempo, metre or harmonic setting. Recurrence, transformation and dramatic association overlap while remaining distinct procedures.

Three kinds of changeTransposition moves a pattern to another pitch level. Augmentation lengthens its durations proportionally. Reharmonisation changes the supporting chords without necessarily changing the melody. Each can preserve different identifying features.

The example uses an invented C–E–G cell. Lowering E changes its mode while preserving the contour; halving the durations changes its pace; doubling them makes the same proportions extend across a barline. A real leitmotif may survive less literal transformations, particularly when an orchestral colour or dramatic context supplies the missing clue. To follow one through an opera, compare its bass and inner voices as well as its melody. A familiar tune above a new bass can disclose a changed allegiance even when its surface rhythm remains intact.

Rackham’s illustration shows Mime at work in the smithy of Siegfried, Act I. The scene belongs to an opera in which the sword’s musical history precedes its successful reforging: the audience has already heard its associations in earlier parts of the Ring. A picture can show the workshop and the broken metal at once; the score can place the sword’s remembered past against the present action.

9.10Diatonic alternatives

Modes: change the centre, change the intervals

The white notes of the piano can support several centres. Give D that role and the collection forms D Dorian: D–E–F–G–A–B–C. Its minor third coexists with a major sixth, B, rather than the B♭ of natural minor. E Phrygian places a semitone immediately above its centre, E–F. F Lydian has a raised fourth, B, compared with F major. G Mixolydian has a lowered seventh, F, compared with G major. A sustained bass, phrase endings and recurring melodic shapes establish which note acts as centre.

Medieval modes are more than scalesHistorical church-mode classification also concerns final, range and reciting tone, with authentic and plagal forms. Modern modal composition often borrows interval patterns without reproducing that complete system.

Transposition carries the interval pattern to a new pitch level. C Lydian therefore needs F♯; D Mixolydian needs F♯ and C♮. A Dorian tune may remain recognisably modal even when its harmony occasionally uses a leading note at a cadence. Modes can govern a phrase, a pedal passage or a thematic strand while the surrounding music works through ordinary major–minor tonality.

Sibelius’s Sixth Symphony, Op. 104 (1923), draws much of its opening string writing from a Dorian-inflected D centre. The natural sixth alters the minor colour without requiring a chromatic detour. Vaughan Williams’s Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis (1910) takes its principal melody from Tallis’s third tune for Archbishop Parker’s Psalter, published in 1567; its Phrygian inheritance contributes to the expanded string-orchestra setting’s harmonic vocabulary.

Listen to the distinctive degreeDorian: natural sixth in a minor collection. Phrygian: lowered second. Lydian: raised fourth in a major collection. Mixolydian: lowered seventh. These comparisons assume the same tonic.

Debussy combines modal and symmetrical collections. In L’isle joyeuse (1904), A Lydian belongs to a network that also includes A major, whole-tone material and the acoustic collection A–B–C♯–D♯–E–F♯–G. The last collection’s natural G distinguishes it from ordinary Lydian. Dmitri Tymoczko’s analysis of Debussy’s scale networks traces these small changes between collections. The two scale examples isolate the distinctive degrees for comparison.

9.11Six equal steps

Whole-tone harmony: no privileged semitone

A whole-tone scale divides the octave into six equal whole tones: C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯–C. It contains no semitone and no perfect fifth. Every note has the same interval environment, placing C, D and F♯ on equal terms within the collection. A bass pedal, accent or melodic recurrence can still establish a centre.

Only two collectionsTranspose a whole-tone collection up a semitone and obtain the other one. Transpose it up a whole tone and recover the original pitch classes in a different order. Messiaen classified it as his first mode of limited transposition.

Taking alternate notes produces an augmented triad: C–E–G♯. Its three major thirds divide the octave equally, making its inversions sound less differentiated than those of a major triad. D–F♯–A♯ supplies the other augmented triad within the same whole-tone collection. Chords can move in parallel while staying inside the scale. The bass and the duration of each sonority can supply direction independently of dominant–tonic functions.

Debussy’s piano prelude Voiles, published in Book I in 1910, uses whole-tone material for most of its span, with a contrasting pentatonic passage. The contrast matters because a five-note collection restores unequal interval sizes, including minor thirds, which the whole-tone field excludes. Its title can mean either “sails” or “veils”. A sustained B♭ gives the symmetrical field a reference point outside conventional B♭ major or minor.

A Russian precedentGlinka used whole-tone material in Ruslan and Lyudmila (1842), notably in the supernatural associations around Chernomor. Russian composers developed that vocabulary before Debussy’s piano preludes.

Whole-tone passages can also arise locally inside tonal music: an altered dominant may share several of their notes. The analyst must distinguish a complete governing collection from a brief chord that happens to fit one. An ornamental chromatic note can occur within a whole-tone passage while its stable degrees remain intact. In the example, the two augmented triads partition all six notes. Their relationship is one of register and pitch level, independent of a leading note or a perfect-fifth root relation.

9.12Eight-note symmetry

Octatonic harmony: alternating whole tones and semitones

An octatonic collection alternates semitones and whole tones. Beginning with a semitone on C gives C–D♭–E♭–E–F♯–G–A–B♭. The octave contains eight pitch classes, compared with the seven of a diatonic scale or six of a whole-tone scale. Starting with a whole tone on C selects a different member of the octatonic family. Once the starting collection is chosen, its rotations preserve the alternation.

Three distinct collectionsTransposition by a minor third reproduces an octatonic collection. Transpositions by zero, one and two semitones therefore exhaust its distinct pitch-class collections. Messiaen’s mode 2 names this symmetry as limited transposition.

The collection contains two interlocking diminished-seventh chords. In the example they are F♯–A–C–E♭ and C♯–E–G–B♭, with C♯ enharmonically equivalent to the scale’s D♭. It also contains several ordinary major and minor triads. A composer can consequently establish a recognisable chord locally while placing it in a larger, symmetrical field. A major triad’s bass, repetition and relation to the other chords determine how much tonal authority it acquires.

Rimsky-Korsakov explored the collection in the orchestral tableau Sadko, Op. 5 (1867), well before the later opera of that name. Its use became associated with supernatural episodes in his operas, often contrasted with more diatonic music for human characters. Stravinsky, who studied with him, adapted octatonic resources to more abrupt juxtapositions and repeating blocks. Octatonic relationships are one resource within the varied pitch language of Petrushka and The Rite of Spring.

A specific superpositionThe famous C-major/F♯-major “Petrushka chord” places two triads together. The ballet also draws on other collections, tonal references and changes of pitch organisation.

Messiaen made the three transpositions of mode 2 explicit in his Technique de mon langage musical (1944). His practice combines the eight-note scale with particular chord voicings, colours and rhythmic procedures. In an octatonic passage, listen for minor-third-related transpositions and for chords that change while remaining within the same collection. Unlike the whole-tone scale, this one supplies semitones in abundance; their symmetrical placement gives several notes a potential claim to centrality.

9.13Without a tonic

Atonality: organised intervals without a home key

Atonal music organises pitch outside the governing tonic hierarchy of major–minor harmony. It can use repetition, melody and consonant intervals. The question is whether one pitch or triad controls the others as a tonal centre over the relevant span. A dense chromatic passage in Wagner can preserve that control; a spare line in Webern can dispense with it.

Before twelve-tone compositionSchoenberg’s Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11 (1909), precede his twelve-tone method. Atonality and serialism describe distinct historical and technical developments.

Without dominant-to-tonic syntax, other relationships become more exposed. A three-note cell can recur at a new pitch level, turn upside down, expand into a chord or pass between instruments. Its intervals may remain recognisable even when its register and rhythm change. A pitch-class analysis treats octave-equivalent notes as members of the same class, making some of these relations easier to describe. Such a reduction leaves out register, dynamics and articulation, all of which may be essential to the phrase as heard.

In the example, C–C♯–F contains an ascending semitone followed by a major third. G–G♯–C transposes that pattern up a fifth. C–B–G reverses the interval directions, producing an inversion, and D–C♯–A transposes that inversion. These controlled relationships connect the cells independently of a V–I cadence, offering one way of relating events outside a key.

Consonance can remain localA major third or perfect fifth inside an atonal piece is still audible as an interval. What disappears is the assumption that it must belong to a tonic, dominant or another chord within a single key.

Schoenberg’s Erwartung, Op. 17 (1909), uses the continuity of a dramatic monologue; Webern’s short instrumental works concentrate on register, silence and articulation; Berg retains tonal references and familiar forms within a more mixed language. Each score’s organisation emerges through its intervals, rhythms and returns. Kandinsky’s response to Schoenberg in 1911 supplies a documented connection with painting.

Kandinsky attended a Schoenberg concert in Munich on 2 January 1911, with Gabriele Münter and Franz Marc among his companions. The programme included the Second String Quartet and the Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11; this painting followed the experience. The Lenbachhaus record documents the connection. The black mass still suggests the piano as the picture reorganises the concert scene. Schoenberg’s twelve-tone method was still to come.

9.14Ordered pitch classes

Twelve-tone writing: a row orders pitch classes

A twelve-tone row orders the twelve chromatic pitch classes, each appearing once. It leaves rhythm, register, dynamics and instrumentation open. A composer can distribute its notes between melody and accompaniment, combine successive notes into chords, repeat an immediately sounding note, or articulate groups from the row as motives. The classroom injunction “never repeat a note until all twelve have sounded” oversimplifies this freedom.

Schoenberg’s Op. 25The basic row is E–F–G–D♭–G♭–E♭–A♭–D–B–C–A–B♭. The Suite for Piano was composed in 1921–23. Its last four notes, read backwards, give B♭–A–C–B: B–A–C–H in German note names.

The prime form presents the chosen order. Inversion reverses each directed pitch-class interval: if the prime moves up one semitone, the inversion moves down one. Retrograde reverses the order of the notes; retrograde inversion reverses the inverted order. Each of these four forms can be transposed to twelve pitch levels, giving forty-eight standard forms before any coincidences caused by a row’s own symmetry. Their relationships preserve specific interval successions or reverse their direction and order.

The examples keep the inversion’s initial E fixed. E–F becomes E–E♭, and the following upward whole tone F–G becomes the downward whole tone E♭–D♭. All notes are placed within one illustrative octave to expose pitch classes. The schematic barlines divide each row into three groups of four. Labelling systems differ about whether the chosen prime is called P0 or, with C numbered zero, P4. Stating the initial pitch avoids that ambiguity here.

A restricted selectionThe Arnold Schönberg Center’s account notes that Op. 25 uses only eight of the forty-eight forms: the four basic operations and their tritone transpositions.

Its dances — Gavotte, Musette, Menuett and Gigue — retain historical formal references while the pitch organisation changes. The row can function as a shared interval resource across different movements. Webern would exploit rows with strong internal symmetries; Berg chose rows that could expose tonal allusions. Later serial composers extended ordering procedures to durations or dynamics. Within the twelve-tone method, rhythm and phrase shape remain compositional decisions.

9.15Grouping time

Rhythm: pulse and accent can diverge

Metre groups recurring pulses into a hierarchy of stronger and weaker positions. Rhythm is the actual pattern of attacks, durations and silences placed against that grouping. A melody can contradict the metre while the accompaniment maintains it. In a hemiola, six equal units are regrouped from two groups of three into three groups of two, or the reverse. Across two bars of 3/4, three minims can therefore displace the expected two sets of three crotchets without changing the underlying duration.

Hemiola is not a tempo changeThe span stays the same. What changes is the location of the grouping accents. Renaissance cadences, Baroque dances and Brahms’s phrase extensions use the device in different textures.

Additive rhythm constructs a longer group from unequal smaller units. A 7/8 bar can divide 2+2+3. Balkan aksak patterns combine short and long groups, commonly in a 2:3 relation. Bartók’s six dances in Bulgarian rhythm at the end of Mikrokosmos, Nos. 148–153, make such groupings explicit; No. 148 uses 4+2+3. The larger pulses depend on the grouping as well as the notated denominator.

Stravinsky uses changing bar lengths in the “Sacrificial Dance” of The Rite of Spring, making a steady small unit support unevenly returning accents. Elsewhere in the ballet, displaced accents operate within an unchanged metre. Messiaen’s added values modify an existing duration with a short note, a rest or a dot. Extending a two-quaver unit to three quavers shifts everything that follows by one quaver; a new time signature may record the effect, but the generative idea is the addition.

Non-retrogradable rhythmA duration sequence such as 2–3–2 reads identically forwards and backwards. Messiaen uses this separate property alongside added values. Asymmetry and rhythmic palindromes are independent properties.

Polyrhythm sounds different regular divisions of the same span simultaneously. In three against two, one part makes three equally spaced attacks while another makes two. Dividing the span into six subunits locates the first part at 0, 2 and 4, the second at 0 and 3. Both return together at 6. This differs from alternating a bar in three with a bar in two, and from polymetre, where parts maintain different metrical groupings. The examples keep the pitches simple to focus the comparison on the attacks.

9.16Instrumental construction

Orchestration: register, balance and the attack of a note

Orchestration assigns musical material to instruments and changes its effect. A flute projects differently in its low and high registers; a horn can merge with woodwind or become a distinct brass voice. A clarinet note with a gentle attack can enter beneath a string tone and temporarily conceal its source. Register, articulation and dynamic determine whether a line separates from its surroundings or combines with another instrument into a composite sound.

Two working booksBerlioz’s Grand traité d’instrumentation et d’orchestration modernes appeared in 1843–44. Rimsky-Korsakov’s posthumous Principles of Orchestration, edited by Maximilian Steinberg, organises practical questions of balance, doubling and the distribution of parts.

Berlioz describes instrumental mechanisms and ranges alongside their dramatic uses, illustrating his claims with passages from actual scores. Rimsky-Korsakov separates problems such as a melody in octaves, sustained harmony and accompaniment, testing how orchestral families combine. Applying either book requires knowledge of the particular player or instrument. A written note has different consequences on a stopped horn, a muted trumpet and an open string, even when the sounding frequency agrees.

Ravel’s Boléro (1928) holds its principal rhythmic ostinato and melodic material relatively constant while changing instrumental presentation and accumulating weight. The first melody arrives on solo flute; later statements exploit instruments including saxophones and trombone. A particularly instructive combination uses horn, celesta and piccolos at different pitch levels to suggest the components of a composite timbre. The harmony’s long stability makes those changes unusually exposed. The work also contains a late tonal disruption.

Doubling has a costAdding instruments can thicken a line without making its articulation clearer. A low, widely spaced chord may project better than the same notes crowded together in the bass, where their partials overlap densely.

Extended techniques change the method of sound production: sul ponticello places the bow near the bridge, col legno uses its wood, and wind multiphonics use specialised fingerings and embouchures to produce several components together. Their notation must specify a reproducible action. The example assigns the same notes to flute and oboe so that timbre is the only intended variable. Its General MIDI playback demonstrates instrumental assignment in isolation from a live player’s breath, attack, dynamics and room.

9.17Audible processes

Minimalism: repetition makes the change measurable

Terry Riley’s In C (1964) provides fifty-three short patterns in a fixed order. Each player chooses how often to repeat a pattern before advancing, listening to the ensemble throughout. A repeated high C commonly supplies the quaver pulse. The resulting overlaps vary between performances, but the order of the material and the shared pulse constrain the freedom. “Repeat” asks the performer to maintain a relationship.

Different processesRiley’s performers occupy different points in a shared sequence. Reich’s phasing shifts the alignment of the same repeating pattern. Glass’s additive process changes a pattern’s length.

In Reich’s Piano Phase (1967), two performers initially align a repeating figure. One gradually moves ahead until the patterns settle one subdivision apart; the process proceeds through further alignments. New composite patterns emerge from the combination of the two parts. The opening example presents two fixed alignments, illustrating the destination of a phase shift through an abrupt displacement.

Philip Glass’s Two Pages explores the extension and contraction of a line through additive process. A schematic C–D–E might become C–D–E–G, then C–D–E–G–A before contracting again. The common pulse remains, but the cycle takes longer to recur. Glass’s work with Ravi Shankar and Alla Rakha helped direct his attention towards grouping small rhythmic units into larger spans. His published recording notes describe Two Pages as an early study of precisely this expansion and contraction.

Tintinnabuli is a separate syntaxPärt combines a melodic voice with a voice drawn from a fixed triad. Rules choose the triadic note’s position relative to the melody, determining pitch relationships independently of loops or phase processes.

Pärt’s Für Alina (1976) is an early statement of tintinnabuli. A stepwise melody and a triadic partner generate intervals that can sound exposed; silence and the piano’s decay are part of their articulation. The second example uses an explicitly invented rule: accompany each melody note with the nearest strictly lower member of A–C–E. Calling Riley, Reich, Glass and Pärt “minimalist” identifies a broad history of reduced materials and repetition. Their pieces differ in who controls the process, which elements change, and how a phrase comes to an end.

9.18Inside the sound

Spectralism: a timbre becomes harmonic material

A harmonic spectrum contains components at whole-number multiples of a fundamental frequency: f, 2f, 3f, 4f and so on. Its spacing is uneven when heard as musical intervals: the first two components form an octave, the next pair a fifth, and higher neighbours become progressively closer. Their relative strengths and their behaviour during the attack and decay help determine timbre. Spectral composition uses such frequency relationships and changes over time as material for harmony, orchestration and form.

Partials and harmonicsA partial is a component of a complex sound. A harmonic partial is an integer multiple of the fundamental. Bells and other inharmonic sounds supply spectral music with different frequency relations.

Gérard Grisey’s Partiels (1975), for eighteen musicians, opens around a low E and spreads a harmonic field across instrumental registers. The ensemble can be heard as separate notes or as a composite sound; changing their entrances, intensity and tuning changes that perception. François-Xavier Féron’s research in the sketches distinguishes the early works’ constructed spectra from the later use of measured sonographic data.

A source correctionIn his 2022 IRCAM presentation, Féron states that no trombone sound served as the model for Périodes or Partiels; direct spectral analyses informed sections of later works including Modulations and Transitoires.

Tristan Murail’s Gondwana (1980) brings inharmonic, bell-like fields into relation with more harmonic spectra. Electronic synthesis supplies compositional models even when the performing medium is an orchestra: calculated frequency relationships can become instrumental pitches, and changes of spectral density can govern a transition. Real instruments contribute spectra of their own, making the ensemble sound an interpretation of the model.

The seventh harmonic illustrates why conventional notation needs qualification. Above an E fundamental it lies near D, about 31.17 cents below the equal-tempered pitch; the fifth harmonic lies about 13.69 cents below an equal-tempered G♯. Quarter-tone or finer notation can approximate these relations, but a normal piano cannot tune them independently. The invented example plays equal-tempered landmarks. Grisey’s later L’Icône paradoxale adds another form of modelling: Piero della Francesca’s spatial proportions and writings on perspective enter the construction of musical time and vocal material.

Grisey explicitly based L’Icône paradoxale (1992–94), for two women’s voices and divided orchestra, on this fresco. In his programme note he describes the opened tent and the opening of Mary’s dress as nested spaces, and identifies the proportions 3:5:8:12 as temporal material. The voices use Piero’s names and text from his perspective treatise. The approximate dating follows the Monterchi museum.
