Periods

Seven ways into the music: the working lives, instruments, institutions and ideas behind the scores. These overlapping periods are a reading guide, not hard borders.

Period I1600–1750

The Baroque

A bass line with numbers over it — the basso continuo — holds everything up. Above it, voices move in imitation, keys are chosen for their character, and a single mood (an affect) governs a whole movement. The fugue reaches its summit in Bach; opera and the concerto spread from Italy across Europe.

Period II1750–1827

Classical

The continuo falls away and melody is set over a lighter accompaniment. Music is now built in balanced phrases and organised by the tension between two keys: the sonata principle, which shapes symphonies, quartets and concertos. Haydn invents its procedures, Mozart makes them sing, Beethoven makes them fight.

4 composers in this collection

Period III1815–1890

Romanticism

The composer becomes a hero and the listener a confidant. Harmony grows chromatic, forms stretch to hold a programme or a poem, the piano becomes an orchestra under ten fingers, and national idioms — Polish, Bohemian, Russian — enter the concert hall. From Schubert’s songs to Wagner’s music drama.

16 composers in this collection

Period IV1880–1918

Fin de siècle

Orchestras of a hundred players and more, symphonies of ninety minutes, harmony stretched to the edge of tonality. Mahler wants the symphony to contain the world; Strauss can make muted, flutter-tongued brass bleat like a flock of sheep; Rachmaninoff builds melodies from chant and bells; Sibelius goes the other way, toward compression and silence.

8 composers in this collection

Period V1870–1937

The French Line

Chords heard as colour, scales drawn from chant, from Java and from the whole-tone collection; orchestration as a precise art of timbre. Fauré refines, Debussy dissolves, Satie subtracts, and Ravel engineers.

6 composers in this collection

Period VI1908–1945

Modernism

In 1908 Schoenberg writes music without a tonal centre; in 1913 Stravinsky’s Rite of Spring makes rhythm the main event. Then come twelve-tone rows, folk music recorded on wax cylinders by Bartók, neoclassicism, and composers like Prokofiev and Shostakovich writing under the eye of the state.

8 composers in this collection

Period VII1945–present

After 1945

After the war: serialism extended to every parameter, then Ligeti’s clouds of sound, Feldman’s long quiet, Reich’s pulse and phase, Pärt’s bells, and the spectral music of Saariaho, built from the physics of a single tone. Many styles, many workshops.
