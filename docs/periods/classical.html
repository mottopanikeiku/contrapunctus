Period II · 1750–1827

Classical

The sonata principle: drama made of keys

The continuo falls away and melody is set over a lighter accompaniment. Music is now built in balanced phrases and organised by the tension between two keys: the sonata principle, which shapes symphonies, quartets and concertos. Haydn invents its procedures, Mozart makes them sing, Beethoven makes them fight.

The story

By 1750, melody with accompaniment was long established. Composers increasingly made its phrases, cadences and changes of key carry the form. A listener could recognise an opening idea, hear it lose its stability, and understand its return as a decisive event. Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven developed that practice in symphonies, quartets and sonatas. Their procedures grew from Italian opera, keyboard music and earlier instrumental genres. The label Classical describes these working habits.

The galant style reduced the density of independent lines, often setting a treble melody above repeated chords or a broken-chord bass. This made the phrase endings unusually exposed: a cadence could sound provisional, conclusive or deliberately misplaced. C. P. E. Bach’s keyboard music brings volatility to this world of elegance and balance. Sudden rests, unexpected harmonic turns and changes of register interrupt his musical speech. His Versuch über die wahre Art das Clavier zu spielen, published in two parts in 1753 and 1762, treats expressive delivery alongside practical questions of fingering, ornament and accompaniment.

Eight bars are a modelA four-bar question and four-bar answer make a useful classroom period. Composers can extend the question, compress the answer or postpone its cadence.

A period joins related phrases whose endings differ in strength; a sentence typically repeats an opening idea before accelerating its activity towards a cadence. Those patterns give an audience something measurable to follow. Repeated accompaniment figures establish a regular pulse, while a pause or an extra bar unsettles the expectation. Haydn’s jokes depend on such precision: the listener has to know where a phrase ought to end before a false ending can register. The light surface is therefore compatible with intricate composition. A single extra repetition may change the proportion of an entire movement without adding a new theme.

The argumentLeaving a key, recovering it

In the practice later called sonata form, the exposition establishes the tonic and makes a second tonal area sufficiently definite to require an answer. In major-key movements that area is commonly the dominant; in minor-key movements the relative major is frequent. Haydn can reuse the opening material in the new key, allowing the same idea to acquire a different structural function. The exposition’s repeat gives listeners another opportunity to learn this geography before the development unsettles it.

A development can fragment a theme, sequence it through new keys, combine it with other material or concentrate on a rhythm that initially seemed secondary. The return of the tonic and opening material then begins the recapitulation, but a composer must alter the earlier route if the later material is also to remain in the tonic. That necessary revision makes the return an act of recomposition. Beethoven enlarges the possibilities further: an introduction, transition or coda may take on substantial harmonic and motivic work.

The symphony conventionally offered four movements at different scales of organisation: an extended first movement, a slower movement, a minuet and trio, and a finale. The quartet carried comparable ambitions into four individual string parts, where an inner voice could become a soloist without a change of personnel. Mozart’s concertos added another participant, the pianist, whose entry could reinterpret material already heard from the orchestra. The theatrical distinction between a collective statement and an individual reply survived in instrumental music.

The instrumentsA hammer changes the phrase

Bartolomeo Cristofori developed the piano around 1700, but its spread was gradual. The decisive mechanism is an escapement: the hammer must strike the string and immediately fall away, leaving it free to vibrate. The force of the player’s touch can then affect loudness. In the later eighteenth century, the lightly built fortepiano offered a quicker decay and more differentiated registers than a modern concert grand. A bass note could speak clearly and then recede during a long passage. Written slurs and rests acquired a particular physical meaning on that instrument.

Viennese and English builders developed different actions and balances of power. Mozart’s keyboard writing exploits rapid changes of touch, transparent basses and the dialogue between singing treble and accompaniment; Beethoven’s increasingly forceful textures also reflect instruments changing during his career. Knee levers or pedals could lift the dampers, but their arrangement varied. Playing this music on a later iron-framed grand brings different acoustics. A player has to reconsider pedal duration and the weight of the bass because the instrument can hold and blend sounds differently.

The natural hornCrooks changed the instrument’s basic tube length. Hand-stopping altered additional pitches and their colour, giving stopped and open notes distinct sounds.

The orchestra acquired a more stable organisation around strings and pairs of winds, while its exact membership remained dependent on venue and score. Oboes, bassoons and horns were familiar resources; clarinets gained a larger role during Mozart’s lifetime. Natural horns and trumpets still shaped the choice of keys and the distribution of notes. Timpani commonly reinforced tonic and dominant. Beethoven could enlarge this palette for a specific formal purpose: the finale of his Fifth Symphony adds piccolo, contrabassoon and trombones to the preceding movements’ forces. The change in orchestration helps make the arrival in C major audible as a change of scale.

The listenersPatrons and ticket buyers

Aristocratic patronage and a growing market supported the same composers. Haydn’s long service to the Esterházy family provided musicians and an institutional setting for experiment; his London visits in 1791–92 and 1794–95 placed him before paying metropolitan audiences. Mozart arranged subscription concerts in Vienna while also seeking positions, teaching and composing on commission. Beethoven sold works to publishers and received aristocratic support, including the annuity agreed in 1809. The same composer might address a private room, a theatre and a large concert audience within one year, adjusting difficulty, duration and forces to each.

Domestic music-making gave publishers a market beyond professional players. Sonatas, chamber music, keyboard reductions and arrangements put works within purchasers’ performing reach. Engraved editions travelled across political boundaries, but unauthorised copying and competing editions complicated a composer’s earnings. Scores became more explicit about dynamics, articulation and texture while leaving room for improvisation. A concerto cadenza still invited the soloist’s invention; written cadenzas preserve particular solutions. Mozart and Beethoven were keyboard improvisers as well as authors of increasingly detailed pages.

Enlightenment’s varietyScientific demonstration, sentimental fiction and revolutionary politics belonged to the same century. Clarity of musical syntax could carry emotional force.

The other arts supplied models of address. In Joseph Wright’s air-pump experiment, an audience is organised around an event, and the spectators’ different responses become the painting’s subject. Mozart’s operatic ensembles likewise let several people react differently within a common musical time. In Le nozze di Figaro of 1786, a change in who knows what can alter the pace and membership of an ensemble. The form accommodates social misunderstanding with technical exactness: entrances, cadences and tempo changes articulate the shifting dramatic situation.

Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony, premiered in Vienna in 1824, brings Schiller’s language of fraternity into a genre previously capable of sustaining its argument without words. Its finale first recalls and rejects music from earlier movements, then introduces a tune that will support variations, a march and contrapuntal combinations. The chorus enters an instrumental construction already under way. By then E. T. A. Hoffmann had described Beethoven’s instrumental music in explicitly Romantic terms. The boundary between Classical and Romantic practice runs through these works.

Terms of art

Vocabulary for the sounds and practices of this period.

Period

Related phrases arranged so that a weaker first ending is answered by a stronger cadence. The familiar eight-bar period has two four-bar phrases, but actual periods can be expanded, compressed or irregular.

Sentence

A phrase design that presents and repeats a basic idea, then continues towards a cadence with fragmentation or increased rhythmic activity. Its organising principle is repetition followed by acceleration, rather than question and answer.

Sonata form

A tonal design in which an exposition establishes contrasting key areas, a development destabilises the material, and a recapitulation revisits it with the tonal opposition resolved. Contrasting themes are common but not essential.

Development

The transformation and recombination of musical material, or the central section of a sonata-form movement where that work is concentrated. Fragmentation, sequence, modulation and contrapuntal combination can all contribute.

String quartet

Two violins, viola and cello, normally with one player to each part; also a composition for those forces. The medium permits independent lines and shared thematic work without an orchestral division between solo and section.

Cadenza

A passage allowing a concerto soloist to play without the orchestra, often near a movement’s close. It may elaborate earlier material, delay a cadence and end with a trill signalling the ensemble’s return.

Fortepiano

The historical piano of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, with hammer action, relatively light stringing and a wooden structure preceding the later full iron frame. Its touch-sensitive attack and shorter sustain affect balance and articulation.

In the score

Quotations and teaching schematics are labelled separately. Playback uses synthesised instruments.

Editorial comparison

A scientific demonstration becomes a scene of differentiated attention: the children fear for the bird, one spectator watches the mechanism, and others turn inward or towards one another. The comparison with a Mozart ensemble concerns the organisation of simultaneous responses. Both give several viewpoints a shared frame. The National Gallery identifies the bird as a cockatoo and describes the experiment’s place in public scientific demonstration.

Documented useFriedrich Schiller

An die Freude — first stanza

1785; revised c. 1803
GermanFreude, schöner Götterfunken, Tochter aus Elysium, Wir betreten feuertrunken, Himmlische, dein Heiligtum. Deine Zauber binden wieder, Was die Mode streng geteilt, Alle Menschen werden Brüder, Wo dein sanfter Flügel weilt.
English · Contrapunctus translationJoy, lovely spark of the gods, Daughter from Elysium, Drunk with fire we enter, Heavenly one, your sanctuary. Your spells bind together again What custom has strictly divided; All people become brothers Where your gentle wing rests.

Schiller’s revised stanza, with spelling modernised, replaces the first version’s explicit pairing of beggars and princes with “all people”. Beethoven set selections from the revised poem in the Ninth Symphony, adding his own introductory appeal, “O Freunde, nicht diese Töne!” The finale tests fraternity through changes of musical company: bass soloist, quartet, chorus and orchestra. The historical versions record the poem’s revisions.
