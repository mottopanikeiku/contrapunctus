Period III · 1815–1890

Romanticism

The song, the self and the swelling orchestra

The composer becomes a hero and the listener a confidant. Harmony grows chromatic, forms stretch to hold a programme or a poem, the piano becomes an orchestra under ten fingers, and national idioms — Polish, Bohemian, Russian — enter the concert hall. From Schubert’s songs to Wagner’s music drama.

The story

In 1815 Schubert set Goethe’s Erlkönig for one singer and piano. The singer must distinguish a narrator, a father, a frightened child and the Erlking; the pianist maintains repeated triplets whose physical insistence continues beneath their incompatible accounts of the night. A domestic combination becomes a small theatre without scenery. Romantic music repeatedly enlarges what a familiar medium can contain: a song can sustain a drama, a piano miniature can preserve an interrupted thought, and a symphony can follow a written programme. The enlargement concerns musical function as much as duration or the number of players.

Poetry supplied composers with more than words to set. A poem’s change of speaker could require a new register; a repeated image could become a returning accompaniment figure; an unresolved ending could leave a piano postlude to complete the statement. In a strophic song the same music returns for successive verses, while a through-composed setting changes with the text. A song cycle adds relationships between separate songs through narrative, recurring motives or a planned sequence of keys. Schubert’s Winterreise of 1827 makes the piano an independent witness: it can contradict the singer’s wish for comfort.

The fragmentA short character piece can find its form in a concentrated gesture, an abrupt break or a withheld cadence.

Schumann assembled piano cycles from sharply differentiated pieces whose titles, repetitions and hidden connections invite comparison with literary fragments. Chopin retained named dances — mazurka, polonaise, waltz — while altering their scale, harmonic density and technical demands. A mazurka’s displaced accent matters even when nobody could conveniently dance to its entire composition. Fanny Hensel’s piano cycle Das Jahr, composed in 1841, associates twelve pieces with the months, followed by a postlude. Such cycles make sequence itself part of the argument: the identity of a piece depends partly on what the listener remembers from its neighbours.

The instrument tradeMore tension, faster repetition

The nineteenth-century piano changed through a series of inventions. Érard’s repetition action, patented in 1821, allowed a note to be restruck before the key had fully returned. Alpheus Babcock patented a cast-iron frame for a square piano in 1825; later makers developed full iron structures for grands. Stronger frames accommodated greater string tension, while heavier strings, felt-covered hammers and altered string layouts changed attack and sustain. Chopin’s preferences and Liszt’s concert practice emerged among competing instruments with different actions and tonal balances.

The sustained bass and extended compass encouraged textures with several simultaneous registers: a bass foundation, inner accompaniment and a high melody projected above them. Pedalling could connect notes beyond the reach of one hand, but also make chromatic harmony blur. A composer therefore had to calculate releases as well as attacks. Virtuoso writing used leaps, repeated notes and alternating hands to create the illusion of more than one instrumental group. Liszt’s transcriptions circulated operatic and symphonic music through this machinery, enabling a pianist to present material that otherwise required a theatre or orchestra.

Orchestral mechanics changed as decisively. Horn valves, developed in the 1810s, redirected air through additional lengths of tubing and made chromatic pitches more readily available; natural horns nevertheless remained in use. Improved keywork helped woodwind players manage difficult fingerings, and Theobald Boehm’s 1847 flute design reorganised the relationship between holes and fingers. Trombones became less exceptional in symphonic scoring, while the tuba added a new bass to the brass family. Berlioz’s orchestration treatise of 1844 examined instruments by their registers and combinations, treating colour as a compositional decision.

The harmonic pressureDistance with a common note

Romantic chromaticism can intensify a cadence, conceal the direction of a bass or connect two apparently remote harmonies through a shared pitch. C major and E major have only E in common, but retaining that E can make the move sound continuous despite the changed key signature. Schubert repeatedly uses relations by thirds to alter the apparent distance between tonal areas. An enharmonic respelling can give the same sounding note a new grammatical role, turning an arrival into a departure without changing its acoustic pitch.

Delayed resolutionThe opening chords of Tristan und Isolde invite resolutions that are postponed, redirected or made provisional; the listener’s tonal expectations do much of the work.

Wagner integrates this pressure with dramatic memory. Changes of harmony, scoring or rhythm can make a leitmotif reveal a connection the characters do not yet understand. In Tristan und Isolde, first performed in 1865, phrases repeatedly continue beyond the points where they seem ready to settle. Liszt’s thematic transformation offers another form of continuity: a recognisable interval pattern changes tempo, metre and character, allowing several dramatic functions to grow from related material. Both procedures organise extended spans through related ideas.

Other composers made inherited forms bear comparable pressures. Brahms derives later figures from small changes to earlier ones and sets conflicting accents against a stable metre. Bruckner works with large tonal regions, accumulating repetitions and contrasts between instrumental groups. Mendelssohn combines contrapuntal training with concise thematic profiles; his 1829 Berlin performance of Bach’s St Matthew Passion helped establish an older work as a public concert event. The period’s interest in history thus affected composition and programming together. Writing a new symphony increasingly meant addressing an audience that already knew influential symphonies by dead composers.

The publicConcert, salon, opera house

Growing cities supported subscription orchestras, choral societies and touring virtuosos, but aristocratic and private patronage continued alongside ticket sales. The salon could be a serious musical institution. Clara Schumann sustained an international performing career, while Hensel’s Sunday musicales in Berlin provided a setting for ambitious work under restrictions that shaped her access to public musical life. Publishers sold scores, arrangements and teaching editions to domestic players. Critical journals helped establish reputations and disputes, turning a compositional choice into something that could be argued over in print between performances.

The score grew more detailed about tempo changes, dynamics, pedalling and instrumental balance, yet performers still supplied conventions not completely captured by notation. Rubato could involve flexibility of the whole phrase or displacement of a melodic line against steadier accompaniment. Editors and virtuosos also altered older music, producing a tension between documentary fidelity and practical performance. The printed page reached more readers while its authority remained contested. Piano reductions, in particular, taught listeners works through textures different from the orchestration they might eventually hear.

National style is constructedA concert work can use a dance rhythm, language or modal pattern without quoting a collected folk tune. National identity and musical source are different questions.

Dvořák draws on dance types and characteristic rhythms; Tchaikovsky brings songful themes into symphonic designs that can intensify through repetition and orchestral accumulation. Mussorgsky pursues the inflections of Russian speech, while Rimsky-Korsakov develops highly differentiated instrumental colours. These choices belonged to debates about education, empire and language. In Italy, Verdi adjusted established operatic forms to dramatic pace and the capacities of singers. A public accustomed to operatic melody could hear a new social or political implication in a familiar formal procedure.

Romantic painting and fiction likewise made perception part of their subject. Friedrich’s ruined abbey is both an architectural object and a measured confrontation between a funeral procession, bare trees and an expanse of sky. Goethe’s Erlking can be explained as mist and wind by the father while remaining terrifyingly real to the child. Schubert gives those accounts different musical identities without resolving the conflict before the final line. The technical problem is how to place incompatible experiences inside one work: alternating speakers, recurrent figures and changes of harmonic perspective accomplish it.

Terms of art

Vocabulary for the sounds and practices of this period.

Chromatic mediant

A relationship between triads or keys whose roots lie a third apart but whose combination introduces chromatic notes, such as C major and E major. A shared note can connect the harmonies despite their different scales.

Character piece

A usually short instrumental work concentrated on a particular gesture, dance type or named character. Its form may be ternary or binary, and its title may suggest a narrative programme.

Song cycle

A sequence of songs designed to be heard as a larger work, connected by poetry, narrative, tonal planning or recurring music.

Thematic transformation

Recasting a recognisable theme through changes of metre, tempo, harmony, articulation or instrumentation. The transformed theme can acquire a different formal or dramatic role.

Leitmotif

A recurring musical idea associated with a dramatic element or relationship. Its modifications and combinations can convey memory, anticipation or connections beyond the characters’ immediate speech.

Cyclic form

A multi-movement design connected by returning themes or motives. The recurrence may be literal, transformed or recalled only briefly; it links movements without necessarily abolishing their separate forms.

Programme music

Instrumental music associated with a stated narrative, scene or sequence of ideas. A programme can guide the listener’s interpretation of the work’s harmonic and rhythmic organisation.

Rubato

Expressive flexibility in timing. It can involve a melody moving around a relatively stable accompaniment or a broader adjustment of phrase tempo; the practice depends on repertoire, instrument and performance tradition.

Quotations and teaching schematics are labelled separately. Playback uses synthesised instruments.

Editorial comparison

The funeral procession occupies only a narrow strip beneath the ruined Gothic window and leafless oaks. Architecture survives as a fragment, while the implied ceremony gives the small figures a temporal direction. The pairing concerns the same Romantic interest in ruins, memory and the incomplete that informs the song cycle and character piece. Documented useJohann Wolfgang von Goethe

Erlkönig — opening stanzas

1782
GermanWer reitet so spät durch Nacht und Wind? Es ist der Vater mit seinem Kind; Er hat den Knaben wohl in dem Arm, Er faßt ihn sicher, er hält ihn warm. “Mein Sohn, was birgst du so bang dein Gesicht?” “Siehst, Vater, du den Erlkönig nicht? Den Erlkönig mit Kron’ und Schweif?” “Mein Sohn, es ist ein Nebelstreif.”
English · Contrapunctus translationWho rides so late through night and wind? It is the father with his child; He has the boy held fast in his arm, He grips him firmly, he keeps him warm. “My son, why hide your face so fearfully?” “Father, can you not see the Erlking? The Erlking with crown and trailing train?” “My son, it is a streak of mist.”

Schubert’s 1815 setting, D 328, distributes the speakers by register and harmonic character while the piano’s repeated triplets maintain the ride. The father explains away what the child sees; the accompaniment refuses to relax when he does so. The final interruption of the running texture makes the narrator’s report physically different from everything preceding it. Goethe’s original is a ballad.
