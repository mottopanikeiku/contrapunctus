Period V · 1870–1937

The French Line

Colour, clarity and the escape from Wagner

Chords heard as colour, scales drawn from chant, from Java and from the whole-tone collection; orchestration as a precise art of timbre. Fauré refines, Debussy dissolves, Satie subtracts, and Ravel engineers.

The story

Between the Franco-Prussian War and Ravel’s death, French composers developed distinctive approaches to harmony, phrase and colour. Fauré, Debussy and Ravel knew German music, learned from it and disagreed about what to keep. Their alternatives concerned particular operations: how a chord moves, how a phrase ends, how an instrument enters and how much a work must explain. In Debussy, a parallel chord can behave as one moving object rather than a succession of harmonic functions. In Ravel, an apparently static repetition can reveal a long formal calculation. Different technical answers coexist within the same Parisian institutions.

The Société Nationale de Musique, founded in 1871, promoted French instrumental music in a culture whose principal public prestige had long belonged to opera. Conservatoire training preserved demanding exercises in harmony, counterpoint and fugue; the Schola Cantorum offered another educational route, with strong interests in chant and older polyphony. Fauré had studied at the École Niedermeyer, where church music and modal practices formed part of his training. Modes were available through sacred repertory as well as through new encounters with non-European music.

Mode and intervalA mode is a particular ordering of intervals. Raising the fourth degree of a major scale produces a different set of melodic and harmonic tendencies.

A major scale places semitones at two unequal positions; those positions help a leading note pull towards the tonic. A whole-tone collection divides the octave into six equal steps and contains no perfect fifth. It therefore withholds several familiar cues to tonal direction. An anhemitonic pentatonic collection has five pitches and no semitones, but its unequal spacing permits different centres and melodic profiles. Debussy juxtaposes such collections with ordinary diatonic and chromatic writing. The contrast between them matters more than an inventory of unusual scales: a returning semitone can suddenly restore a pressure that a preceding passage had suspended.

Harmony and lineWhat a chord can do

Parallel movement, often called planing, lets a chord retain its shape while moving through successive pitches. Strict transposition preserves every interval; diatonic parallelism adjusts the chord to remain within a scale. Both procedures can preserve a tonal centre. A bass pedal can hold the passage to one pitch while the upper harmonies move, and a later cadence can retrospectively clarify its direction. Debussy’s dissonances sometimes remain as parts of a sonority rather than resolving separately, while carefully placed resolutions carry weight elsewhere.

Fauré’s harmonic mobility often comes from independent lines and unobtrusive changes of bass. A chord acquires a new function through the movement of one part. His songs give French verse a flexible, closely measured declamation, with the accompaniment carrying continuity across pauses in the text. Ravel’s harmonic vocabulary can be more sharply profiled: a diatonic tune meets an added-note chord, a conventional dance acquires an unexpected bass, or a repeated pattern persists while its surroundings change. The notation frequently demands exact rhythm precisely where the surface seems easiest to play freely.

Satie removes other kinds of expected motion. The Gymnopédies of 1888 set simple melodic lines above recurring bass-and-chord patterns whose pace resists dramatic acceleration. His later titles and verbal instructions can place an ironic commentary beside the notes without requiring the performer to announce it. Poulenc draws on song, the music hall and older sacred practice while using abrupt changes of register and harmonic direction. A plainly shaped tune can inhabit a complex context. Across these composers, economy may mean limiting the material so that each alteration becomes more audible.

Mechanical colourStrings, pedals, breath

The modern grand piano supplied both power and sustain. French keyboard writing often requires the performer to distinguish layers: a held bass, soft inner figure and projected treble line need different weights of attack. Pedal changes can preserve a selected resonance while clearing other notes; their effectiveness depends on instrument and room. Ravel’s Gaspard de la nuit of 1908 makes repeated notes, tremolos and wide spacing structural features of the texture. Debussy’s préludes likewise distinguish sharply between dry articulation, overlapping resonance and a melodic line that must remain audible inside a moving figuration.

Two rival harpsThe Pleyel chromatic harp used crossing rows of strings. Érard’s double-action pedal harp changes the available pitch of each string through its pedal mechanism.

Instrument manufacturers sometimes commissioned the very works that demonstrated their machines. Debussy wrote the Danse sacrée et danse profane in 1904 for Pleyel’s chromatic harp and strings. Ravel’s Introduction et Allegro of 1905 was commissioned by Érard for pedal harp, flute, clarinet and string quartet. The pieces survive in the pedal-harp repertoire, but their origins record competing solutions to chromatic playing. A harp glissando, unlike a piano glissando, depends on which pitches its strings have been set to produce. The apparently effortless sweep is therefore prepared by mechanical choices.

French orchestration often makes the entrance of an instrument define a formal boundary. A flute’s low register, divided muted strings or a horn emerging from an inner part can alter the perceived weight of a chord without changing its pitches. In Ravel’s orchestral Ma mère l’Oye, new timbres and spacing develop relationships suggested by the original piano texture. Debussy’s Jeux, first performed in 1913, continually changes instrumental participation as short ideas appear and disappear. The orchestra’s articulation becomes one of the means by which the listener follows the work.

The imported and the localParis as an exchange

Debussy heard Javanese gamelan at the Paris Exposition of 1889. Its instruments use tunings different from those of the tempered piano. European exhibitions presented non-European cultures through colonial frameworks, and composers selected what they heard for their own purposes. Japanese prints offered another widely circulated resource: asymmetric framing, flattened areas of colour and cropped motifs challenged habits of European representation. Hokusai’s wave appeared in an adapted form on the first edition of La Mer in 1905.

Symbolist poetry provided a more direct compositional partnership. In Mallarmé, a sentence can withhold the identity or status of what it describes; in Verlaine, recurring sounds and uncertain references complicate an apparently simple line. Debussy’s Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune, premiered in 1894, responds to Mallarmé through a recurring flute idea in changing harmonic surroundings. Ravel’s songs can expose the precision of a particular verbal or visual conceit. Literature supplies methods of implication and interruption.

Prizes and accessLili Boulanger became the first woman to win the Prix de Rome in composition in 1913. The achievement marks an institutional barrier crossed.

Lili Boulanger joins modal writing, chromatic harmony and sharply differentiated orchestral masses. Her career also exposes the importance of competition, conservatoire networks and publication in obtaining performances. Salons, churches, concert societies and opera houses supported different kinds of work; Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes added an international institution joining composers to dancers, choreographers and painters. Satie’s Parade of 1917 brought Cocteau’s scenario and Picasso’s designs together with music that admitted the gestures of popular entertainment. Collaboration could change the status of what counted as concert material.

Scores and piano reductions circulated beside discs, reproducing-piano rolls and, increasingly during the 1920s, radio. These media favoured different durations and balances, while also allowing composers to hear performers and repertories outside their immediate circle. Jazz was encountered through touring musicians and recordings. Ravel’s late concertos use selected jazz inflections within highly organised forms. The old keyboard returned in another guise when Poulenc wrote his Concert champêtre for Wanda Landowska’s harpsichord. Mechanical inheritance and modern idiom could occupy the same instrument.

Terms of art

Vocabulary for the sounds and practices of this period.

Planing

Parallel motion of a chord treated as a single shape. Exact planing preserves all intervals by transposition; diatonic planing changes some intervals to keep the notes within one scale.

Whole-tone collection

Six pitches separated by whole tones, such as C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯. Its symmetry removes semitones and perfect fifths, weakening the leading-note and dominant relationships familiar from major–minor harmony.

Anhemitonic pentatonic scale

A five-note scale without semitones, such as C–D–E–G–A. Its intervals are unequal, unlike a whole-tone scale.

Modal harmony

Harmony drawing its notes and characteristic intervals from a mode. A tonal centre can remain clear without a raised leading note or conventional V–I cadence.

Mélodie

The French art song for voice and accompaniment, distinguished as a repertory from the chanson and popular song. Its musical syntax is closely affected by French prosody, vowel placement and the treatment of poetic line endings.

Arabesque

A flexible ornamental melodic line, often built from repeated curves or changing patterns.

Orchestral transcription

The recasting of existing music for orchestra. Register, articulation, instrumental decay and the distribution of simultaneous layers may be substantially rethought.

In the score

Quotations and teaching schematics are labelled separately. Playback uses synthesised instruments.

Editorial comparison

Seurat built his colour from separate touches meant to combine in the viewer’s eye, following the colour theory of Chevreul and Ogden Rood; when he reworked this canvas around 1887 he added small dots of complementary colour to the boy’s hat and the water. The affinity with Debussy and Ravel lies in their orchestral colour: they repeatedly set pure colours side by side — a flute’s low register beside muted horns, a harp harmonic on a string chord — so that the blend happens in the ear.

Art poétique — first and fourth stanzas

written 1874; published in Jadis et naguère, 1884
FrenchDe la musique avant toute chose, Et pour cela préfère l’Impair Plus vague et plus soluble dans l’air, Sans rien en lui qui pèse ou qui pose. Car nous voulons la Nuance encor, Pas la Couleur, rien que la nuance ! Oh ! la nuance seule fiance Le rêve au rêve et la flûte au cor !
English · Contrapunctus translationMusic before everything else, and for that, prefer the odd-numbered line, vaguer and more soluble in air, with nothing in it that weighs or poses. For what we want is Nuance still, not Colour — nothing but nuance! Oh, nuance alone can betroth dream to dream, and flute to horn!

A manifesto in nine-syllable lines — l’Impair, the odd count Verlaine recommends, which cannot divide into the symmetrical halves of the classical alexandrine. The musical analogue is the French line’s distrust of four-square phrasing and emphatic arrival. Verlaine was the poet these composers set most: Fauré’s La Bonne Chanson and Cinq mélodies “de Venise”, Debussy’s Ariettes oubliées and two sets of Fêtes galantes.
