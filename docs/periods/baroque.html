Period I · 1600–1750

The Baroque

Figured bass, fugue and the tempered keyboard

A bass line with numbers over it — the basso continuo — holds everything up. Above it, voices move in imitation, keys are chosen for their character, and a single mood (an affect) governs a whole movement. The fugue reaches its summit in Bach; opera and the concerto spread from Italy across Europe.

The story

Around 1600, a singer could deliver a dramatic text above a bass line while a keyboard player supplied the missing harmonies. This division of labour changed both composition and rehearsal: the page could leave some sounding parts to the performers. Opera, the solo cantata and the instrumental sonata developed around that practical arrangement. Renaissance polyphony continued alongside them. Church musicians studied imitative vocal writing, and Bach could place an old chorale inside a movement driven by concerto rhythms. The period called Baroque contains both a new insistence on the individual voice and increasingly elaborate ways of making independent voices work together.

Numbers above the bassA 6 normally asks for a sixth above the written bass; 6/4 asks for a sixth and fourth. The figures name intervals measured from that bass.

The basso continuo usually combined a chord-playing instrument — harpsichord, organ, lute or theorbo — with an instrument sustaining the bass. Its membership depended on place and occasion. A cellist might reinforce a harpsichord in a concerto, a bassoon join an organ in church, or a theorbo accompany a voice almost alone. The player reading the figures chose spacing, register, rhythm and often the number of notes. A modern edition that writes out these chords records one possible realisation of those choices.

Above this bass, dissonance could become a precisely timed event. A note consonant with one harmony was held while the bass changed, making a suspension, then moved by step to a consonance. Repeating a short pattern at successive pitches produced a sequence; descending fifths in the bass could carry it through several keys. Major–minor tonality developed through these recurring cadences and bass patterns. Rameau’s Traité de l’harmonie of 1722 gave chords an explanatory fundamental bass, which need not coincide with the lowest note actually played. Theory made an existing compositional practice newly systematic.

The employersChapel, theatre, household

A court paid musicians to mark rank and occasion: worship, dining, dancing, weddings and visits. A town church needed music tied to its calendar, often with little rehearsal. In Leipzig, where Bach became Thomaskantor in 1723, a cantata belonged within a service, beside readings, congregational chorales and a sermon. Its recitatives could explain the appointed text while an aria stopped to consider one implication. The resulting alternation of exposition and reflection also organised opera. Church cantatas and opera arias took their place within these larger occasions.

Venice’s first public opera house opened in 1637, making admission receipts another source of patronage without removing aristocratic investment. Singers became expensive attractions, and the da capo aria offered them an organised opportunity to display invention. An opening section established one state of feeling, a contrasting middle section changed the argument, and an instruction to return to the beginning invited fresh ornamentation. Handel brought Italian opera to London’s commercial theatre, then developed English oratorio for audiences who could hear biblical drama without seeing it staged. His choruses gave collective action a prominence quite different from the succession of solo arias.

Instrumental genres travelled between these institutions. Vivaldi taught and composed for the Ospedale della Pietà in Venice, whose female musicians attracted visitors to its performances. In a concerto, a returning orchestral passage — the ritornello — could establish the tonic, reappear in other keys and recover its opening authority at the end. Solo episodes supplied faster figuration and less stable harmony. A concerto grosso distributed the contrast between a small group and the larger ensemble. Changes of participation organised the listener’s attention.

The machinesPluck, bow, breath

A harpsichord’s jack carries a plectrum past the string when a key rises; the string is plucked rather than struck. Pressing harder does not give the graded loudness of a piano. A player instead articulates, spreads chords, changes texture or couples sets of strings. Two manuals can facilitate contrasts of registration and passages with crossed hands. The clavichord behaves differently: its metal tangent stays in contact with the string, permitting small changes of pressure and pitch, but its quiet tone suits an intimate room. The same keyboard notation could therefore demand different solutions on different instruments.

Unequal temperaments“Well-tempered” leaves open how Bach’s twelve semitones were tuned. Several circulating temperaments make every key usable while leaving their intervals unequal.

Keyboard tuning also affected composition. Pure fifths carried repeatedly around the circle do not return to an exact multiple of the starting frequency; some intervals must be adjusted. Meantone temperaments favoured clear thirds in a restricted group of keys, while other arrangements distributed the discrepancy more widely. Bach’s two books of The Well-Tempered Clavier, dated 1722 and 1742, explore preludes and fugues in all twenty-four major and minor keys, but specify no unique tuning recipe. The problem of temperament was material: a keyboard could not retune an awkward interval during a phrase as a singer or violinist might.

Gut strings, bow design and playing technique shaped the attack and decay of a violin ensemble. Instruments supplied by makers in Cremona often survive with later alterations to their original fittings. Recorders and transverse flutes coexisted; oboes and bassoons increasingly gave orchestras distinct reed colours. Natural trumpets and horns selected notes from the harmonic series rather than changing tubing with valves. In high trumpet writing the available harmonics lie close enough together to permit melodic passages. Bach’s ceremonial brass parts demand that specialised technique.

The written and unwrittenHow a style circulated

Printed music carried Italian concertos and sonatas across borders, while manuscripts remained indispensable in churches and courts. A published set could establish a composer’s reputation far beyond the place where the music was first heard. Bach copied and arranged Italian concertos, learning their organisation through the physical work of transcription. Couperin supplied tables explaining his keyboard ornaments, and in L’Art de toucher le clavecin of 1716 discussed the practical business of fingering and touch. Such instructions matter because a trill sign, a dotted rhythm or an apparently plain succession of notes did not mean precisely the same thing in every national practice.

The dance suite translated social movement into instrumental form. An allemande, courante, sarabande and gigue could share a tonic while differing in metre, tempo and accent; optional dances enlarged the sequence. French overtures set a weighty dotted opening against faster imitation. Scarlatti’s keyboard sonatas often use two repeated sections, the first moving away from the tonic and the second restoring it, but their hand crossings and repeated notes belong to a particular keyboard imagination. These designs draw their large-scale coherence from related keys, recurring rhythmic types and the placement of cadences.

Affect and rhetoricThe period’s writers discussed passions and figures of speech. A musical figure’s emotional meaning depended on its local context.

A movement could maintain one affect through varied expression. A lament’s descending bass permits different dissonances above each recurrence; a vigorous concerto movement can interrupt its regular pulse with a sustained solo note. In a fugue, a subject passes between voices, accompanied by material that must work in several vertical arrangements. Episodes derive motion from fragments of the subject. Bach’s counterpoint often makes the listener follow two kinds of time simultaneously: the immediate progress of each line and the slower harmonic itinerary generated by their combination.

Painting, poetry and music shared the work of making an argument perceptible. Poussin’s dancing figures turn abstract conditions of human life into a measured circle; theatrical perspective places rulers and gods inside calculated spaces. Dryden’s St Cecilia ode assigns different powers to trumpet, flute, violin and organ, and Handel’s setting makes those instrumental distinctions audible. Each art builds persuasion from ordered contrasts. In music, these include solo and chorus, consonance and suspension, repeated bass and changing upper part. A performer completes that argument through choices the notation deliberately leaves open.

Terms of art

Vocabulary for the sounds and practices of this period.

Basso continuo

A continuously supplied bass with improvised or partly improvised chords. Figures specify intervals above the bass; a keyboard or plucked instrument realises them, often with a bowed bass instrument reinforcing the written line.

Ritornello

A returning passage, especially the orchestral material framing and interrupting solo episodes in a concerto. Returns may be shortened or moved to another key; the final tonic return gives the movement harmonic closure.

Affect

A recognisable state of feeling articulated through rhythm, melodic gesture, harmony and delivery. A movement may sustain one principal affect, but Baroque practice also permits interruption, contrast and changes prompted by a text.

Fugue

Contrapuntal writing organised around successive entries of a subject in different voices. An opening answer usually enters at the dominant; episodes, further entries and overlapping entries develop the material within a flexible formal plan.

Suite

A sequence of movements, commonly stylised dances in a shared key. Metre and characteristic accent distinguish an allemande, courante, sarabande or gigue even when the music is intended for listening away from the dance floor.

Da capo aria

An aria with an opening section, contrasting middle section and return to the opening: A–B–A. A direction often sends the singer back to the opening, allowing fresh ornamentation.

Temperament

A practical adjustment of intervals so that a fixed-pitch instrument can play the required keys. Equal temperament divides the octave into twelve equal semitones; other systems preserve unequal key colours or favour particular intervals.

Quotations and teaching schematics are labelled separately. Playback uses synthesised instruments.

Shared context

Father Time plays a lyre while four figures dance in a ring. Poussin made the picture in Rome for Giulio Rospigliosi, the future Pope Clement IX, who also wrote opera librettos. The connection to Baroque music is both institutional and technical: an educated patron receives an allegory whose cyclical movement is made legible by measured gestures. The Wallace Collection record gives its date and provenance.

Documented useJohn Dryden

A Song for St Cecilia’s Day — opening

1687
EnglishFrom harmony, from heavenly harmony, This universal frame began. When Nature underneath a heap Of jarring atoms lay, And could not heave her head, The tuneful voice was heard from high, “Arise, ye more than dead!” Then cold, and hot, and moist, and dry, In order to their stations leap, And Music’s power obey.

These lines turn the ordering of the elements into a musical act. Handel set the poem in his Ode for St Cecilia’s Day, HWV 76, in 1739; later stanzas summon particular instruments and the passions attributed to them. The public-domain text supplies a direct example of musical rhetoric.
