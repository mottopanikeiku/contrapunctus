V1870–1937

The French Line

A flute alone, a piano full of bells

James McNeill WhistlerNocturne: Blue and Gold — Old Battersea Bridge, c. 1872–75
Tate Britain, London

The Story

On 22 December 1894, Gustave Doret conducted Debussy’s Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune at a Société Nationale de Musique concert in Paris. A flute began alone. Harp and horns answered; the orchestra had no trumpets, trombones or timpani. Mallarmé’s faun, dreaming of nymphs in the afternoon heat, had found a voice. The audience demanded the whole piece again.

The society had been founded in February 1871 by Saint-Saëns, Romain Bussine and their colleagues to get new French music performed. Fauré joined them. His education had taken place at the École Niedermeyer, where Saint-Saëns taught him; his first job, in 1866, was playing the organ in Rennes. He later taught Ravel at the Conservatoire. In 1905 the former organist became its director.

Mode and intervalC Lydian has the notes of C major with F raised to F♯. That one change puts a tritone above the home note.

The piano offers a quick way into these composers’ scales. C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯ divides the octave into six equal steps: the whole-tone collection, with neither a semitone nor a perfect fifth. C–D–E–G–A is pentatonic: five notes, with wider gaps from E to G and A to C. Its C–G fifth still has a firm ring.

Harmony and lineWhat a chord can do

A chord can travel intact. C–E–G rises to D–F♯–A and then E–G♯–B, every voice moving a whole tone. This is planing. In Debussy’s La cathédrale engloutie, open fifths climb through the piano like distant bells. Later, broad C-major chords fill both hands, an organ sounding above a deep bass C. When that tune returns softly, lower down, the cathedral has sunk beneath the water.

Fauré’s Clair de lune sets Verlaine’s masked dancers against a minuet in the piano. The singer moves at the pace of the words while the dance continues underneath. Verlaine’s figures scarcely believe in the happiness they sing about. Even their song of conquering love is in the minor.

Satie’s first Gymnopédie, written in 1888, begins with a low G, then a chord on the second beat; D takes the next bass note. Four bars pass before the melody enters. The music comes round again with almost everything in place, until F♮ replaces F♯ near the end and the last chord turns to D minor.

Mechanical colourStrings, pedals, breath

Ravel’s Gaspard de la nuit (1908) asks a piano to contain three of Aloysius Bertrand’s nightmares. In Le Gibet, B♭ tolls in octaves through the whole piece while chords gather and fade around it. The bell must keep its place among sounds written across three staves. In Scarbo, repeated notes and sudden leaps bring a goblin darting into the room. The pianist has rather more work to do than the dreamer.

Two rival harpsPleyel’s chromatic harp supplied the sharps and flats on a second row of strings. Érard’s harp changed their pitch with pedals.

In 1904 Pleyel commissioned Debussy’s Danse sacrée et danse profane to promote its chromatic harp. Érard answered the following year by commissioning Ravel’s Introduction et Allegro for its pedal harp, with flute, clarinet and string quartet. The sales demonstration became a little concerto. Flute and clarinet introduce a theme in thirds; the harp decorates it, then takes command. Debussy’s dances survived the commercial contest too, and are now played on the pedal harp.

Ravel’s Ma mère l’Oye began as piano duets. Two young Conservatoire pupils, Jeanne Leleu and Geneviève Durony, gave the first performance in Paris in 1910. When Ravel orchestrated the pieces, the fairy tales acquired physical bodies: a contrabassoon became the Beast, while harp, celesta and glockenspiel helped furnish Laideronnette’s tiny court.

The imported and the localParis as an exchange

At the Paris Exposition of 1889, Debussy kept returning to the Javanese village in the Dutch colonial section. There he heard the gamelan’s gongs and tuned percussion accompanying dancers. These instruments had tunings of their own. In Pagodes (1903), a low B and F♯ ring through the pedal like a gong; quicker figures and a slower tune occupy different parts of the keyboard. The hands move on while the bass keeps sounding.

A Japanese wave reached the printed score of La Mer in 1905. Durand’s cover used a detail from Hokusai’s Great Wave off Kanagawa, cropping away Mount Fuji and keeping the great curl of water.

Prizes and accessIn 1913 Lili Boulanger won the Prix de Rome in composition with Faust et Hélène, the first woman to take the prize.

Diaghilev’s Ballets Russes brought the painters into the theatre. For Satie’s Parade, first performed in Paris on 18 May 1917, Cocteau supplied the scenario, Picasso the designs and Massine the choreography. Satie’s orchestra included a typewriter, sirens and a pistol. The composer of the Gymnopédies could make a considerable racket when the occasion called for it.

Older instruments returned too. Wanda Landowska played the first performance of Poulenc’s harpsichord concerto Concert champêtre in Paris on 3 May 1929, with Pierre Monteux conducting. Ravel’s Piano Concerto in G, completed in 1931, opens with a whip-crack and sends blue notes through the piano and winds.

Ravel’s Boléro had already carried repetition to an outrageous length: two tunes, an obstinate snare drum, and an orchestra gradually filling the stage with sound. In a 1932 interview for the London Evening Standard, he said which scene he wanted behind it: “It was a factory which inspired my Boléro. I would like it always to be played with a vast factory in the background.”

Terms of Art

Planing

A chord moved up or down as a single shape. C–E–G becoming D–F♯–A preserves every interval; C–E–G becoming D–F–A stays on the white notes and changes the chord from major to minor.

Whole-tone collection

Six notes a whole tone apart: C–D–E–F♯–G♯–A♯, for example. Every step is the same size, so there is no semitone to act as a leading note and no perfect fifth to support a dominant chord.

Anhemitonic pentatonic scale

A five-note scale without semitones. C–D–E–G–A is one; the five black keys make another. Whole tones and minor thirds divide the octave unevenly.

Modal harmony

Chords drawn from a mode. In C Mixolydian, B♭ replaces the B♮ of C major: C can remain the home note while the familiar semitone pull of B to C disappears.

Mélodie

A French art song, usually for voice and piano. Fauré’s Clair de lune is a mélodie: Verlaine’s words have a sung line while the piano supplies a dance of its own.

Arabesque

An ornamental melody that curls around its notes, rising and falling in little arches. A flute run or a piano’s flowing upper line can take this shape.

Orchestral transcription

Music rewritten for orchestra from another set of instruments. Ravel’s Ma mère l’Oye moves from piano duet to winds, strings, harp and percussion; the Beast acquires the voice of a contrabassoon.

In the Score

Sister Arts

Men and boys rest beside the Seine, with factory chimneys on the far bank. Seurat planned this large canvas in studies before the Salon rejected it in 1884. Later he added dots of yellow and blue to the orange hat. That small patch makes a useful companion to Ravel’s orchestral mixtures: in Boléro, a flute joins a muted trumpet and the two become a new voice.

Paul Verlaine

Art poétique — first and fourth stanzas

written 1874; published in Jadis et naguère, 1884
FrenchDe la musique avant toute chose, Et pour cela préfère l’Impair Plus vague et plus soluble dans l’air, Sans rien en lui qui pèse ou qui pose. Car nous voulons la Nuance encor, Pas la Couleur, rien que la nuance ! Oh ! la nuance seule fiance Le rêve au rêve et la flûte au cor !
Our translationMusic before everything else, and for that, prefer the odd-numbered line, vaguer and more soluble in air, with nothing in it that weighs or poses. For what we want is Nuance still, not Colour — nothing but nuance! Oh, nuance alone can betroth dream to dream, and flute to horn!

Verlaine’s Art poétique, collected in Jadis et naguère in 1884, asks for music first and makes its case in nine-syllable lines. The fourth stanza actually pairs a flute with a horn. Fauré gave this poet’s masked dancers a minuet in Clair de lune; the piano carries on dancing beneath words that doubt their own happiness.

Composers

1845–1924

Gabriel Fauré

He spent his days at the organ and in the classroom, saving for songs and chamber music the harmonies nobody else could quite imagine. 1862–1918

Claude Debussy

He trusted his ear over the rules — “pleasure is the law” — and made the piano, the flute and the orchestra sound new. 1866–1925

Erik Satie

The composer of the <i>Gymnopédies</i> asked pianists to prepare for 840 repetitions with ‘serious immobilities’. 1875–1937

Maurice Ravel

He made a ballet from two tunes and a snare drum, and gave his war dead a suite of dances. 1893–1918

Lili Boulanger

At nineteen she won the Prix de Rome; before her death at twenty-four she had written songs of lost love and psalms that shake the room. 1899–1963

Francis Poulenc

The pianist who wanted butter in the sauce wrote prayers, love songs and an opera in which a guillotine silences the choir.
