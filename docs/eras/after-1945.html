VII1945–present

After 1945

Piano silent, orchestra playing

Paul KleeAd Parnassum, 1932
Kunstmuseum Bern

Overview

On 7 June 1945, Peter Pears sang the title role in Britten’s Peter Grimes at Sadler’s Wells in London. The fisherman faces an inquest over his apprentice’s death, and then the suspicion of a whole Suffolk village. The Borough has a chorus for its accusations, and Pears’s solo voice is set against the massed villagers. In the Act II Passacaglia, a solo viola plays above a seven-note bass that repeats throughout. The phrases of the variations do not begin and end with the statements of the bass. The orchestra carries the scene towards the death of a second apprentice.

The summer courses at Darmstadt began in 1946. Within a few years, the city was a meeting place for composers who wanted to extend serial ordering beyond pitch. In Messiaen’s piano study Mode de valeurs et d’intensités (1949), each pitch returns with its own allotted length, loudness and touch. Boulez took the first division of Messiaen’s pitch collection for Structures I, then subjected the piano’s other instructions to serial tables too. The notes jump across the keyboards, some struck hard, others barely sounded. Boulez and Messiaen played the first section together in Paris in May 1952.

Rows beyond pitchA twelve-note row puts pitches in an order. Integral serialism applied the same principle to other parameters, so that a numbered list also governs the lengths of the notes played by Boulez’s two pianists.

Boulez also commissioned Messiaen’s Oiseaux exotiques (1955–56), which was first played in his Domaine musical concerts with Yvonne Loriod at the piano. The work is scored for solo piano with winds and percussion. A low oboe and clarinet play the nasal call of the prairie chicken, and the last piano cadenza sets the bobolink against the catbird in two-part counterpoint. In the middle of the work, snare drum and woodblock play rhythms drawn from Hindu and Greek music.

The studioMusique concrète, tape and spectralism

At French radio in 1948, Pierre Schaeffer made a composition from the sounds of a railway. His Étude aux chemins de fer rearranges recorded train sounds into recurring phrases. The early musique concrète studies used discs. A closed groove could hold a fragment and make it repeat, and a change of playback speed altered its pitch. With magnetic tape the loop became a physical object, a strip of tape joined end to end and played round again by the machine.

In Stockhausen’s Gesang der Jünglinge, a recording of a boy singing is combined with tones from electronic generators. Words from the Book of Daniel are heard clearly at some points and broken into fragments at others. Stockhausen layered recordings of the same boy’s voice to make a choir from one singer, and loudspeakers distribute the voices around the room. A vowel can merge with an electronic tone, and the next syllable is again recognisably sung by a human voice.

A single low E supplies the harmonic starting point for Gérard Grisey’s Partiels (1975). The ensemble builds chords from the components within a musical sound, called partials. In a harmonic spectrum their frequencies are whole-number multiples of a fundamental; some of the higher ones fall between the notes of a piano. Grisey wrote quarter-tones and smaller adjustments to reach them. In this spectral approach the players’ sounds fuse into a single invented instrument, and the chord then roughens towards noise.

IRCAM opened in Paris in 1977, bringing composers into the same institution as acousticians and computer researchers. For Lichtbogen (1986), Saariaho used its computers to analyse short cello sounds as they moved from a clear artificial harmonic into a complex multiphonic. She turned stages of that change in the bowed sound into chords for the ensemble. Her rhythms also came from computer calculations, which she transcribed into playable notation for nine musicians with live electronics.

Keeping the electronics playableÉric Daubresse rebuilt the resonating instruments of Grisey’s Prologue as computer models. In 2001 Garth Knox played the viola at the première of the new version at IRCAM, where the software produced the rings and rattles of the earlier instruments in response to his live playing.

The performerCage’s 4′33″, Woodstock, 1952

On 29 August 1952, at the Maverick Concert Hall in Woodstock, David Tudor performed John Cage’s 4′33″. He played no piano note in any of its three movements. What could be heard came from the audience and from the sounds around the hall. Cage had used chance differently in Music of Changes, composed the year before. He consulted the I Ching while deciding what to write, and the result was a fully specified piano part for Tudor, with the pedalling also written out.

A player chooses the lengthIn Feldman’s Durations, the players begin together, then choose how long to hold their written notes within a broad tempo. The pitches are fixed, and the chords formed where the notes overlap change from performance to performance.

Feldman was in Houston for the opening of the Rothko Chapel in 1971 when its donors asked him for a piece. Fourteen paintings filled the octagonal room. In his Rothko Chapel, the viola begins with a wide-ranging solo, punctuated by quiet timpani; wordless choral chords sound between the instrumental phrases. Near the end, the viola plays a melody he had written as a schoolboy, over a repeating vibraphone figure. His later music asked for much longer stretches of quiet. String Quartet No. 2 can last more than five hours.

Ligeti’s Atmosphères (1961) writes forty-eight string lines that together sound as one slowly contracting chord. Twenty-eight violins descend while twenty violas and cellos climb, all playing so softly that the separate melodies merge. The technique is known as micropolyphony, counterpoint packed so closely that no single strand stands out. The canon narrows from three and a half octaves to a minor third. In another passage double basses six octaves lower cut off high piccolos, and near the end the piano’s strings are brushed.

Return and differenceMinimalism and tintinnabuli

Late in 1966, Reich recorded a short piano pattern, made a tape loop and tried playing against it himself, and found that a technique discovered with tape recorders could be done by hand. In Piano Phase (1967), two pianists begin with the same repeated phrase. One holds the tempo while the other speeds up a little and moves ahead until the notes lock into a new alignment. Each shift brings different notes together. In this strand of minimalism, the process of repetition determines the music. The players work through the offsets until they reach unison again, then change the pattern.

John Adams developed Shaker Loops (1978) with students at the San Francisco Conservatory. Seven string players repeat small figures, and rapid bow tremolos and trills supply the shaking of the title. The piece grew from a failed quartet, which Adams enlarged to give the sound more force. In 1987 his Nixon in China was staged at Houston Grand Opera, with a verse libretto by Alice Goodman and a production by Peter Sellars. A large replica of Air Force One brought Nixon, his wife and Kissinger onto the stage to meet a line of Chinese officials.

In Pärt’s Für Alina (1976), the piano’s two voices remain exposed. One moves through the scale; the other takes its notes from the B minor triad, B–D–F♯. This is tintinnabuli, in which a rule determines how the triadic voice accompanies the melody. The phrases grow by one note at a time and then contract. At one point the triadic voice touches C♯, outside the triad, and the pedal that has sustained the preceding sounds is released.

In Kurtág’s Játékok the whole palm falls onto the piano keys, and fists and elbows are used alongside the fingertips. These little games also include greetings and memorials to friends. György and Márta Kurtág made them part of their concert repertoire. In György’s Hungarian Radio recordings of 1978, each of the two Palm Stroke pieces lasts less than half a minute.

Gubaidulina learnt the bayan’s possibilities from Friedrich Lips. When she finished De profundis in 1978, she brought it to his class and played it to him on the grand piano. The work calls for clusters and sliding pitches on the button accordion, and for the sound of air passing through the instrument when the air button is pressed. Before Lips could play it in Scandinavia, he had to take the score to an official of the Soviet Ministry of Culture, who questioned him about its unfamiliar signs.

The third movement of Adès’s Asyla (1997), Ecstasio, brings a rave into the orchestra. Repeated beats build up in instruments that usually play symphonies. The score calls for six percussionists and two keyboard players, and one upright piano is tuned a quarter-tone flat. The percussion list includes a bag of metal knives and forks.

Britten and Pears built a public for new music as well as writing and performing it. The first Aldeburgh Festival opened in June 1948 with Arts Council help; Albert Herring was played at the Jubilee Hall and Saint Nicolas at the parish church. Britten also wrote for television. David Attenborough, then head of BBC2, commissioned Britten’s Owen Wingrave, about a young man refusing his family’s military profession. It was filmed at Snape Maltings and first broadcast on 16 May 1971.

In 1978, at Mills College in Oakland, Cage recorded a reading of Empty Words lasting about ten hours. He had taken his words from Thoreau’s Journal and broken them down across four parts, progressively removing the larger units of language. In the last part only letters and vocal sounds remain.

Terms of Art

Integral serialism

Integral serialism applies ordered series to note lengths and other features of sound as well as pitch. In Boulez’s Structures I, the tables also govern the pianist’s touch, so that how hard each note is struck is part of the plan.

Indeterminacy

Indeterminacy leaves a specified choice open during performance. In Feldman’s Durations, the notes are written but the players choose their lengths. Cage’s Music of Changes uses chance earlier, during composition, and gives the pianist a fully written part.

Musique concrète

Musique concrète is music made from recorded sounds. Schaeffer’s Étude aux chemins de fer takes its sounds from trains; his early disc experiments repeat fragments through closed grooves and alter their pitch by changing playback speed.

Micropolyphony

Micropolyphony packs independent moving parts so closely together that they merge into a single changing sound. In Ligeti’s Atmosphères, a forty-eight-part string canon contracts into a narrow chord as the violins descend and the violas and cellos climb.

Phase shifting

Phase shifting gradually changes the alignment of a repeated pattern against a second copy. In Reich’s Piano Phase, one pianist briefly plays faster while the other holds steady. The same notes meet in new combinations as the parts move apart.

Spectralism

Spectral music builds its harmonies from the components within a sound. Grisey’s Partiels starts from the harmonic spectrum of a low E. An ensemble playing quarter-tones and smaller adjustments can use pitches that fall between the piano’s keys.

Tintinnabuli

Tintinnabuli pairs a scale-based melody with notes chosen from a single triad according to a rule. In Pärt’s Für Alina, that triad is B minor. Its notes B, D and F♯ may sound consonantly against the melody or form close dissonant intervals with it.

Extended technique

An extended technique changes the customary way an instrument is played. Ligeti has the piano’s strings brushed in Atmosphères; Gubaidulina writes for the sound of the bayan’s air button in De profundis.

In the Score

Sister Arts

Mondrian arrived in New York in 1940 and grew fond of boogie-woogie. In Broadway Boogie Woogie, red and blue blocks interrupt the yellow grid; the black lines of his earlier paintings are gone. The small blocks are distributed across a fixed framework. Reich’s Piano Phase uses a similarly small stock of notes, whose shifting positions produce new combinations.

Henry David Thoreau

Walden — from “Sounds”

1854
EnglishI did not read books the first summer; I hoed beans. Nay, I often did better than this. There were times when I could not afford to sacrifice the bloom of the present moment to any work, whether of the head or hands. I love a broad margin to my life.

In Walden, Thoreau followed these words with an account of a morning spent in his doorway, from sunrise to noon, while birds sang around the house. Cage used another of his books, the Journal, for Empty Words (1974). Across its four parts he removed first sentences, then smaller units of language, until only letters and sounds were left for the voice.

Composers

1908–1992

Olivier Messiaen

Organist at La Trinité for sixty years, whose music drew on birdsong and was played by the pianist Yvonne Loriod. 1912–1992

John Cage

Composer who put bolts between piano strings, composed with chance operations, and wrote a piece of four minutes and thirty-three seconds made up of the sounds around a concert. 1913–1976

Benjamin Britten

Composer who wrote Grimes and Aschenbach for Peter Pears, a work with an ark full of animals for Suffolk children, and a requiem in which dead soldiers speak. 1918–1990

Leonard Bernstein

American conductor, pianist and composer, music director of the New York Philharmonic from 1958 to 1969 and composer of West Side Story and the Chichester Psalms. 1922–2001

Iannis Xenakis

Greek composer, born in Romania and naturalised French in 1965, who trained as an engineer, worked for Le Corbusier in Paris and composed with probability theory, set theory and computers. 1923–2006

György Ligeti

Composer of orchestral works in which dozens of moving parts are contained within one sustained sound, and of piano études in which the two hands play at different speeds. 1925–2016

Pierre Boulez

Composer of music built on explosive attacks and long echoes, who also founded the ensembles, concert series and laboratories needed to perform it. 1926–1987

Morton Feldman

Composer of quiet, softly struck piano writing and of a string quartet that can last six hours. b. 1926

György Kurtág

Composer known for <i>Játékok</i>, which began as children’s piano games and grew to include pieces for friends, birthdays and farewells. 1931–2025

Sofia Gubaidulina

Her <i>Offertorium</i>, for Gidon Kremer’s violin, takes apart and rebuilds Bach’s royal theme. b. 1935

Arvo Pärt

Composer who rebuilt his music on a melody and three notes, and who wrote a lament for Britten based on a descending scale. b. 1936

Steve Reich

Composer who built music from two tape machines drifting out of time with each other, and later applied the same phasing process to pianists, drummers and singers. b. 1937

Philip Glass

American composer and keyboard player, born in Baltimore, who wrote operas, symphonies, string quartets and film scores and led his own ensemble in New York. b. 1947

John Adams

A New England clarinettist and composer, known for bringing Nixon to the stage and for a fast orchestral piece driven by a woodblock beat. 1952–2023

Kaija Saariaho

Composer who built music from the timbre of a single cello note and wrote an opera about two people who had never met. b. 1971

Thomas Adès

Composer who wrote fishing reels into the pit orchestra of an opera.
