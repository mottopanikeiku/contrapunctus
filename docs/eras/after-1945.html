VIIChapter 8 · 1945–present

After 1945

Total control, chance, texture, pulse — and then everything at once

Paul KleeAd Parnassum, 1932
Kunstmuseum Bern

Key ideas

  • Integral serialism. Serial organisation applied to parameters beyond pitch, potentially including duration, dynamics and attack.
  • Indeterminacy. The deliberate leaving-open of some aspect of a work’s realisation, such as ordering, coordination or choice of material.
  • Musique concrète. Composition beginning with recorded sounds treated as material for selection, transformation and combination.

8.1The Story

Musical life after the Second World War pursued several paths. Britten’s Peter Grimes, first performed in London in 1945, rebuilt public opera around an English text and an individual’s conflict with a community. Within a few years, composers meeting at Darmstadt were examining ways to organise pitch, duration and dynamics through related numerical procedures. Radio studios made it possible to compose directly with recorded or electronically generated sound. These practices required different institutions, performers and kinds of listening. Their coexistence defines the period.

The Darmstadt summer courses, established in 1946, provided an international meeting place after years of war and censorship. Boulez and other composers studied the possibilities of extending serial organisation beyond pitch. Messiaen’s piano study Mode de valeurs et d’intensités of 1949 associated pitches with particular durations, dynamics and attacks, influencing experiments that went beyond his own method. Integral serialism could order several parameters, but composers differed in how strictly they coordinated them. A numerical plan still left choices about density, register, pacing and what a listener could distinguish within the resulting texture.

Interacting parametersA loud note changes its spectrum; a short attack may not establish a stable pitch. Serial tables can separate properties that an instrument physically joins.

Messiaen’s own music draws on birdsong, Catholic theology, modes and rhythmic procedures extending well beyond serialism. Added values interrupt regular pulse, while non-retrogradable rhythms read the same forwards and backwards. Boulez allowed controlled alternatives in some works and revised scores over long periods. The score became a place to specify relationships beyond the capacities of ordinary bars and recurring key signatures. Unusual subdivisions, detailed dynamics and differentiated attacks increased the demand for specialist rehearsal, even when the instrumental forces remained small.

The studioSound becomes editable material

Pierre Schaeffer’s experiments with musique concrète began in Paris in 1948, taking recorded sounds as their starting material. Magnetic tape subsequently allowed a composer to cut, splice, loop, reverse and change the speed of sound. Electronic studios could generate tones and noise, then combine them by filtering and mixing. The distinction between recorded and synthesised sources mattered technically, although composers soon crossed it. A tape piece fixed particular sounds and timings, while loudspeakers, level and the room shaped each playback.

Radio institutions supplied expensive equipment, engineers and time, becoming patrons of composition as well as distributors. Later computer analysis and synthesis made the internal components of a sound available for calculation. IRCAM opened in Paris in 1977 as a centre joining research and musical production. Saariaho’s work there helped her develop relationships between instrumental tone, breath noise and electronic transformation. Live electronics added another practical problem: a system had to react during performance. Scores could now require software, microphones, loudspeaker placement and technicians alongside instrumental parts.

Spectral composition takes the changing frequency components of sound as a resource for harmony, orchestration and form. A harmonic spectrum contains partials related by whole-number multiples; some fall between the pitches of an equal-tempered keyboard. Composers such as Gérard Grisey and Tristan Murail used this discrepancy productively, constructing instrumental approximations and transformations. Saariaho extends related thinking into song, chamber music and opera. A change from noisy attack to sustained tone can become a formal process, just as a nineteenth-century movement might organise a progression between keys.

The performerWhat can remain undecided

John Cage distinguished methods that used chance during composition from situations leaving aspects of performance undetermined. Those are different kinds of openness: a score produced by chance can still prescribe every resulting note. In 4′33″, first performed in 1952, the player’s timed non-playing directs attention towards sounds already present in the environment. Graphic notation and verbal instructions offered other ways to specify actions without giving every pitch on a staff. Composer, performer, mechanism and circumstance could each determine different aspects of a work.

Listening at low volumeA soft instrumental attack has a register, duration and spectrum. At low volume, the time between attacks and the behaviour of the room can become unusually prominent.

Feldman explored very soft dynamics, unstable repetition and, in later works, extended duration. Patterns return with small differences that make exact recollection difficult; the listener cannot always decide whether a figure has repeated or changed. His friendships with New York painters were working relationships with another artistic community. Rothko Chapel of 1971, for chorus, solo voices, viola, celesta and percussion, responds to a specific place and its paintings. The placement of a viola phrase or choral chord matters within an acoustic and visual environment already charged with sustained attention.

Ligeti arrived at dense textures through minutely organised independent lines. In Atmosphères of 1961, many close pitches and overlapping changes make individual parts difficult to follow, although the parts remain precisely composed. Micropolyphony transfers attention from the progress of one line to the transformation of a mass. His later music investigates sharply audible rhythmic layers. Both approaches ask how many simultaneous processes a listener can follow before separate events fuse into another perceptual object. A change of compositional method changes the threshold between line, pulse and texture.

Return and differencePulse, triad, memory

Reich makes a process audible through repeated patterns and changing alignment. In tape and instrumental phasing, versions of a pattern gradually separate, revealing combinations concealed in unison. Repetition exposes change. Minimalism includes several practices, with differing uses of consonance and regular beat. John Adams brings repeated figures into larger dramatic and harmonic trajectories, including opera, while preserving opportunities for a pulse to change weight or break. The relation between a small repeated unit and a long span replaces some of the work formerly done by contrasting themes.

Pärt’s tintinnabuli style, developed in the 1970s, combines a line moving through a scale with another drawing on the notes of a triad according to a rule. The moving line creates dissonances against the triadic voice. Kurtág concentrates gestures into short spans whose articulation and silence must be exact. Gubaidulina gives instrumental actions and contrasts a structural and often spiritual significance; her De profundis of 1978 makes the bayan, a button accordion, a solo concert instrument. These composers recover inherited sounds within distinct harmonic languages.

Acoustic resourcesA bow pressed heavily against a string can alter the balance of pitched vibration and friction. An acoustic instrument can therefore support a composition concerned with noise and spectrum.

The modern orchestra remains usable alongside amplified ensembles, electronics and older instruments. Extended string techniques expose friction, pressure and unstable harmonics; multiphonics ask wind instruments to produce complex sounds beyond a conventional single tone. The full iron-framed piano can be struck, prepared or played inside as well as through its keys, while harpsichords and period instruments invite different constraints of attack and decay. Adès’s Asyla of 1997 brings the repetitive pressure of club music into a large acoustic orchestra. Each choice of apparatus brings its own powers of articulation.

Opera companies, festivals, universities, foundations and broadcasting organisations now share patronage with artist-run ensembles and individual commissions. Long-playing records and later digital media changed how extended works could circulate; repeated listening could reveal relationships inaccessible at a single premiere. Britten also wrote for broadcast media, including the television opera Owen Wingrave, first transmitted in 1971. A score may survive while its original electronic equipment becomes unusable, making maintenance and migration part of performance practice. Conversely, a portable instrumental piece can travel through small venues without the infrastructure on which a technically simpler-looking electronic work depends.

The other arts provide specific models for those decisions about time and attention. Mondrian’s New York paintings distribute small coloured units across a grid, giving regular division and local displacement equal prominence. Thoreau’s prose attends to durations that ordinary work would treat as empty; Cage later made texts from his Journal into material for performances. These comparisons identify a practical problem shared across media: how repetition, spacing, an imposed frame or an interruption can change what a viewer or listener notices while the basic material stays the same.

8.2Terms of Art

The vocabulary you need to hear this period clearly.

Integral serialism

Serial organisation applied to parameters beyond pitch, potentially including duration, dynamics and attack. Systems vary in which parameters they order and how they coordinate them.

Indeterminacy

The deliberate leaving-open of some aspect of a work’s realisation, such as ordering, coordination or choice of material. It differs from using chance to compose a score whose final performance instructions are fixed.

Musique concrète

Composition beginning with recorded sounds treated as material for selection, transformation and combination. Tape editing, looping and changes of playback speed can organise sounds without first translating them into conventional notation.

Micropolyphony

Closely interwoven independent parts whose individual movements become difficult to hear separately. Changes in density, register and spacing produce the audible form of the resulting texture; the underlying notation may remain highly exact.

Phase shifting

A gradual change in alignment between repeated versions of a pattern. A small difference of speed produces new offsets and composite rhythms; a sequence of fixed offsets can demonstrate the results without reproducing the gradual process.

Spectralism

Compositional thinking that uses the frequency components and temporal behaviour of sound to inform pitch, timbre and form. Spectral models undergo transformation and orchestration.

Tintinnabuli

Pärt’s method of relating a scale-based melodic voice to a voice restricted to a triad. A chosen rule determines their relationship, producing both consonance and friction without conventional functional chord changes.

Extended technique

A way of playing outside an instrument’s customary concert practice, such as bowing at an unusual contact point, producing wind multiphonics or sounding a piano’s strings directly. The resulting noise, pitch or altered attack becomes specified musical material.

8.5Composers

1908–1992

Olivier Messiaen

An organist who made Catholic theology, symmetrical modes, unequal rhythms and transcribed birdsong the materials of a new musical language. 1913–1976

Benjamin Britten

An opera composer who made the English speaking voice, the Suffolk coast and the repeated bass into instruments of drama. 1923–2006

György Ligeti

He packed canons so closely that their lines disappeared, then made competing pulses and unequal tunings audible again. 1925–2016

Pierre Boulez

He extended the series beyond pitch, then spent decades turning its ordered material into proliferating instrumental forms. 1926–1987

Morton Feldman

Soft attacks, unequal repetitions and lengths beyond ordinary musical memory replace the argument towards a climax. b. 1926

György Kurtág

A composer of messages, memorials and interrupted sentences, who can make two notes carry the weight of a scene. 1931–2025

Sofia Gubaidulina

She made the crossing of registers, the pressure of a bow and the breath of an accordion carry the argument of a sacred drama. b. 1935

Arvo Pärt

He joined a moving scale to the notes of a single triad, then used that rule to rebuild his musical language. b. 1936

Steve Reich

He made the changing relationship between repeated patterns audible, then brought speech, Hebrew psalms and recorded testimony into the process. b. 1947

John Adams

Repeated pulses support changing harmonies, long orchestral arguments and operas about the people who make public history. 1952–2023

Kaija Saariaho

She made the passage between a bowed note and the noise around it into a way of composing harmony, form and opera. b. 1971

Thomas Adès

His harmonies move by independent interval cycles, so a familiar chord can become unfamiliar without any voice breaking its rule.
