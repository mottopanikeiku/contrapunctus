IV1880–1918

Fin de siècle

The orchestra at night

Gustav KlimtBeethoven Frieze: The Well-Armed Strong One, 1901–02
Secession Building, Vienna (Belvedere)

Overview

On 21 April 1915, Sibelius saw sixteen swans and wrote a theme in his diary: “One of the great experiences of my life! My God, what beauty!” The theme was used in the finale of his Fifth Symphony. There, after fast string writing, the horns play a broad, rocking figure, and a separate woodwind melody moves above it.

By this time a score could call on several sizes of the same instrument. A clarinettist might change to a bass clarinet; a fourth bassoon player could take up the contrabassoon. Mahler’s Third Symphony needs eight horns, two harpists and two timpanists, as well as women’s and children’s choruses. Its first movement opens with a horn call. The same score includes a minuet with a solo oboe.

Large forces, small texturesMahler’s Third places a brass solo offstage in its third movement. The solo is heard over a quiet accompaniment.

Strauss’s Don Quixote, composed in 1897, gives the knight to a solo cello and gives Sancho Panza parts for bass clarinet, tenor tuba and viola. In the second variation the knight attacks a flock of sheep, and the orchestra imitates their bleating. Later a wind machine depicts the rush of air during an imaginary flight on a wooden horse.

The tonal argumentCommon-tone modulation and unresolved keys

A chord can change its surroundings while keeping one note in place. In the example below, C major becomes A♭ major with C still sounding. A♭ can then be renamed G♯ and kept inside an E-major chord. The bass moves through three major chords without a dominant–tonic cadence. A melody can continue through the change while the bass makes the larger move.

Strauss left the keys unreconciled at the end of Also sprach Zarathustra. High woodwinds play B major more and more softly, while the basses return to C, the note on which the work began. The two keys stay a semitone apart, and the work ends without resolving them.

Rachmaninoff’s Second Piano Concerto begins with the piano alone, playing bell-like chords. When the orchestra’s C-minor melody enters, the soloist plays an accompaniment beneath it. The piano can support a long orchestral phrase and still be clearly heard, because the hands move through the chords while the sustaining pedal keeps the low notes sounding. Later the piano has a melodic passage of its own.

Scriabin wrote for a keyboard of another kind. His Prometheus: The Poem of Fire, composed in 1909–10, includes a part for a keyboard controlling coloured light. A solo piano plays with a large orchestra; near the end, a chorus can join, singing wordless vowels. The work ends on an F♯-major chord. At the Moscow premiere in 1911 Scriabin played the piano part, but the light part was not performed.

The human voiceSong, symphony, stage

In Mahler’s Das Lied von der Erde, written in 1908–09, two singers take turns through six movements with orchestra. The final farewell is built from short phrases, with stretches where the voice sings over very sparse accompaniment. At the end, the C major chord contains an added sixth, A, held in the flute and clarinet. The singer repeats “ewig”, forever, as the orchestra thins out.

HeterophonyA flute can decorate a tune while an oboe plays its simpler outline. Both follow the same melody, meeting on some notes and parting on others.

Janáček collected another sort of vocal music on the streets and on trains. From 1897 he wrote down fragments of speech in musical notation, often noting who had spoken, where, and in what circumstances. His opera Jenůfa, first performed in Brno in 1904, sets a prose play. Its short vocal phrases follow a remark as it changes from an ordinary one into an accusation. Repeated figures in the orchestra continue while a character struggles for the next words.

In Puccini’s Tosca Cavaradossi is to be executed, and Tosca believes the firing squad will only pretend to shoot him. While he waits for the execution, he sings E lucevan le stelle, recalling their nights together. The aria lasts a few minutes. When the stage action resumes, the firing squad shoots him with real bullets.

In the fourth movement of Mahler’s Third Symphony, an alto sings Nietzsche’s midnight song, asking what deep midnight says, over slow rocking notes in the orchestra. In the next movement, children imitate bells with “bimm, bamm”.

The institutionsTheatres, exhibitions and recordings

Getting a new opera into a theatre could take years. Prague turned down Jenůfa before its Brno premiere and went on rejecting it afterwards. The Prague production came in 1916, with changes made by the conductor Karel Kovařovic. Max Brod then translated the libretto into German and brought the opera to the attention of Universal Edition.

Vienna’s Secession exhibition of 1902 placed Beethoven at the centre of a room. Max Klinger supplied the sculpture; Klimt painted his Beethoven Frieze for a neighbouring wall. Twenty-one artists worked on the exhibition under Josef Hoffmann. Klimt’s figures pass through suffering towards an embrace and a choir of angels, following a reading of Beethoven’s Ninth.

A side of a recordAn acoustic recording collected sound through a horn. Its short playing time could force a composer to cut a piece before recording it.

On 26 June 1914 Elgar conducted his first recording of Pomp and Circumstance No. 1. The ensemble was made up of about thirty musicians recruited from London orchestras. The march was cut to just over four minutes and began with its big trio tune. Customers could buy Elgar’s own performance on disc, with the music rearranged to fit it.

After the first performance of his Fifth Symphony, given on his fiftieth birthday in 1915, Sibelius returned to the work. He revised it twice and completed the final version in 1919. The swan theme was kept through both revisions. At the end the orchestra plays six separate chords with rests between them.

Terms of Art

Progressive tonality

A plan in which a work begins in one key and ends in another. The final key is the goal of the work as a whole.

Orchestral song

A song for a solo voice accompanied by an orchestra. It may have been written for these forces or arranged from an earlier version for voice and piano.

Tone poem

An orchestral piece, usually in one continuous movement, written around a story, character, landscape or other stated subject. “Symphonic poem” means the same thing.

Enharmonic reinterpretation

Giving a note or chord a new role in the harmony by changing its spelling. G♯ and A♭ share a key on an equal-tempered piano, but their names describe different relationships to the notes around them.

Added sixth

A note a sixth above a chord’s root, included alongside the ordinary three notes of the chord. Adding A to C–E–G makes a C-major chord with an added sixth.

Pedal point

A note held or repeated while the chords around it change. It usually lies in the bass and may clash with some of the passing harmonies.

Heterophony

Two or more versions of the same melody sounding together. One part may add ornaments or change the rhythm while another keeps the simpler tune.

Sister Arts

The figure presses its hands to its head while two companions walk away. Behind it, the sky and fjord bend into red and blue curves. In a notebook entry of 1892, Munch described a walk with two friends when the sky turned blood-red and he felt a scream passing through nature.

Friedrich Nietzsche

The midnight song from Also sprach Zarathustra

1884; repeated in Part IV, 1885
GermanO Mensch! Gib acht! Was spricht die tiefe Mitternacht? “Ich schlief, ich schlief —, Aus tiefem Traum bin ich erwacht: — Die Welt ist tief, Und tiefer als der Tag gedacht. Tief ist ihr Weh —, Lust — tiefer noch als Herzeleid: Weh spricht: Vergeh! Doch alle Lust will Ewigkeit —, — will tiefe, tiefe Ewigkeit!”
Our translationO human being, pay heed! What does the deep midnight say? “I slept, I slept — From a deep dream I have awakened: The world is deep, Deeper than the day has thought. Deep is its grief — Delight is deeper still than heartache: Grief says: Pass away! But all delight desires eternity — Desires deep, deep eternity!”

Mahler set these words for alto in the fourth movement of his Third Symphony. Strauss took chapter titles from the same book for Also sprach Zarathustra, composed in 1896; near its end, a bell strikes midnight. In Nietzsche’s text, the clock’s numbered strokes fall between the lines of the poem. They are omitted here.

Composers

1854–1928

Leoš Janáček

Composer who wrote down the voices of strangers in Brno and wrote operas known for their recognisable characters. 1857–1934

Edward Elgar

Composer of the <i>Enigma Variations</i>, a set of variations drawn from his friendships, who also recorded his own music for the gramophone. 1858–1924

Giacomo Puccini

He held a theatre audience through a scene of a woman at a window, then brought back a tune it had heard before. 1860–1911

Gustav Mahler

Conductor who composed during the summers and intended each symphony to contain a whole world, combining the sublime with the music of the street band. 1864–1949

Richard Strauss

Composer, the son of a horn player, who wrote long farewell songs for soprano. 1865–1931

Carl Nielsen

Danish composer, violinist and conductor, born on Funen and based in Copenhagen, who wrote six symphonies, the opera Maskarade, a wind quintet and a large body of songs and hymn tunes. 1865–1957

Jean Sibelius

Composer who took the horn theme of his Fifth Symphony from sixteen swans he watched circling over Ainola, and who worked on the symphony for four years. 1867–1944

Amy Beach

American composer and former prodigy from New Hampshire who taught herself orchestration by attending Boston Symphony concerts; known for a symphony built on Irish tunes, first given in Boston. 1872–1915

Alexander Scriabin

Composer who wrote a part for coloured light that had to be omitted at the work’s first performance. 1873–1943

Sergei Rachmaninoff

Russian composer and pianist who earned his living at the piano in exile and whose music uses the sound of the bells of his childhood.

Further reading

  • Carl E. Schorske, Fin-de-Siècle Vienna: Politics and Culture (Vintage, 1980 edition).study

    A cultural and political history of Vienna that places musical developments beside the city’s other arts and institutions.

  • Carl Dahlhaus, Between Romanticism and Modernism, translated by Mary Whittall (University of California Press, 1989 edition).study

    Studies late-nineteenth-century music at the boundary between inherited Romantic practices and modernism.

  • Jim Samson, ed., The Cambridge History of Nineteenth-Century Music (Cambridge University Press, 2001).survey

    Combines repertory studies with aesthetics, institutions, nationalism, popular culture and the emergence of the avant-garde.
