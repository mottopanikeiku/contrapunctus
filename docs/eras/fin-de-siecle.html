IThe Story

By 1900 a composer could write for an orchestra whose instruments had wider chromatic resources, more reliable mechanisms and greater collective power than those available to Mozart. That did not require every page to be loud. Mahler can separate a single wind instrument from a vast ensemble, while Richard Strauss makes several independent lines collide without abandoning their individual profiles. The enlarged orchestra becomes a collection of possible chamber groups. The technical problem is no longer simply obtaining a particular pitch or volume, but controlling attention when harmony, rhythm, register and instrumental colour can all change independently.

Instrument making had supplied the means over several decades. Valves let brass players redirect the air column through additional tubing; improved woodwind keywork facilitated passages that once required awkward cross-fingerings. Bass clarinet, cor anglais and contrabassoon extended familiar families downwards, each with a distinct register rather than merely a darker version of another instrument. Percussion could mark a physical location or dramatic event: a tam-tam attack does not decay like a timpani roll, and a bell’s complex spectrum does not behave like a sustained orchestral triad. Choosing the instrument therefore changes the harmonic result as well as the colour.

Large forces, small texturesAn orchestral roster records the available players, not how many sound at once. Mahler often reaches maximum exposure by leaving one player almost alone.

Scores increasingly specify the mechanics of delivery: mutes, harmonics, stopped horn notes, flutter-tonguing, bow position and changes of instrument. Strauss’s Don Quixote of 1897 uses flutter-tongued wind and brass textures in its sheep episode, making the manner of producing a note part of the narrative. Mahler’s offstage instruments alter perceived distance rather than merely adding another section. Such effects require practical organisation: extra players, rehearsal, suitable rooms and a conductor who can coordinate events that do not reach every listener at the same instant. Orchestration now includes the geography of performance.

The tonal argumentContinuity without a straight road

Late-Romantic harmony retains triads and tonal centres while loosening the expectation that each phrase must confirm one obvious key. Chromatic inner parts can move by semitone while a bass or common note holds the texture together. Chords a third apart may share a pitch; an enharmonic change can make one sounding interval serve two grammatical functions. The ear follows the smooth local voice-leading even when a passage has travelled far from its starting tonic. This is why counting accidentals cannot measure how unstable the music feels: a dense chord over a persistent bass may sound more settled than a plain triad in an unexpected place.

Mahler extends this freedom to the whole work. His Fifth Symphony begins in C♯ minor and ends in D major; the Ninth begins in D major and closes in D♭ major. These are planned tonal itineraries, not failures to recover an opening key. Conversely, Sibelius can make a restricted group of pitches yield a long process of change through altered basses, spacing and repetition. His Fifth Symphony, first performed in 1915 and revised twice, reaches its final version in 1919, beyond the nominal boundary of this chapter. An era label is less precise than a composition’s actual working history.

Scriabin gradually moves away from conventional dominant–tonic syntax towards harmonies whose identity lies in a characteristic collection of pitches. Rachmaninoff preserves stronger tonal destinations, but postpones them through sequences, inner chromatic lines and extended melodic spans. His piano writing depends on layered registers and carefully sustained basses, not only on large hand stretches. By this period the iron-framed concert grand can carry considerable string tension and project through an orchestra. Its capacity to sustain sound allows a melody to continue above moving inner parts, but makes the timing of pedal changes a structural responsibility.

The competing scalesSong, symphony, stage

The orchestral song combines a short text with instrumental resources formerly associated with large forms. A poem can organise a compact sequence of timbres, while related songs can become a cycle with a substantial tonal plan. Mahler lets song enter the symphony directly; his Das Lied von der Erde of 1908–09 distributes six movements between two singers and orchestra. At the end, an added sixth remains within the final C-major sonority. The effect depends not on a spectacular dissonance but on leaving one pitch that a stricter cadence might have removed.

HeterophonyA melody and a decorated version of that melody can sound together. Unlike a chordal doubling, the lines do not coincide at every instant.

Janáček arrives at another kind of concentration through short motives, repeated accompaniment figures and close attention to speech inflection. His opera Jenůfa, first staged in Brno in 1904, is not built from the same phrase lengths as a Wagnerian music drama. Repetition can hold a character inside a particular state until a small alteration changes its meaning. Sibelius also makes transformation emerge from limited material: a rhythmic cell or instrumental figure can acquire thematic identity gradually. The period’s long symphonies therefore coexist with procedures that compress a dramatic argument into a few notes.

In the opera house, Puccini uses recurring motives, orchestral detail and calculated changes of pace within scenes whose stage action remains sharply legible. Strauss’s Salome, premiered in Dresden in 1905, brings Oscar Wilde’s drama into a one-act structure with an unusually large orchestra. The voice must be intelligible against that instrumental apparatus; scoring and register determine when a singer can dominate without simply singing louder. New theatrical lighting and coordinated production also changed how a musical scene could be presented. At the Vienna Court Opera, Mahler’s collaboration with Alfred Roller treated colour and light as components of the performance rather than interchangeable scenery.

The institutionsA repertoire begins to accumulate

Permanent orchestras and opera companies offered continuity, but they also concentrated power in conductors, administrators and publishers. Mahler and Strauss earned much of their living by conducting; rehearsal time and access to players shaped their compositional schedules. Elgar found an important public through British choral festivals, where amateur singers and professional instrumentalists could undertake works on a large scale. The Dream of Gerontius was first performed at Birmingham in 1900. Such institutions made ambitious scores possible while imposing deadlines, local expectations and the practical limits of available performers.

Publishing served both professionals and domestic readers. Full scores, orchestral parts and vocal scores circulated a work in different forms; a pianist might know an opera before hearing its orchestration. Detailed metronome marks and verbal directions attempted to preserve increasingly specific intentions across that circulation. They did not eliminate disagreement. A conductor still had to negotiate the difference between the written dynamic and what a singer or inner instrumental line could actually project in a particular hall. The modern idea of fidelity to a score grew alongside a performance culture that depended on those acts of adjustment.

Rolls are not recordings of soundA reproducing-piano roll operates an instrument mechanically. Its timing and dynamics depend on the system and its restoration; it is not an acoustic photograph of the pianist.

The gramophone added another kind of circulation. Acoustic recording directed sound into a horn and onto a physical recording surface, with severe restrictions on balance, frequency range and duration. Elgar began recording in 1914; these early documents differ materially from his later electrical recordings. Mahler’s 1905 reproducing-piano rolls preserve information about his playing but no recording of his orchestral sound. Broadcast radio would become a major musical institution chiefly after the First World War. At this earlier stage, a recording could carry a singer’s manner across continents while still being unable to reproduce the full scale of an orchestral climax.

Visual art and literature made the relation between outward appearance and inward experience an explicit subject. Munch’s The Scream bends the surrounding landscape into repeated curves; Klimt’s Beethoven Frieze turns a symphony’s reception into a sequence of figures and decorative fields. Nietzsche’s Also sprach Zarathustra supplied Mahler with the words of the Third Symphony’s fourth movement and Strauss with the point of departure for his 1896 tone poem. Those musical responses differ: one sets a poem, the other constructs an instrumental argument. The same text could generate incompatible forms without becoming an all-purpose explanation of the period’s harmony.

IITerms of Art

The vocabulary you need to hear this period clearly.

Progressive tonality

A large-scale tonal plan in which a work ends in a different tonic from the one with which it began. The destination must be understood across the complete work, not as an isolated modulation.

Orchestral song

A setting for solo voice and orchestra, whether conceived in that medium or orchestrated from a piano version. Instrumental register and timbre can carry parts of the poem’s argument independently of the vocal line.

Tone poem

An orchestral work, usually in one continuous movement, associated with a poem, character, narrative or idea. Its form can combine thematic transformation with procedures inherited from sonata, variation or rondo designs.

Enharmonic reinterpretation

A change of harmonic meaning achieved by respelling a pitch without changing its equal-tempered sound: G♯ may become A♭, for example. The new spelling identifies a different interval and potentially a different tonal destination.

Added sixth

A triad retaining an additional note a sixth above its root, such as A within C–E–G–A. Depending on context, that note can colour a stable ending rather than behave as a dissonance requiring resolution.

Pedal point

A sustained or reiterated pitch against changing harmonies, usually but not always in the bass. It can anchor remote chords to one tonal reference or become dissonant with the parts moving above it.

Heterophony

The simultaneous sounding of a melody and one or more variants of it. Ornament, rhythm or timing distinguishes the versions; the texture is neither strict unison nor wholly independent counterpoint.

The rigid diagonal of the walkway meets curves that pass through sky, water and the central figure. Munch makes an experience alter the organisation of the visible scene. The comparison with late-Romantic music concerns that transfer between individual perception and an enlarged surrounding field: an orchestral texture can change the apparent meaning of a simple melodic figure. Friedrich Nietzsche

The midnight song from Also sprach Zarathustra

1884; repeated in Part IV, 1885
GermanO Mensch! Gib acht! Was spricht die tiefe Mitternacht? “Ich schlief, ich schlief —, Aus tiefem Traum bin ich erwacht: — Die Welt ist tief, Und tiefer als der Tag gedacht. Tief ist ihr Weh —, Lust — tiefer noch als Herzeleid: Weh spricht: Vergeh! Doch alle Lust will Ewigkeit —, — will tiefe, tiefe Ewigkeit!”
Our translationO human being, pay heed! What does the deep midnight say? “I slept, I slept — From a deep dream I have awakened: The world is deep, Deeper than the day has thought. Deep is its grief — Delight is deeper still than heartache: Grief says: Pass away! But all delight desires eternity — Desires deep, deep eternity!”

Mahler sets this text for alto in the fourth movement of the Third Symphony; Strauss responds to the larger book in Also sprach Zarathustra. The words first appear at the end of “Das andere Tanzlied” in Part III. The intervening numbered strokes of midnight are omitted here. Nietzsche’s opposition between grief, delight and recurrence gives the musical return a philosophical subject without prescribing a particular chord progression.

VComposers

1854–1928

Leoš Janáček

A Moravian composer who studied the inflections of speech and built operas from short cells, repeated figures and abrupt changes of perspective. 1857–1934

Edward Elgar

A Worcester violinist who made orchestral variations into portraits of friends and the concerto into an argument with memory. 1858–1924

Giacomo Puccini

An opera composer who made a remembered tune, a stage noise or a change of orchestral register determine the timing of a scene. 1860–1911

Gustav Mahler ✦

A conductor who composed in the summers, and wanted each symphony to hold a whole world — the sublime and the street band together. 1864–1949

Richard Strauss

An orchestral storyteller who took opera to the harmonic edge in Salome and Elektra, then made conversation and recollection its central events. 1865–1957

Jean Sibelius

A symphonist who let small figures gather into themes and movements change shape while their underlying pulse continued. 1872–1915

Alexander Scriabin

A pianist who replaced the need for a resolving tonic with recurring harmonic fields, accelerating figures and carefully organised light. 1873–1943

Sergei Rachmaninoff ✦

A composer-pianist who built long melodies over tolling basses, and carried Russian chant into the concertos and dances of his American exile.
