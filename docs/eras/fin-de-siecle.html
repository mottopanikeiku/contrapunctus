IV1880–1918

Fin de siècle

The orchestra after dark

Gustav KlimtBeethoven Frieze: The Well-Armed Strong One, 1901–02
Secession Building, Vienna (Belvedere)

The Story

On 21 April 1915, Sibelius saw sixteen swans and wrote a theme in his diary. “One of the great experiences of my life! My God, what beauty!” The theme found its way into the finale of his Fifth Symphony. There, after rushing strings, the horns begin a broad, rocking call. A separate woodwind melody moves above it. The orchestra can give an encounter outdoors a scale that the diary’s stave only hints at.

By this time a score could call on several sizes of the same instrument. A clarinettist might change to a bass clarinet; a fourth bassoon player could take up the contrabassoon. Mahler’s Third Symphony needs eight horns, two harpists and two timpanists, as well as women’s and children’s choruses. Its first movement opens with a horn call that seems capable of addressing a mountain. Yet the same score has room for an oboe minuet played with exquisite delicacy.

Large forces, small texturesMahler’s Third places a brass solo offstage in its third movement. The distant sound reaches the audience across a quiet accompaniment.

Strauss’s Don Quixote, composed in 1897, turns a solo cello into the knight and gives Sancho Panza parts for bass clarinet, tenor tuba and viola. In the second variation, the knight attacks a flock of sheep. The orchestra bleats back. Later a wind machine supplies the rush of an imaginary flight on a wooden horse. The instruments are good at telling the truth about adventures that their hero has misunderstood.

The tonal argumentContinuity without a straight road

A chord can change its surroundings while keeping one note in place. In the example below, C major becomes A♭ major with C still sounding. A♭ can then be renamed G♯ and kept inside an E-major chord. The bass has crossed between three major chords without a dominant–tonic cadence. A melody can pass smoothly through the change while the bass makes the larger move.

Strauss leaves a particularly strange disagreement at the end of Also sprach Zarathustra. High woodwinds play B major ever more softly, while the basses return to C, the note with which the work began. The two keys remain a semitone apart. The opening’s blazing certainty has dwindled into sounds that share a silence without settling their difference.

Rachmaninoff’s Second Piano Concerto begins with the piano alone, striking chords like bells. When the orchestra’s C-minor melody enters, the soloist spreads an accompaniment beneath it. The instrument can support a long orchestral phrase and still be clearly present: the hands move through the chords while the sustaining pedal lets the low notes ring. Later the piano takes its own turn to sing.

Scriabin wanted a keyboard to do something still more extravagant. His Prometheus: The Poem of Fire, composed in 1909–10, includes a part for a keyboard controlling coloured light. A solo piano plays among a huge orchestra; near the end, a chorus can join on wordless vowels. The music arrives at a brilliant F♯-major chord. At the Moscow premiere in 1911, Scriabin played the piano, but the projected light remained on the page.

The human voiceSong, symphony, stage

In Mahler’s Das Lied von der Erde, written in 1908–09, two singers take turns through six movements with orchestra. The final farewell grows out of short phrases, with stretches where the voice seems to be speaking into an almost empty room. At the end, C major contains an extra note: A, the added sixth, remains in the flute and clarinet. The singer repeats “ewig”, forever, as the sound thins away.

HeterophonyA flute can decorate a tune while an oboe plays its simpler outline. Both follow the same melody, meeting on some notes and parting on others.

Janáček collected another sort of vocal music on the streets and trains. From 1897 he wrote down fragments of speech in musical notation, often recording who had spoken, where, and in what circumstances. His opera Jenůfa, first performed in Brno in 1904, sets a prose play. Its brief vocal phrases can catch the change from an ordinary remark to an accusation. Repeated figures in the orchestra keep going while someone struggles to find the next words.

The people in Puccini’s Tosca have very little time. Cavaradossi is to be executed; Tosca believes the firing squad will only pretend to shoot him. Awaiting execution, he remembers their nights together in E lucevan le stelle. The aria holds him inside that memory for a few minutes. Then the stage action resumes, and the shots are real.

Nietzsche’s midnight song gave Mahler a different kind of utterance. In the fourth movement of the Third Symphony, an alto asks what deep midnight says, over the orchestra’s slow rocking notes. In the next movement, children imitate bells with “bimm, bamm”. The two movements put an adult’s troubled meditation beside a sound a child can make.

The institutionsMusic leaves the concert hall

Getting a new opera into a theatre could take years. Prague refused Jenůfa before its Brno premiere and continued to resist it afterwards. The Prague production finally came in 1916, with changes made by the conductor Karel Kovařovic. Max Brod then translated the libretto into German and brought the opera to the attention of Universal Edition.

Vienna’s Secession exhibition of 1902 placed Beethoven at the centre of a room. Max Klinger supplied the sculpture; Klimt painted his Beethoven Frieze for a neighbouring wall. Twenty-one artists worked on the exhibition under Josef Hoffmann. Klimt’s figures pass through suffering towards an embrace and a choir of angels, following a reading of Beethoven’s Ninth. A symphony had become something visitors could walk around.

A side of a recordAn acoustic recording collected sound through a horn. Its short playing time could force a composer to cut a piece before recording it.

On 26 June 1914 Elgar conducted his first recording of Pomp and Circumstance No. 1. About thirty musicians, recruited from London orchestras, made up the ensemble. The march was cut to just over four minutes and began with its big trio tune. A customer could carry Elgar’s own performance home, though the familiar music had been rearranged to fit the disc.

Sibelius kept returning to his Fifth Symphony after its first performance on his fiftieth birthday in 1915. He revised it twice, reaching the final version in 1919. The swan theme survived. At the end, the orchestra delivers six separate chords, with silence opening between them. Even after the last chord, the preceding gaps can make the air seem to wait for another.

Terms of Art

Progressive tonality

A plan in which a work begins in one key and ends in another. The final key becomes the destination of the work as a whole.

Orchestral song

A song for a solo voice accompanied by an orchestra. It may have been written for these forces or arranged from an earlier version for voice and piano.

Tone poem

An orchestral piece, usually in one continuous movement, written around a story, character, landscape or other stated subject. “Symphonic poem” means the same thing.

Enharmonic reinterpretation

Giving a note or chord a new role in the harmony by changing its spelling. G♯ and A♭ share a key on an equal-tempered piano, but their names describe different relationships to the notes around them.

Added sixth

A note a sixth above a chord’s root, included alongside the ordinary three notes of the chord. Adding A to C–E–G makes a C-major chord with an added sixth.

Pedal point

A note held or repeated while the chords around it change. It usually lies in the bass and may clash with some of the passing harmonies.

Heterophony

Two or more versions of the same melody sounding together. One part may add ornaments or change the rhythm while another keeps the simpler tune.

In the Score

Sister Arts

The figure presses its hands to its head while two companions walk away. Behind it, the sky and fjord bend into red and blue curves. In a notebook entry of 1892, Munch described a walk with two friends when the sky turned blood-red and he felt a scream passing through nature. Here the landscape seems to carry the sound that the figure cannot bear.

Friedrich Nietzsche

The midnight song from Also sprach Zarathustra

1884; repeated in Part IV, 1885
GermanO Mensch! Gib acht! Was spricht die tiefe Mitternacht? “Ich schlief, ich schlief —, Aus tiefem Traum bin ich erwacht: — Die Welt ist tief, Und tiefer als der Tag gedacht. Tief ist ihr Weh —, Lust — tiefer noch als Herzeleid: Weh spricht: Vergeh! Doch alle Lust will Ewigkeit —, — will tiefe, tiefe Ewigkeit!”
Our translationO human being, pay heed! What does the deep midnight say? “I slept, I slept — From a deep dream I have awakened: The world is deep, Deeper than the day has thought. Deep is its grief — Delight is deeper still than heartache: Grief says: Pass away! But all delight desires eternity — Desires deep, deep eternity!”

Mahler sets these words for alto in the fourth movement of his Third Symphony. Strauss took chapter titles from the same book for Also sprach Zarathustra, composed in 1896; near its end, a bell strikes midnight. In Nietzsche’s text, the clock’s numbered strokes fall between the lines of the poem. They are omitted here.

Composers

1854–1928

Leoš Janáček

He wrote down the voices of strangers in Brno, then gave opera some of its most painfully recognisable people. 1857–1934

Edward Elgar

He made the <i>Enigma Variations</i> from his friendships and preserved his own way with the music on gramophone records. 1858–1924

Giacomo Puccini

He could make a whole theatre wait with a woman at a window, then break its heart with a tune it had heard before. 1860–1911

Gustav Mahler

A conductor who composed in the summers, and wanted each symphony to hold a whole world — the sublime and the street band together. 1864–1949

Richard Strauss

The son of a horn player, he gave the orchestra a taste for scandal and the soprano some of her longest, loveliest farewells. 1865–1957

Jean Sibelius

He watched sixteen swans circle over Ainola, gave their flight to the horns, and spent four years getting the Fifth Symphony right. 1872–1915

Alexander Scriabin

He wrote a part for coloured light that had to be omitted at the work’s first performance. 1873–1943

Sergei Rachmaninoff

In exile he earned his living at the piano, while the bells of his Russian childhood kept sounding through the music he wrote.

Further reading

  • Carl E. Schorske, Fin-de-Siècle Vienna: Politics and Culture (Vintage, 1980 edition).study

    A cultural and political history of Vienna that places musical developments beside the city’s other arts and institutions.

  • Carl Dahlhaus, Between Romanticism and Modernism, translated by Mary Whittall (University of California Press, 1989 edition).study

    Studies late-nineteenth-century music at the boundary between inherited Romantic practices and modernism.

  • Jim Samson, ed., The Cambridge History of Nineteenth-Century Music (Cambridge University Press, 2001).survey

    Combines repertory studies with aesthetics, institutions, nationalism, popular culture and the emergence of the avant-garde.
