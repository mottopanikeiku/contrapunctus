VIChapter 7 · 1908–1945

Modernism

Breaking the key, the beat and the frame

Wassily KandinskyImpression III (Concert), 1911
Städtische Galerie im Lenbachhaus, Munich

Key ideas

  • Atonality. Music without a consistently governing tonic or conventional hierarchy of tonal functions.
  • Twelve-note row. An ordered set containing all twelve pitch classes.
  • Sprechstimme. A notated delivery between speech and sustained singing, particularly associated with Schoenberg.

7.1The Story

In the finale of his Second String Quartet, completed in 1908, Schoenberg sets Stefan George’s words about air from another planet while relinquishing a stable tonal centre. The ensemble includes a soprano, so the departure is both harmonic and institutional: a string quartet has admitted a human voice. Five years later, Stravinsky’s The Rite of Spring brings blocks of repeated material, irregular accents and sharply differentiated orchestral groups into the ballet theatre. Modernism develops through competing decisions about which inherited relationships to preserve and which to reorganise.

Atonality opens several paths to audible continuity: a short interval pattern can recur in several registers; a chord can return as a recognisable object; rhythmic and instrumental relationships can hold a work together. Schoenberg’s works around 1909 often compress their argument as they move beyond the familiar machinery of tonal prolongation. His monodrama Erwartung, composed that year, pursues a different solution, sustaining an extended theatrical sequence through changing gestures and orchestral detail. Atonal music can be concentrated or expansive.

Twelve notes in orderA twelve-note row is an ordering of all twelve pitch classes. Its intervals, subdivisions and transformations give it an identity.

In the early 1920s Schoenberg formulated his method of composing with twelve tones. A row can be transposed, inverted, reversed or subjected to combinations of those operations. It can supply melodies and chords, its notes distributed between parts and given differing prominence. Webern often builds especially close relationships between a row’s subdivisions, while Berg allows tonal implications and dramatic associations to remain audible within serial designs. The twelve-tone method regulates pitch relationships, leaving rhythm, dynamics, instrumentation and the form of a complete work to further compositional decisions.

The beatRepetition becomes unstable

Stravinsky frequently makes a repeated figure less predictable by changing its accents, duration or relationship to neighbouring layers. A stable pulse can continue while groups of three and two redistribute the apparent beat. A passage may alternate blocks without providing the connecting transition a nineteenth-century listener expects. The tension lies between recurrence and discontinuity: the material is familiar almost immediately, but the timing of its next appearance remains uncertain. Rhythmic analysis must distinguish four separate elements: pulse, written metre, accent and phrase length.

Bartók and Zoltán Kodály’s collection of rural songs supplied documented alternatives to conservatoire assumptions about scale, phrase and metre. Wax-cylinder recordings preserved details that a collector might miss while taking immediate dictation; repeated listening could test a transcription. Bartók distinguished the different repertories within Hungarian music. Asymmetrical dance rhythms, modal scales and narrow melodic patterns became resources for new compositions, sometimes without a literal quotation. His procedure was analytical as well as receptive: the interval structure of a melody could generate chords, counterpoint and larger formal relationships.

Ives arrived independently at combinations of conflicting musical layers through American hymnody, marches and experimental practice. A familiar tune can remain intelligible while another part proceeds at a different pace or in another key. Such superposition differs from replacing tonality with a row: the clash may depend on hearing each component’s conventional identity. Ives’s delayed publication and uneven performance history reveal the gaps between composition, revision, circulation and first substantial public reception. These can be separated by decades, giving different listeners different versions of what counts as new.

Old forms, altered materialsThe uses of distance

Neoclassicism reused earlier forms and gestures. Stravinsky’s Pulcinella of 1920 reworks music then attributed largely to Pergolesi, changing harmony, rhythm and instrumental presentation. Prokofiev’s Classical Symphony, composed in 1916–17, invokes eighteenth-century proportions while placing unexpected harmonic turns inside them. A clear cadence or dance form can become a frame against which a wrong-footing accent is especially audible. Modernism therefore includes deliberately plain surfaces as well as dense dissonance. The historical reference works because listeners recognise a convention before hearing what the composer has done to it.

Berg’s Wozzeck, premiered in Berlin in 1925, gives operatic scenes forms including suite, passacaglia and invention, using their constraints to organise drama. Webern’s brevity and widely spaced instrumental entries make the connection between individual sounds unusually exposed. Schoenberg’s Pierrot lunaire of 1912 places a reciter using Sprechstimme beside five instrumentalists who double on additional instruments. A small ensemble can produce structural complexity through changing membership, articulation and the separation of one event from the next.

Speech-songSchoenberg’s instructions distinguish Sprechstimme from ordinary singing. The notation coordinates pitch contour and rhythm, but the performer must leave the pitch rather than sustain it as a sung tone.

Composers reassessed instrumental mechanics. Bartók’s Sonata for Two Pianos and Percussion of 1937 combines the piano’s tuned attack with instruments whose pitches and decays behave differently. Strings can play near the bridge, use harmonics or strike with the wood of the bow; winds can distinguish flutter-tongued, muted or breath-shaped attacks. The resulting noise components can become compositional material. A composer can organise their contrast with sustained tones, just as earlier composers organised contrasts between consonance and dissonance. The orchestra becomes an apparatus for producing several kinds of sound.

Distribution and authorityThe microphone and the state

New-music societies and festivals provided performances that an established subscription programme might avoid. Schoenberg’s Society for Private Musical Performances, founded in Vienna in 1918, concentrated on rehearsed presentations and protected listening conditions. Publishers had to print increasingly exact articulation, unusual metres and new performance instructions. A score’s density could increase even as the ensemble became smaller, because fewer conventions could safely be left unstated. The practical burden moved into rehearsals: counting an irregular succession together might require more preparation than playing a large but rhythmically familiar orchestral passage.

Radio organisations became employers, commissioners and owners of orchestras during the interwar period. Electrical recording, commercially established in the mid-1920s, used microphones and amplification to improve what could be captured, although shellac disc sides still limited duration. Film created another collaboration between measured time and musical form. Prokofiev’s score for Eisenstein’s Alexander Nevsky of 1938 had to function with edited images and recorded sound; the later concert cantata reorganised that material for a different venue. A work might now circulate as broadcast, film soundtrack, disc, printed score and live performance, each with its own balance and constraints.

Different kinds of coercionNazi racial persecution and Soviet cultural directives both altered careers and repertories, through different institutions, doctrines and forms of violence.

Political power changed who could compose, perform and remain in a country. Schoenberg left Germany in 1933; Bartók moved to the United States in 1940. Shostakovich faced the 1936 Pravda attack on Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District, followed by a drastically changed public situation for his music. Prokofiev’s return to the Soviet Union placed his commissions and stage works within another system of approval.

Modernist painting and poetry similarly separated representation from inherited grammar. Marc’s Fate of the Animals of 1913 breaks the forest into intersecting planes; Apollinaire’s Zone moves between the Eiffel Tower, religion, advertisements and memory without restoring a classical hierarchy of subjects. Schoenberg’s exchanges with Kandinsky document a direct relationship between compositional and painterly experiments. They asked how relationships between colours, intervals, words or durations could make an intelligible work when perspective, tonal hierarchy or regular verse no longer supplied the entire frame.

7.2Terms of Art

The vocabulary you need to hear this period clearly.

Atonality

Music without a consistently governing tonic or conventional hierarchy of tonal functions. Recurring intervals, motives, pitch collections, register and rhythm can establish strong relationships.

Twelve-note row

An ordered set containing all twelve pitch classes. Transposition, inversion, retrograde and retrograde inversion create related forms; the row may generate simultaneous harmonies as well as successive notes.

Sprechstimme

A notated delivery between speech and sustained singing, particularly associated with Schoenberg. Rhythm is specified, while pitches establish contours or points of departure for the voice.

Polyrhythm

The simultaneous combination of distinct rhythmic divisions, such as three evenly spaced notes against two within the same duration.

Additive metre

A metrical organisation understood as a sum of unequal beat groups, such as 2+2+3 quavers. Written time signatures may show the total or its subdivisions; the accent pattern establishes the grouping in sound.

Neoclassicism

A modern reuse of earlier forms, textures or genres, often with altered harmony and rhythm. It describes a compositional relationship with the past.

Polymodality

The simultaneous combination of different modes, sometimes sharing a common tonic. In Bartók, overlapping modal structures can generate the resulting chromatic notes.

Ostinato

A persistently repeated pattern of pitches, rhythm or both. Its repetition can stabilise one layer while accents, harmonies, metre or other patterns shift against it.

The opening gives a steel tower the role of a shepherdess and brings bridges, antiquity and motorcars into successive lines without punctuation. That assembly of incompatible registers offers a concrete literary counterpart to musical collage and abrupt changes of block. The 1913 collection places “Zone” first, making its argument with the past the reader’s point of entry.

7.5Composers

1874–1954

Charles Ives

A church organist and insurance executive who made hymns, marching bands and incompatible tempos coexist inside concert music. 1874–1951

Arnold Schoenberg

A Viennese teacher who took motivic development beyond the key signature, then made twelve notes carry the work of a tonal system. 1881–1945

Béla Bartók

A pianist and fieldworker who turned the modes, rhythms and performance habits of peasant music into new kinds of quartet and orchestral argument. 1882–1971

Igor Stravinsky

A composer of ballets who made the placement of an accent as consequential as a change of harmony. 1883–1945

Anton Webern

A conductor and scholar of Renaissance polyphony who compressed canons and variations into a few exposed notes. 1885–1935

Alban Berg

A composer of operas and coded chamber music who made twelve-tone rows accommodate triads, folk song and a Bach chorale. 1891–1953

Sergei Prokofiev ✦

A pianist who made the keyboard strike like percussion, and a theatre composer whose longest melodies keep their footing through abrupt changes of key. 1906–1975

Dmitri Shostakovich

Fifteen symphonies and fifteen quartets turn marches, fugues and a four-note signature into arguments about public ceremony and individual memory.

Questions to take away

  1. Can the distance between two notes identify a pair as clearly as its tone colour?

    Five instrumental pairs keep five different distances between their notes. A brass chorale interrupts the procession before the pairs return with new companions.

    Concerto for Orchestra, Sz. 116 · II. Giuoco delle coppie — Allegretto scherzando

  2. How can stillness and sudden outcry belong to the same musical argument?

    The central Elegia recalls the first movement’s slow introduction, surrounding its lament with separate layers of rustling accompaniment and exposed calls. Its strongest outbursts grow from a nearly motionless background.

    Concerto for Orchestra, Sz. 116 · III. Elegia — Andante non troppo
