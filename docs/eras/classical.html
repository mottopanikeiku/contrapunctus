Classical

The surprise at the end of a phrase

Jacques-Louis DavidThe Oath of the Horatii, 1784
Musée du Louvre, Paris

The Story

At London’s Hanover Square Rooms on 23 March 1792, Haydn offered a new symphony with an unusually innocent slow movement. The violins picked out a little tune, marked semplice. It came round again, quieter. Then the whole orchestra struck a fortissimo chord, with timpani. The joke in the Surprise Symphony needs that small, regular tune: Haydn gives it just long enough to become comfortable before he upsets the room.

A tune with a clear accompaniment leaves its phrase endings exposed. Four bars can come to rest on G, the dominant of C major, with enough of a pause for a breath. Four more can return to C and close the sentence. These are the two endings in the invented period below. An extra bar would delay the answer; a sudden silence would make the missing beat conspicuous.

Eight bars are a modelA period often has a four-bar opening phrase and a four-bar answer. The first ends with a weaker cadence than the second. Composers regularly lengthen or shorten either phrase.

The keyboard could speak less evenly. C. P. E. Bach’s music breaks off, changes register and finds a new direction mid-phrase. His Essay on the True Manner of Playing the Clavier, published in two parts in 1753 and 1762, puts performance alongside fingering and ornaments. The practical questions belong together: which finger takes the note, how an ornament begins, where a phrase needs to breathe.

The argumentLeaving a key, recovering it

Sonata form gives a change of key consequences that can last through a movement. In a C-major exposition, G may begin as an ordinary chord within C. An F♯ in a D-major chord then pulls towards G, and a firm cadence establishes G as a key in its own right. The exposition can end there and repeat. The second key has had time to become a place with tunes of its own.

During the development, a composer can take a few notes from a tune and set them moving through unfamiliar keys. A fragment passes from violin to bassoon; a repeated figure climbs a step each time. The opening theme eventually returns in the tonic. This recapitulation has work to do: material first heard in the second key must now find a way to close in the home key. A familiar passage comes back with its turning altered.

Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony makes an entire first movement out of the consequences of four notes. The opening rhythm keeps interrupting the singing phrases, shifts between instruments and drives passages where hardly any melody remains. The orchestra can hammer at a short figure until its next note matters desperately. In a string quartet, two violins, viola and cello can pursue the same kind of argument at speaking distance, with each player responsible for one exposed line.

A concerto adds a person who can interrupt the whole company. In Mozart’s piano concertos, the orchestra may establish a theme before the soloist has played a note. The piano can answer with a decorated version, or offer something new. Near a movement’s end, a pause on a chord can leave the soloist alone for a cadenza. A trill prepares the orchestra’s return; the private excursion has to find its way back to the shared beat.

The instrumentsA hammer changes the phrase

Mozart visited the Augsburg piano maker Johann Andreas Stein in October 1777. In a letter to his father on the 17th, he praised the care Stein took with his instruments: he “scrapes and works until the piano can do everything”. Mozart was particularly taken with the escapement. The hammer fell away from the string as soon as it had struck, even while the finger held the key down. A clear note could ring without the hammer choking it.

The force of the touch changed the sound. On a fortepiano, a melody could be louder than the broken chords beneath it, and a single note could carry an accent. The relatively quick fading of the strings left space after each attack. Mozart also admired Stein’s knee lever, which lifted the dampers with scarcely a touch. Releasing it stopped the lingering sound. A phrase could join smoothly across the bar, then end cleanly.

The natural hornA crook is a detachable length of tubing that changes a horn’s basic key. A player can also alter pitches by moving a hand inside the bell; stopped notes have a different colour from open ones.

Wind instruments gave the orchestra voices that a keyboard could only suggest. An oboe could hold a note after the strings’ initial attack; horns could fill out a cadence without taking the melody. Beethoven saved a much larger change for the finale of his Fifth. After the quiet timpani-led passage out of the scherzo, C major bursts in with piccolo, contrabassoon and three trombones added to the orchestra. The new key has new lungs.

The listenersPatrons and ticket buyers

Haydn’s London symphonies were written for paying audiences after decades in the service of the Esterházy family. The death of Prince Nikolaus in 1790 gave him the freedom to travel. At his first London season in 1791, a composer accustomed to a court’s musicians entered a city where a concert had to attract customers. The Surprise belonged to his second season. Its sudden chord was an excellent piece of public address.

A concert could also be an ordeal. On 22 December 1808 Beethoven presented the first performances of his Fifth and Sixth symphonies in a bitterly cold Vienna theatre. The programme included the Fourth Piano Concerto and ended with the Choral Fantasy. These works now fill separate evenings. On that night, performers and audience had to get through them together.

An audience inside a paintingWright’s Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump dates from 1768. One girl covers her eyes, another watches the bird, and a young couple look only at each other.

Mozart’s Le nozze di Figaro gives its people equally different things to attend to. In the last act, the Count woos his own wife, disguised as her maid. Figaro sees a betrayal; Susanna, wearing the Countess’s clothes, has to deal with his jealousy. The orchestra holds these mistaken conversations together. When the real Countess reveals herself, her husband has to ask forgiveness in front of everyone.

On 7 May 1824, in Vienna, Beethoven brought voices into the finale of his Ninth Symphony. A bass soloist interrupts with words Beethoven wrote himself: “O friends, not these sounds!” Schiller’s poem follows, and the chorus takes up its invitation to become brothers. A tune first played by the orchestra has acquired words that a crowd can sing.

Terms of Art

Period

A pair of related musical phrases in which the second ends more firmly than the first. An eight-bar period often divides into two four-bar phrases.

Sentence

A phrase that presents a short idea, repeats it, then presses towards a cadence. The closing part often uses shorter fragments of the opening idea or quicker notes.

Sonata form

A design for a movement with three main sections. The exposition establishes a home key and a second key; the development works on the musical ideas; the recapitulation brings them back, with the later material now resolving in the home key.

Development

The reshaping of an existing musical idea, for example by breaking it into fragments, changing its key or combining it with another tune. The term also names the middle section of a sonata-form movement, where this often happens.

String quartet

An ensemble of two violins, viola and cello, with one player to each part. The term also means a piece written for this ensemble.

Cadenza

A solo passage, often near the end of a concerto movement, during which the orchestra is silent. It may be improvised or written out, and often ends with a trill before the orchestra returns.

Fortepiano

A name used for the pianos of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and modern copies of them. Their hammers strike strings in a lightly built wooden frame, producing a sound that generally fades more quickly than a modern concert grand’s.

In the Score

Sister Arts

The cockatoo struggles inside the glass vessel. One girl covers her eyes; her father tries to reassure her. Across the table, a young couple look at each other and miss the experiment altogether. Wright gives a single event several private stories, much as Mozart does when his operatic characters sing together with quite different things on their minds.

Friedrich Schiller

An die Freude — first stanza

1785; revised c. 1803
GermanFreude, schöner Götterfunken, Tochter aus Elysium, Wir betreten feuertrunken, Himmlische, dein Heiligtum. Deine Zauber binden wieder, Was die Mode streng geteilt, Alle Menschen werden Brüder, Wo dein sanfter Flügel weilt.
Our translationJoy, lovely spark of the gods, Daughter from Elysium, Drunk with fire we enter, Heavenly one, your sanctuary. Your spells bind together again What custom has strictly divided; All people become brothers Where your gentle wing rests.

“All people become brothers”: Beethoven gives Schiller’s invitation to soloists and chorus in the Ninth Symphony. Before the poem begins, the bass sings Beethoven’s own words, “O Freunde, nicht diese Töne!” — “O friends, not these sounds!” The orchestra has already played the melody that the voices will take up.

Composers

1714–1788

Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach

Frederick the Great’s accompanist wrote keyboard music that breaks off mid-sentence and makes a single note tremble. 1732–1809

Joseph Haydn

He could empty an orchestra one player at a time, or keep a quartet going after everyone thought the piece had ended. 1756–1791

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

In his operas a servant can outwit a count; in his concertos the piano can interrupt before the orchestra has finished its opening sentence. 1770–1827

Ludwig van Beethoven

He could make a symphony out of four notes, and spent his last years finding things four string players had never said.

Further reading

  • Charles Rosen, The Classical Style: Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, expanded edition (W. W. Norton, 1998 paperback).study

    Explains musical conventions through Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven; the expanded edition includes a chapter on late Beethoven.

  • William E. Caplin, Classical Form: A Theory of Formal Functions for the Instrumental Music of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven (Oxford University Press, 1998).study

    Analyses the functions of phrases, themes and larger formal units, developing ideas from Schoenberg and Erwin Ratz.

  • Simon P. Keefe, ed., The Cambridge History of Eighteenth-Century Music (Cambridge University Press, 2009).survey

    Organised around music for church, theatre and concert room, rather than a succession of composer biographies.
