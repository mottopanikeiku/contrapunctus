I1600–1750

The Baroque

A bass line beneath the drama

Johannes VermeerThe Music Lesson (Lady at the Virginals with a Gentleman), c. 1662–65
Royal Collection, London

The Story

In 1637 Venice opened its first public opera house. A performance that had belonged to the court could now earn its living by selling admission. At the centre stood a singer, with a text to deliver and a bass line beneath the voice. The accompanying player filled in the chords. Much of the music we call Baroque begins with that economical arrangement: enough on the page to bring the performers together, room left for them to decide how it would sound.

Numbers above the bassA 6 normally asks for a sixth above the written bass; 6/4 asks for a sixth and fourth. The figures count intervals from the bass note.

The basso continuo could be a harpsichord and cello, an organ with bassoon, or a plucked instrument supporting a voice. The bass player supplied the line; the chord player chose how to fill the space above it. A harpsichordist could spread a chord, keep the right hand close to the singer, or let it fall silent between phrases. Two performances from the same figured bass could contain quite different notes.

Even two moving parts can make a dissonance ache. In the example below, an upper F begins comfortably over another F. The bass moves to C, leaving the upper note stranded; only when F falls to E does the strain ease. This is a suspension. A chain of them can keep the music leaning forward, one voice arriving while another has still to catch up. Repeated bass patterns and cadences also establish the pull of major and minor keys.

The employersChapel, theatre, household

In Leipzig, where Bach became Thomaskantor in 1723, the church calendar kept the composer at his desk. Sundays and feast days required cantatas; choirboys helped copy the parts. In his second annual cycle, Bach built the cantatas around familiar chorales. A hymn could emerge in long notes above busy instrumental writing, pass through the changed words of an aria, then return in a plain harmonisation at the end.

Getting the players was another matter. In August 1730 Bach sent the Leipzig council a memorandum listing what church music needed and what he actually had. Several indispensable posts were vacant. Students filled the gaps, and schoolboys taken from the choir had to play instruments. The document counts violins and oboes with the exasperated care of a man who knows exactly which part will be missing on Sunday.

Vivaldi had a different body of musicians at the Ospedale della Pietà in Venice, which employed him as a violin teacher in 1703. Its girls and women sang and played for visitors who came to hear them. His concertos make the division between soloist and ensemble wonderfully plain: the orchestra announces a returning passage, the ritornello, and the solo violin escapes into quicker notes. When the orchestral passage returns in the home key, the solitary player is gathered back into the company.

The opera singer had a comparable opportunity in the da capo aria. After a contrasting middle section, the score sent the singer back to the beginning, where ornaments could make the familiar melody newly elaborate. Handel wrote these arias for London’s Italian opera stage, then brought English biblical drama to audiences in his oratorios. A chorus could take up the story, giving a crowd its own voice.

The machinesPluck, bow, breath

“I much prefer what moves me to what surprises me,” Couperin wrote in the preface to his first book of harpsichord pieces in 1713. The instrument made that preference a practical challenge. Its strings are plucked; a harder blow on the key cannot produce a pianist’s swelling accent. A tiny delay, a carefully released note or a chord spread between the hands has to do the expressive work. Couperin filled his pages with signs showing exactly where an ornament belonged.

Unequal temperamentsBach’s title “well-tempered” does not name a single tuning recipe. Several systems make every key usable while leaving the semitones unequal.

A keyboard also has to live with its tuning. A singer can adjust a note during a phrase; a harpsichord string remains where the tuner left it. Temperament distributes small compromises among the intervals so that the required keys will work. Bach’s The Well-Tempered Clavier tries all twenty-four major and minor keys, pairing a prelude with a fugue in each. The first book dates from 1722; about twenty years later he assembled a second.

Trumpeters faced a different limit. Without valves, they drew their notes from the harmonic series of one length of tubing. High in that series, the notes lie close enough together to make melodies; lower down, there are large gaps. A Baroque trumpet part lives with those gaps. The ringing high notes in Bach’s festive church music demanded a player who could find them reliably.

The written and unwrittenHow a style circulated

Printed music could travel much farther than its author. Couperin’s books carried his ornaments into other people’s houses; Rameau’s Traité de l’harmonie, published in Paris in 1722, set out principles of composition and accompaniment. Bach’s church musicians still worked from manuscript parts. At Café Zimmermann in Leipzig, where he directed the Collegium Musicum from 1729, a public could hear his concertos and orchestral suites alongside other composers’ music.

A suite keeps the feet in the music. Its dances often share a key, but an allemande’s flowing steps and a gigue’s quick compound beat put different demands on the bow or the fingers. The sarabande makes its slow triple time felt through a weight on the second beat. Even on a harpsichord, with no dancers present, the bar can lift and settle.

Affect and rhetoricBaroque writers used “affect” for a state of feeling, such as grief or anger. Its musical expression depended on the words and occasion.

A fugue begins by making a tune recognisable. One voice states the subject; another answers while the first continues. As more voices join, the same melody can be heard from above or below, or begin before the preceding entry has finished. The difficulty is delicious: every line must remain singable while its notes fit the other lines. Bach could turn a brief subject into pages of these encounters.

Dryden’s A Song for St Cecilia’s Day gives the flute a verse of melancholy complaints. Handel set the poem in 1739, letting the named instrument play alongside the voice. Elsewhere the trumpet rouses soldiers to battle.

Terms of Art

Basso continuo

A bass part accompanied by chords supplied by a keyboard or plucked instrument, often with a cello or another bass instrument playing the written line. Figures above or below the bass tell the chord player which intervals to include.

Ritornello

A passage that returns during a piece. In a Baroque concerto, it is usually played by the orchestra between solo episodes; later returns may be shorter or in another key.

Affect

A state of feeling, such as grief, joy or anger, expressed in music. Baroque composers often sustained one principal affect through a movement.

Fugue

A piece or passage in which several independent voices enter in turn with the same main melody, called the subject. The voices continue together, and the subject returns at different pitches and in different combinations.

Suite

A set of instrumental movements played in sequence. A Baroque suite usually brings together dances in the same key, each with its own metre and characteristic rhythm.

Da capo aria

A solo vocal piece in three sections: an opening, a contrasting middle, and a return to the opening. “Da capo” means “from the head”; the singer commonly adds ornaments on the return.

Temperament

A system for adjusting intervals when tuning a keyboard or another fixed-pitch instrument. Equal temperament divides the octave into twelve equal semitones; unequal systems give some intervals purer tuning at the expense of others.

In the Score

Sister Arts

Four dancers hold hands while Father Time plays the lyre; beside him, a child holds an hourglass. Music measures the turning circle. Poussin painted it in Rome for Giulio Rospigliosi, the future Pope Clement IX. At the other side of the dance, another child blows bubbles that will soon burst.

John Dryden

A Song for St Cecilia’s Day — opening

1687
EnglishFrom harmony, from heavenly harmony, This universal frame began. When Nature underneath a heap Of jarring atoms lay, And could not heave her head, The tuneful voice was heard from high, “Arise, ye more than dead!” Then cold, and hot, and moist, and dry, In order to their stations leap, And Music’s power obey.

Dryden’s poem begins with the creation of the universe, commanded into order by a voice from above. Handel set it in his Ode for St Cecilia’s Day, HWV 76, in 1739. Later stanzas call up the trumpet and the flute, and the orchestra supplies the instruments the words demand.

Composers

1668–1733

François Couperin

He played for Louis XIV on Sundays and taught the harpsichord to speak through a held note, a trill and a well-placed silence. 1678–1741

Antonio Vivaldi

He wrote concertos for the women of Venice’s Pietà, operas for its theatres, and a barking dog for the viola. 1683–1764

Jean-Philippe Rameau

He explained the roots of chords in a treatise, then sent them through hell in his first opera, at fifty. 1685–1750

Johann Sebastian Bach

He supplied Leipzig’s churches with music for twenty-seven years and left keyboard players enough work for a lifetime. 1685–1759

George Frideric Handel

He filled London theatres with Italian opera, then put an English-speaking chorus at the centre of the drama. 1685–1757

Domenico Scarlatti

A royal teacher whose sonatas send the hands flying across the keyboard, then bring them together for a song.

Further reading

  • Manfred F. Bukofzer, Music in the Baroque Era (W. W. Norton, 1947).survey

    A historical survey of Baroque musical styles; its early publication date makes it a counterpart to the later Cambridge histories.

  • Tim Carter and John Butt, eds, The Cambridge History of Seventeenth-Century Music (Cambridge University Press, 2005).survey

    Places opera, church music and other repertories beside institutions, politics, science and the other arts.

  • Simon P. Keefe, ed., The Cambridge History of Eighteenth-Century Music (Cambridge University Press, 2009).survey

    Organised around music for church, theatre and concert room, rather than a succession of composer biographies.
