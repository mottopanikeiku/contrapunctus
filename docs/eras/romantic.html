III1815–1890

Romanticism

Drama at the piano

Caspar David FriedrichTwo Men Contemplating the Moon, 1819–20
Galerie Neue Meister, Dresden

Overview

The piano begins before the voice enters, with repeated triplets fast enough to tire the pianist’s arm. Then a voice asks who is riding through the night. In Schubert’s Erlkönig, written in 1815, a single singer takes the parts of the narrator, the father with a child in his arms, the frightened boy and the creature calling him away. The father explains that the shapes are mist and the noises are wind.

A singer and pianist can perform this kind of theatre in a room. The voice changes register when the speaker changes, and the accompaniment supplies what the words leave unsaid. In Schubert’s Winterreise of 1827, twenty-four poems by Wilhelm Müller follow a solitary traveller through winter. Near the end of the first song, Gute Nacht, D minor gives way to D major as the traveller thinks of the woman he is leaving. The major key lasts only a short time.

The fragmentA short piano piece can end before its melody is complete, or open partway through a phrase whose beginning is not heard.

Schumann gathered small piano pieces into cycles in which one character interrupts another. Chopin’s mazurkas brought the uneven accent of a dance into the drawing room, with the weight often falling late in the bar so that the three beats are unequal. Hensel’s Das Jahr, composed in 1841, gives each of the twelve months a separate piece and adds a postlude.

The instrument tradeThe piano, Liszt and Berlioz

The piano changed during these years. Érard’s repetition action of 1821 let a hammer strike again before the key had risen all the way back. In Boston in 1825, Alpheus Babcock patented a cast-iron frame for a square piano. Iron could bear greater string tension than a wooden frame alone. As makers enlarged the instrument and strengthened its structure, the bass rang longer and the loudest chords gained weight.

The sustaining pedal lifts the dampers, so the strings go on vibrating after the fingers have left the keys. A pianist can strike a low bass note, leap to the middle of the keyboard and play an accompaniment beneath a high melody while the bass is still sounding. If the foot does not clear the pedal, the old harmony goes on sounding when the new one arrives. A singing line on the piano therefore depends on exact pedalling.

Liszt used the piano to bring music from the concert hall to other players. He made a transcription of Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique after hearing its first performance in Paris in 1830. In it a single pianist had to represent the different voices of the orchestra. A melody could stand out in the middle of the keyboard while chords sounded above it and a bass moved below.

Berlioz himself wrote for the orchestra in unusually graphic terms. In the final movement of the Symphonie fantastique, the beloved’s recurring tune returns in a witches’ gathering. Later in the movement the strings play with the wood of their bows, producing a dry clatter. His orchestration treatise grew from this close attention to instruments and from what orchestral players taught him.

The harmonic pressureChromatic harmony and delayed resolution

A change of harmony can be made with very small movements of the fingers. In the example below, C major becomes E major: E is kept, G moves up a semitone to G♯ and C moves down a semitone to B. Only two notes move, although the new chord belongs to a different scale. E major then leads to A minor. Chromatic notes allow a move to a distant key with only small steps in the individual parts.

Delayed resolutionA dissonant note is approached as though it will resolve, but the bass moves beneath it and forms another dissonant chord. Wagner repeats this procedure throughout Tristan und Isolde.

At the opening of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde, the cellos begin a phrase and the winds enter with the chord that has become inseparable from the opera’s name. Its notes move, but the next chord does not resolve it. The music stops and the phrase is repeated. Later in the opera, recurring ideas return between scenes: a leitmotif can come back on a different instrument or with changed harmony, recalling an earlier moment.

Berlioz also brought back a returning tune, and cut it short. In the March to the Scaffold, the fourth movement of the Symphonie fantastique, the clarinet plays the beloved’s theme just before the execution. The orchestra cuts the tune off when it has scarcely begun. The theme is familiar from the preceding movements.

The publicConcert, salon, opera house

In 1844 Hensel told her sister Rebecka about a Sunday concert at the family home in Berlin. There had been “twenty-two carriages in the courtyard, and Liszt and eight princesses in the hall”. She had revived these concerts in 1831, conducting and playing the piano herself. The garden room at Leipziger Straße 3 gave her an audience and musicians for her own work, even while her family discouraged a public career.

Clara Schumann took her piano playing across Europe. She also had to arrange much of the business behind it, with help from her daughters and friends. From the 1840s, trains made journeys faster and concerts more frequent; appearances could follow only a day or two apart. Her programmes increasingly placed Bach and Beethoven beside Schumann and Chopin. On her last visit to London in 1888, she bade farewell with Schumann’s Carnaval.

Two against threeSix quavers can fall into two groups of three or three groups of two. Changing the accents switches the grouping without changing the length of the bar. The score example below uses this effect, called hemiola.

Publishers wanted music for people who played at home. Fritz Simrock commissioned Dvořák’s first set of Slavonic Dances in 1878 for piano duet. Dvořák then orchestrated the dances. Two people at one keyboard could play the same pieces that a concert orchestra performed, though the orchestral version added trumpets and cymbals.

Older music was acquiring a new public too. Bach had written his St Matthew Passion for church in Leipzig. In 1829 Mendelssohn conducted it for a concert audience in Berlin.

At the end of Winterreise, the traveller meets an old hurdy-gurdy player standing barefoot on the ice. The piano repeats open fifths in imitation of the instrument’s drone. The singer asks whether the old man will accompany his songs. The song ends without an answer.

Terms of Art

Chromatic mediant

A relationship between two major chords or two minor chords whose roots are a third apart and which share one note. C major and E major are one example: they share E, while the second chord introduces G♯.

Character piece

A short instrumental piece, often for piano, built around a particular mood, scene or dance. A descriptive title may identify its subject.

Song cycle

A group of songs intended to form one larger work. They may tell a continuing story, set related poems or share musical ideas.

Thematic transformation

Changing a recognisable theme so that it takes on a new character. A slow melody might become a quick march, or a lyrical tune might return with harsher harmony.

Leitmotif

A recurring musical idea associated with a person, object, feeling or event in a drama. Its return can recall an earlier scene, and changes to it can reflect what has happened since.

Cyclic form

A way of linking a work’s separate movements by bringing back a theme or motif from one movement in another. The returning music may be changed or quoted exactly.

Programme music

Instrumental music written in connection with a stated story, scene or other subject outside music. A title or written programme identifies that subject.

Rubato

Expressive flexibility in musical timing. A performer may stretch and shorten parts of a phrase, or let the melody move a little ahead of or behind a steadier accompaniment.

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Erlkönig — opening stanzas

1782
GermanWer reitet so spät durch Nacht und Wind? Es ist der Vater mit seinem Kind; Er hat den Knaben wohl in dem Arm, Er faßt ihn sicher, er hält ihn warm. “Mein Sohn, was birgst du so bang dein Gesicht?” “Siehst, Vater, du den Erlkönig nicht? Den Erlenkönig mit Kron’ und Schweif?” “Mein Sohn, es ist ein Nebelstreif.”
Our translationWho rides so late through night and wind? It is the father with his child; He has the boy held fast in his arm, He grips him firmly, he keeps him warm. “My son, why hide your face so fearfully?” “Father, can you not see the Erlking? The Erlking with crown and trailing train?” “My son, it is a streak of mist.”

One singer takes both the child’s terrified lines and the father’s reassuring answers. In Schubert’s 1815 setting, D 328, the piano plays repeated triplets beneath them. When the ride stops, the narrator states that the child in the father’s arms is dead.

Composers

1792–1868

Gioachino Rossini

Composer known for ensembles of quarrelling characters sung with clockwork precision, and for the orchestral depiction of mountains and storms in William Tell. 1797–1828

Franz Schubert

Composer who at seventeen wrote a song for Gretchen with a spinning-wheel accompaniment and at thirty-one wrote music for two cellos. 1803–1869

Hector Berlioz

He used the full orchestra at loud volume and also a single cor anglais playing alone against silence. 1805–1847

Fanny Hensel

Berlin composer who ran the city’s Sunday concerts, wrote more than 450 works and published collections under her own name only in the last year of her life. 1809–1847

Felix Mendelssohn

Composer who wrote a work for eight string players at the age of sixteen. 1810–1849

Frédéric Chopin

Composer for the piano, known for music that drew on operatic singing and on the irregular accents of the mazurka. 1810–1856

Robert Schumann

A bookseller’s son, composer of piano music depicting lovers, masks and arguments, and a music critic. 1811–1886

Franz Liszt

Pianist whose playing drew orchestral sound from the keyboard, and who at the end of his life turned to sparse music at the piano. 1813–1901

Giuseppe Verdi

Composer whose catchy tenor tunes could carry a fatal meaning when the wrong man heard them. 1813–1883

Richard Wagner

Composer who placed the orchestra beneath the stage at Bayreuth, hidden from the audience. 1819–1896

Clara Schumann

Performer on the concert platform for forty years, who wrote of making music, ‘It is the air I breathe.’ 1824–1896

Anton Bruckner

Composer who studied his craft for half his life before writing symphonies in which a horn call can expand into full brass. 1824–1884

Bedřich Smetana

Czech composer and conductor from Litomyšl, whose operas for Prague’s Provisional Theatre include The Bartered Bride; deaf from 1874, he then wrote Má vlast and the quartet From My Life. 1833–1887

Alexander Borodin

Russian chemist, professor at the Medical-Surgical Academy in Saint Petersburg, who composed two symphonies, two string quartets and the opera Prince Igor. 1833–1897

Johannes Brahms

Composer who completed his First Symphony at the age of forty-three and began his Second beside an Austrian lake the following summer. 1838–1875

Georges Bizet

A factory worker sings that love obeys no law, while four bass notes beneath her voice keep the dance going. 1839–1881

Modest Mussorgsky

He could make a singer sound like a frightened child, a drunken monk or a tsar who could no longer bear his own thoughts. 1840–1893

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

Composer who could write a brilliant ballroom scene and, within it, a single melody showing that someone present was unhappy. 1841–1904

Antonín Dvořák

Composer of the <i>Slavonic Dances</i>, who played viola in an opera orchestra for nine years before his music became widely known. 1843–1907

Edvard Grieg

Son of a Bergen merchant; used Norwegian fiddle tunes, a troll king and a wedding party in concert-hall music. 1844–1908

Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov

Russian composer and professor, known for fairy-tale operas and his detailed knowledge of the orchestra; he became a professor before he had learned counterpoint.
