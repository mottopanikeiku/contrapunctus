III1815–1890

Romanticism

A whole drama at the piano

Caspar David FriedrichTwo Men Contemplating the Moon, 1819–20
Galerie Neue Meister, Dresden

The Story

The piano starts before anyone speaks: repeated triplets, fast enough to make the pianist’s arm ache. Then a voice asks who is riding through the night. In Schubert’s Erlkönig, written in 1815, a single singer must become the narrator, the father with a child in his arms, the frightened boy and the creature calling him away. The father explains that the shapes are mist and the noises are wind. The piano keeps running.

A singer and pianist can fit this theatre into a room. The voice changes register as the speaker changes; the accompaniment supplies what the words leave unsaid. In Schubert’s Winterreise of 1827, twenty-four poems by Wilhelm Müller follow a solitary traveller through winter. Near the end of the first song, Gute Nacht, D minor gives way to D major as he thinks tenderly of the woman he is leaving. The warmer key lasts only briefly.

The fragmentA short piano piece can end before its melody seems finished, or begin as if the preceding phrase had been lost. Its small size leaves room for an unfinished thought.

Schumann gathered small piano pieces into cycles, letting one character interrupt another. Chopin’s mazurkas brought a dance’s awkward accent into the drawing room: the weight can fall late in the bar, making three beats feel lopsided. Hensel’s Das Jahr, composed in 1841, gives the months twelve separate pieces and adds a postlude. A pianist can travel through a year without leaving the stool.

The instrument tradeMore tension, faster repetition

The piano was changing under those fingers. Érard’s repetition action of 1821 let a hammer strike again before the key had risen all the way back. In Boston in 1825, Alpheus Babcock patented a cast-iron frame for a square piano. Iron could bear greater string tension than a wooden frame alone. As makers enlarged the instrument and strengthened its structure, the bass acquired a longer ring and the loudest chords more weight.

The sustaining pedal lifts the dampers, leaving strings free to vibrate after the fingers have moved away. A pianist can strike a low bass note, leap to the middle of the keyboard and play an accompaniment beneath a high melody, with the bass still sounding. But the old harmony will linger when the new one arrives unless the foot clears it. The apparent ease of a singing line depends on quite exact work below the keyboard.

Liszt used the piano to carry music from the concert hall into other hands. He made a transcription of Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique after hearing its first performance in Paris in 1830. One player had to suggest the orchestra’s different voices. A melody could stand out in the middle of the keyboard while chords sounded above it and a bass moved below.

Berlioz himself made the orchestra do things with almost indecent vividness. In the final movement of the Symphonie fantastique, the beloved’s recurring tune returns in a witches’ gathering. The strings eventually strike with the wood of their bows, producing a dry clatter. His orchestration treatise grew from this close attention to instruments and from what orchestral players taught him. A violin was a wooden object with possibilities still to be tried.

The harmonic pressureDistance with a common note

A change of harmony can happen almost under the fingers. In the example below, C major becomes E major by keeping E, moving G up a semitone to G♯ and C down to B. The new chord has arrived with two small movements, although it belongs to a different scale. E major then leads to A minor. Chromatic notes can make a sudden change of scene feel as smooth as a door opening.

Delayed resolutionA dissonant note can seem about to resolve, only for the bass to move beneath it and create another unsettled chord. Wagner makes this repeated disappointment part of the sound of Tristan und Isolde.

At the opening of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde, the cellos begin a phrase and the winds enter with the chord that has become inseparable from the opera’s name. Its notes move, but the next chord still asks for something. The music falls silent and tries again. Later, recurring ideas carry memories between scenes: a leitmotif can return on a different instrument or with a changed harmony, bringing an earlier moment into the present conversation.

Berlioz gives a returning tune a more brutal fate. In the March to the Scaffold, the fourth movement of the Symphonie fantastique, the clarinet recalls the beloved just before the execution. The tune has scarcely begun when the orchestra cuts it off. A theme familiar from the preceding movements makes the interruption personal; the notes themselves have become someone the condemned man remembers.

The publicConcert, salon, opera house

In 1844 Hensel told her sister Rebecka about a Sunday concert at the family home in Berlin: there had been “twenty-two carriages in the courtyard, and Liszt and eight princesses in the hall”. She had revived these concerts in 1831, conducting and playing the piano herself. The garden room at Leipziger Straße 3 gave her an audience and musicians for her own work, even while her family discouraged a public career.

Clara Schumann took her piano playing across Europe. She also had to arrange much of the business behind it, with help from her daughters and friends. From the 1840s, trains made journeys faster and concerts more frequent; appearances could follow only a day or two apart. Her programmes increasingly placed Bach and Beethoven beside Schumann and Chopin. On her last visit to London in 1888, she bade farewell with Schumann’s Carnaval.

Two against threeSix quavers can fall into two groups of three or three groups of two. Changing the accents switches the grouping without changing the length of the bar. The score example below uses this effect, called hemiola.

Publishers wanted music for people who played at home. Fritz Simrock commissioned Dvořák’s first set of Slavonic Dances in 1878 for piano duet. Dvořák then orchestrated the dances. Two people at one keyboard could know the same pieces that a concert orchestra would play, though the orchestral version added the bite of trumpets and the crash of cymbals. The music had two ways of reaching a public.

Older music was acquiring a new public too. Bach had written his St Matthew Passion for church in Leipzig. In 1829 Mendelssohn conducted it for a concert audience in Berlin.

At the end of Winterreise, the traveller finds an old hurdy-gurdy player standing barefoot on the ice. The piano repeats open fifths, imitating the instrument’s drone. The singer asks whether the old man will accompany his songs. No answer comes. A cycle that began with footsteps ends with a wheel still turning.

Terms of Art

Chromatic mediant

A relationship between two major chords or two minor chords whose roots are a third apart and which share one note. C major and E major are one example: they share E, while the second chord introduces G♯.

Character piece

A short instrumental piece, often for piano, built around a particular mood, scene or dance. A descriptive title may identify its subject.

Song cycle

A group of songs intended to form one larger work. They may tell a continuing story, set related poems or share musical ideas.

Thematic transformation

Changing a recognisable theme so that it takes on a new character. A slow melody might become a quick march, or a lyrical tune return in a harsher harmony.

Leitmotif

A recurring musical idea associated with a person, object, feeling or event in a drama. Its return can recall an earlier scene, and changes to it can reflect what has happened since.

Cyclic form

A way of linking a work’s separate movements by bringing back a theme or motif from one movement in another. The returning music may be changed or quoted exactly.

Programme music

Instrumental music written in connection with a stated story, scene or other subject outside music. A title or written programme identifies that subject.

Rubato

Expressive flexibility in musical timing. A performer may stretch and shorten parts of a phrase, or let the melody move a little ahead of or behind a steadier accompaniment.

Sister Arts

Monks carry a coffin towards the ruined church. Their procession is tiny beneath the bare oaks and the surviving Gothic window; a pale moon hangs in the sky. Friedrich showed the painting in Berlin in 1810 beside Monk by the Sea. Its winter landscape makes an apt neighbour for Schubert’s solitary traveller, with so little shelter left in sight.

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Erlkönig — opening stanzas

1782
GermanWer reitet so spät durch Nacht und Wind? Es ist der Vater mit seinem Kind; Er hat den Knaben wohl in dem Arm, Er faßt ihn sicher, er hält ihn warm. “Mein Sohn, was birgst du so bang dein Gesicht?” “Siehst, Vater, du den Erlkönig nicht? Den Erlenkönig mit Kron’ und Schweif?” “Mein Sohn, es ist ein Nebelstreif.”
Our translationWho rides so late through night and wind? It is the father with his child; He has the boy held fast in his arm, He grips him firmly, he keeps him warm. “My son, why hide your face so fearfully?” “Father, can you not see the Erlking? The Erlking with crown and trailing train?” “My son, it is a streak of mist.”

One singer has to deliver both the child’s terror and the father’s reassuring answer. In Schubert’s 1815 setting, D 328, the piano keeps up its repeated triplets beneath them. The ride finally stops, leaving the narrator to say that the child in the father’s arms is dead.

Composers

1792–1868

Gioachino Rossini

He made a roomful of quarrelling people sing like clockwork, then gave William Tell an orchestra of mountains and storms. 1797–1828

Franz Schubert

At seventeen he set a spinning wheel turning under Gretchen’s voice; at thirty-one he could make two cellos sing as if they had always belonged together. 1803–1869

Hector Berlioz

He could make an orchestra thunder, and knew when to leave a single cor anglais calling into silence. 1805–1847

Fanny Hensel

She ran Berlin’s Sunday concerts, wrote more than 450 works, and put her own name on published collections only in the last year of her life. 1809–1847

Felix Mendelssohn

At sixteen, he made eight string players sound like the most exhilarating orchestra in Europe. 1810–1849

Frédéric Chopin

He brought the opera singer’s breath and the mazurka’s wayward accents to a piano that could whisper across a room. 1810–1856

Robert Schumann

A bookseller’s son who put lovers, masks and arguments into piano music, and made criticism a form of friendship. 1811–1886

Franz Liszt

He could fit an orchestra under ten fingers, then end a lifetime at the piano with a few notes left hanging in the air. 1813–1901

Giuseppe Verdi

He could make a catchy tenor tune sound like a death sentence simply by letting the wrong man hear it. 1813–1883

Richard Wagner

He hid the orchestra beneath the stage at Bayreuth and taught it to remember everything his characters wanted to forget. 1819–1896

Clara Schumann

‘It is the air I breathe,’ she wrote of making music, after forty years on the concert platform. 1824–1896

Anton Bruckner

He spent half his life learning his trade, then wrote symphonies in which a horn call can grow into a wall of brass. 1833–1897

Johannes Brahms

He kept his First Symphony waiting until he was forty-three, then began the Second beside an Austrian lake the next summer. 1838–1875

Georges Bizet

A factory worker sings that love obeys no law. Beneath her voice, four bass notes keep the dance going. 1839–1881

Modest Mussorgsky

He could make a singer sound like a frightened child, a drunken monk or a tsar who can no longer bear his own thoughts. 1840–1893

Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky

He could make a ballroom glitter, then let one unguarded melody tell us who was unhappy there. 1841–1904

Antonín Dvořák

He had played viola in the opera pit for nine years before the world discovered the composer of the <i>Slavonic Dances</i>. 1844–1908

Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov

He became a professor before he could write counterpoint, then built Russian fairy-tale operas around an orchestra he knew instrument by instrument.
