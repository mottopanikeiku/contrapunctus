The comparison

The <i>Butterfly</i> Étude springs from a tiny four-note figure, with clipped octaves above a leaping left hand. Chopin keeps the same idea flitting through new keys and different reaches of the keyboard. Eugen d’Albert, a pupil of Liszt, shares the piece here with Alfred Cortot and Edward Neeman.

How the recordings are matched

The matching follows sequences of pitches, using a method called dynamic time warping. Every recording is mapped to the reference, so switching between two versions goes through that shared clock. Where several reference times match one point, the middle time is used.

  • Eugen d’Albert, 1912–13: match score 0.920. Matched passages available.
  • Alfred Cortot, 1920: match score 0.871. Matched passages available.

Switching stays within the matched span, and trimmed recordings keep their original clock times. Match scores describe similarity in the sound.
