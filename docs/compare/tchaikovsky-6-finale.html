The comparison

In February 1893, Tchaikovsky told his nephew Vladimir Davydov that his new symphony would end with “a most long-drawn-out Adagio”. The finale begins with a falling melody whose notes pass between the two violin sections. Furtwängler’s Berlin Philharmonic and the Musopen Symphony carry that lament through its great outbursts to the quiet low strings at the end.

  2. 02

    The D-major song

    2:50 in the reference

    Horns support a broad descending melody in the first violins and violas; trombones answer it.

  3. 03

    The orchestra rushes down

    4:30 in the reference

    Fast descending scales carry the broad melody to its climax. The whole orchestra joins the fall.

  4. 04

    Brass joins the lament

    7:00 in the reference

    The returning lament reaches another outburst. Trombones and tuba reinforce the strings as the music presses towards a long-held F♯.

  5. 05

    The last descent

    9:05 in the reference

    The broad melody returns in B minor. Muted strings descend, and the tune sinks gradually towards the cellos and basses.

How the recordings are matched

The matching follows sequences of pitches, using a method called dynamic time warping. Every recording is mapped to the reference, so switching between two versions goes through that shared clock. Where several reference times match one point, the middle time is used.

  • Wilhelm Furtwängler, Berlin Philharmonic: match score 0.875. Matched passages available.

Switching stays within the matched span, and trimmed recordings keep their original clock times. Match scores describe similarity in the sound.
