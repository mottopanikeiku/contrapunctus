The comparison

A traveller meets a six-winged seraph in the desert; the angel tears out his heart and puts a burning coal in its place. Rimsky-Korsakov gives Pushkin’s terrible vision to a bass voice, beginning quietly and ending with God’s command to speak. Chaliapin recorded it with Percy Pitt in 1921 and returned to it with Albert Coates in 1927.

Hear each version in card order, covering up to 12 seconds of reference music or the next marked passage, whichever comes first.

  1. 01

    The traveller speaks

    0:35 in the reference

    The bass tells his story in measured phrases. Flowing notes in the accompaniment surround the voice as the seraph appears.

  2. 02

    The voice rises

    1:20 in the reference

    The bass reaches higher over rippling accompaniment, describing the sounds that fill the traveller’s newly opened ears.

  3. 03

    A pause in the telling

    1:55 in the reference

    The accompaniment continues between the sung phrases, growing softer and slowing before the story resumes.

  4. 04

    God’s command

    3:20 in the reference

    The bass delivers God’s charge above firm chords. Rimsky-Korsakov marks the passage <i>con forza e maestoso</i>: forcefully and majestically.

How the recordings are matched

The matching follows sequences of pitches, using a method called dynamic time warping. Every recording is mapped to the reference, so switching between two versions goes through that shared clock. Where several reference times match one point, the middle time is used.

  • Fyodor Chaliapin, Albert Coates: match score 0.950. Matched passages available.

Switching stays within the matched span, and trimmed recordings keep their original clock times. Match scores describe similarity in the sound.
