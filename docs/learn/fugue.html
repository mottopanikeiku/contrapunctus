The idea in detail

A fugue starts by making a melody identifiable before making it compete with other melodies. One voice states the subject; another enters with an answer, normally at the dominant level. A real answer transposes every interval exactly. A tonal answer adjusts an interval, often exchanging a tonic-to-dominant gesture for dominant-to-tonic, to keep the opening within the home key. The distinction concerns exact and adjusted transposition.

One exact subjectThe example is the opening alto line of Bach’s C major Fugue, BWV 846, from Book I of The Well-Tempered Clavier (1722). The subject reaches E on beat three of bar 2; the remaining semiquavers continue the line.

The first voice continues when the second begins. If its accompanying material regularly returns against later statements of the subject, that material is a countersubject. A useful countersubject often works above or below the subject, requiring invertible counterpoint. BWV 846 proceeds without a regular countersubject; its initial answer is real, and its four opening entries arrive in the order subject, answer, answer, subject.

The opening round of entries is called the exposition. Subsequent entries may move into related keys, changing the subject’s harmonic surroundings while preserving enough of its intervals and rhythm for recognition. An episode is a passage without a complete subject statement, often made from a fragment sequenced at successive pitch levels. An episode can move the harmony towards a new entrance or thin the texture after a crowded passage. BWV 846 sustains its argument largely through entries and overlaps.

StrettoA new statement begins before the previous statement has finished. The distance between entries changes while the tempo can remain steady.

In stretto, the subject must combine contrapuntally with a displaced version of itself. Bach can shorten the interval between entrances, change their registers, or let more voices join the overlap. Those choices produce intensification without introducing a louder dynamic or a new theme. Augmentation lengthens the subject’s note values; inversion reverses the direction of its intervals. Listen for the moment of each entrance: it may interrupt an episode, complete a cadence, or turn an apparently accompanying voice into the principal speaker.
