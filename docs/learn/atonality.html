What holds music together without a home key?

Atonality: organised intervals without a home key

Hear itReadPerformancesObjectsContinue

Recognise the small three-note shape as it moves higher and then turns upside down.

Synthesised example

The idea in detail

Atonal music organises pitch outside the governing tonic hierarchy of major–minor harmony. It can use repetition, melody and consonant intervals. The question is whether one pitch or triad controls the others as a tonal centre over the relevant span. A dense chromatic passage in Wagner can preserve that control; a spare line in Webern can dispense with it.

Before twelve-tone compositionSchoenberg’s Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11 (1909), precede his twelve-tone method. Atonality and serialism describe distinct historical and technical developments.

Without dominant-to-tonic syntax, other relationships become more exposed. A three-note cell can recur at a new pitch level, turn upside down, expand into a chord or pass between instruments. Its intervals may remain recognisable even when its register and rhythm change. A pitch-class analysis treats octave-equivalent notes as members of the same class, making some of these relations easier to describe. Such a reduction leaves out register, dynamics and articulation, all of which may be essential to the phrase as heard.

In the example, C–C♯–F contains an ascending semitone followed by a major third. G–G♯–C transposes that pattern up a fifth. C–B–G reverses the interval directions, producing an inversion, and D–C♯–A transposes that inversion. These controlled relationships connect the cells independently of a V–I cadence, offering one way of relating events outside a key.

Consonance can remain localA major third or perfect fifth inside an atonal piece is still audible as an interval. What disappears is the assumption that it must belong to a tonic, dominant or another chord within a single key.

Schoenberg’s Erwartung, Op. 17 (1909), uses the continuity of a dramatic monologue; Webern’s short instrumental works concentrate on register, silence and articulation; Berg retains tonal references and familiar forms within a more mixed language. Each score’s organisation emerges through its intervals, rhythms and returns. Kandinsky’s response to Schoenberg in 1911 supplies a documented connection with painting.

Objects and sources

Shared context

Kandinsky attended a Schoenberg concert in Munich on 2 January 1911, with Gabriele Münter and Franz Marc among his companions. The programme included the Second String Quartet and the Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11; this painting followed the experience. The Lenbachhaus record documents the connection. The black mass still suggests the piano as the picture reorganises the concert scene. Schoenberg’s twelve-tone method was still to come.
