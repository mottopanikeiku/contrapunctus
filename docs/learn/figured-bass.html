How can numbers tell a player what to play?

Figured bass: numbers above a moving foundation

Hear itReadPerformancesContinue

Listen to the low note change beneath the same upper chord: the foundation changes what the numbers describe.

Synthesised example

The idea in detail

Figured bass gives a player a written bass line and numbers specifying intervals above it. A harpsichordist, organist or theorbo player usually supplies the upper voices, choosing their spacing, rhythm and register to suit the singers or instrumentalists. In a Baroque continuo group, a cello or bassoon may reinforce the bass without playing chords. The division between bass instrument and chordal instrument answers practical needs, and the combination can vary between ensembles.

Count from the bassOver C, a 6 asks for A; over E, a 6 asks for C. The numeral names an interval above whichever bass note is sounding.

An unfigured bass commonly implies a third and fifth, adjusted to the key signature and the musical context. A 6 normally abbreviates 6/3; 6/4 asks for a fourth and sixth. Over E in C major, G and C therefore produce a first-inversion C major chord. Over G, C and E form a six-four sonority. At a cadence those upper notes often move down to B and D over the same G bass: the figures 6–5 and 4–3 describe the movement directly. A bare accidental usually alters the third; an accidental beside a numeral alters that numbered interval.

The numbers leave essential decisions open. Doubling a note can strengthen a chord, but doubling a leading note may make a clean resolution difficult. A singer’s sustained dissonance needs space in the accompaniment. A slow aria may invite an arpeggiated texture; a rapid recitative requires the player to follow the delivery of the text. Realisation is therefore both harmony and counterpoint: the player is composing several lines while accompanying an existing one.

Figures are not chord symbolsA modern “C/E” names a C chord with E in the bass. The historical figure 6 over E describes the intervals needed at that particular moment; the identity of the root is an analytical inference.

The example moves through C, C/E and D minor/F before its dominant seventh and tonic. Its upper voices are one possible realisation. The flexible notation constrains the harmony while leaving a trained player responsible for the texture and the local voice-leading.
