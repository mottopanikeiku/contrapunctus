Can the same notes make different scales?

Modes: change the centre, change the intervals

The idea in detail

The white notes of the piano can support several centres. Give D that role and the collection forms D Dorian: D–E–F–G–A–B–C. Its minor third coexists with a major sixth, B, rather than the B♭ of natural minor. E Phrygian places a semitone immediately above its centre, E–F. F Lydian has a raised fourth, B, compared with F major. G Mixolydian has a lowered seventh, F, compared with G major. A sustained bass, phrase endings and recurring melodic shapes establish which note acts as centre.

Medieval modes are more than scalesHistorical church-mode classification also concerns final, range and reciting tone, with authentic and plagal forms. Modern modal composition often borrows interval patterns without reproducing that complete system.

Transposition carries the interval pattern to a new pitch level. C Lydian therefore needs F♯; D Mixolydian needs F♯ and C♮. A Dorian tune may remain recognisably modal even when its harmony occasionally uses a leading note at a cadence. Modes can govern a phrase, a pedal passage or a thematic strand while the surrounding music works through ordinary major–minor tonality.

Sibelius’s Sixth Symphony, Op. 104 (1923), draws much of its opening string writing from a Dorian-inflected D centre. The natural sixth alters the minor colour without requiring a chromatic detour. Vaughan Williams’s Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis (1910) takes its principal melody from Tallis’s third tune for Archbishop Parker’s Psalter, published in 1567; its Phrygian inheritance contributes to the expanded string-orchestra setting’s harmonic vocabulary.

Listen to the distinctive degreeDorian: natural sixth in a minor collection. Phrygian: lowered second. Lydian: raised fourth in a major collection. Mixolydian: lowered seventh. These comparisons assume the same tonic.

Debussy combines modal and symmetrical collections. In L’isle joyeuse (1904), A Lydian belongs to a network that also includes A major, whole-tone material and the acoustic collection A–B–C♯–D♯–E–F♯–G. The last collection’s natural G distinguishes it from ordinary Lydian. Dmitri Tymoczko’s analysis of Debussy’s scale networks traces these small changes between collections. The two scale examples isolate the distinctive degrees for comparison.
