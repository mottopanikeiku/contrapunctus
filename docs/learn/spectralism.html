Can one sound supply a whole harmony?

Spectralism: a timbre becomes harmonic material

Hear itReadObjectsContinue

Follow the climb from a low note into the higher pitches that help give a sound its character.

Synthesised example

The idea in detail

A harmonic spectrum contains components at whole-number multiples of a fundamental frequency: f, 2f, 3f, 4f and so on. Its spacing is uneven when heard as musical intervals: the first two components form an octave, the next pair a fifth, and higher neighbours become progressively closer. Their relative strengths and their behaviour during the attack and decay help determine timbre. Spectral composition uses such frequency relationships and changes over time as material for harmony, orchestration and form.

Partials and harmonicsA partial is a component of a complex sound. A harmonic partial is an integer multiple of the fundamental. Bells and other inharmonic sounds supply spectral music with different frequency relations.

Gérard Grisey’s Partiels (1975), for eighteen musicians, opens around a low E and spreads a harmonic field across instrumental registers. The ensemble can be heard as separate notes or as a composite sound; changing their entrances, intensity and tuning changes that perception. François-Xavier Féron’s research in the sketches distinguishes the early works’ constructed spectra from the later use of measured sonographic data.

A source correctionIn his 2022 IRCAM presentation, Féron states that no trombone sound served as the model for Périodes or Partiels; direct spectral analyses informed sections of later works including Modulations and Transitoires.

Tristan Murail’s Gondwana (1980) brings inharmonic, bell-like fields into relation with more harmonic spectra. Electronic synthesis supplies compositional models even when the performing medium is an orchestra: calculated frequency relationships can become instrumental pitches, and changes of spectral density can govern a transition. Real instruments contribute spectra of their own, making the ensemble sound an interpretation of the model.

The seventh harmonic illustrates why conventional notation needs qualification. Above an E fundamental it lies near D, about 31.17 cents below the equal-tempered pitch; the fifth harmonic lies about 13.69 cents below an equal-tempered G♯. Quarter-tone or finer notation can approximate these relations, but a normal piano cannot tune them independently. The invented example plays equal-tempered landmarks. Grisey’s later L’Icône paradoxale adds another form of modelling: Piero della Francesca’s spatial proportions and writings on perspective enter the construction of musical time and vocal material.

Objects and sources

Documented use

Grisey explicitly based L’Icône paradoxale (1992–94), for two women’s voices and divided orchestra, on this fresco. In his programme note he describes the opened tent and the opening of Mary’s dress as nested spaces, and identifies the proportions 3:5:8:12 as temporal material. The voices use Piero’s names and text from his perspective treatise. The approximate dating follows the Monterchi museum.
