How do unexpected notes change a familiar harmony?

Chromaticism: the spelling records the pressure

Hear itReadMore examplesPerformancesContinue

Follow the notes that move by the smallest steps as the unsettled chords find a resting place.

Synthesised example

The idea in detail

A chromatic note lies outside the prevailing diatonic collection. It may decorate a line without changing the harmony, alter a chord’s function, or prepare another key. These are different events: a passing C♯ between C and D need not announce D major. In nineteenth-century music, chromatic notes often make otherwise familiar functions more insistent. The decisive evidence is their resolution. Two chords with the same piano keys can have different harmonic purposes if their notes are spelled and directed differently.

NeapolitanIn C minor, the major triad on the lowered second degree is D♭–F–A♭. It commonly appears with F in the bass, hence “Neapolitan sixth”, and usually prepares the dominant rather than establishing D♭ as a new tonic.

An augmented-sixth chord puts the lowered sixth and raised fourth degrees around the dominant. In C, A♭ below F♯ expands outward to G–G. Add C to obtain the Italian form; add C and D for the French; add C and E♭ for the German. The national names are later classifications. The German form commonly moves through a cadential six-four before the dominant, avoiding the parallel fifths that a direct resolution can produce.

Enharmonic modulation changes a chord’s spelling and destination while preserving its sounding pitches in equal temperament. A♭–C–E♭–F♯, a German augmented sixth in C, can be respelled A♭–C–E♭–G♭, the dominant seventh of D♭. Its outer notes take on new functions. Fully diminished sevenths offer another resource: B–D–F–A♭ can point towards C; respelling A♭ as G♯ lets the same keyboard collection act as G♯–B–D–F, directed towards A. The musical context establishes the new interpretation.

Equal-tempered equivalenceF♯ and G♭ share a key on a modern piano. They need not have identical frequencies in every historical tuning, or identical melodic inflections in a string quartet.

Chopin frequently embeds these resources within carefully directed inner voices: chromatic lines continue through the chord changes. His Prelude in E minor, Op. 28 No. 4, is a compact lesson in descending chromatic accompaniment beneath a restricted melody. Liszt uses enharmonic reinterpretation to connect more distant regions within extended forms, including the Sonata in B minor, S. 178. The invented examples isolate the harmonic mechanisms. Follow each altered note to its destination.
