How does changing instruments change a tune?

Orchestration: register, balance and the attack of a note

Hear itReadPerformancesContinue

Hear the same short tune twice and notice what changes when a different instrument takes it over.

Synthesised example

The idea in detail

Orchestration assigns musical material to instruments and changes its effect. A flute projects differently in its low and high registers; a horn can merge with woodwind or become a distinct brass voice. A clarinet note with a gentle attack can enter beneath a string tone and temporarily conceal its source. Register, articulation and dynamic determine whether a line separates from its surroundings or combines with another instrument into a composite sound.

Two working booksBerlioz’s Grand traité d’instrumentation et d’orchestration modernes appeared in 1843–44. Rimsky-Korsakov’s posthumous Principles of Orchestration, edited by Maximilian Steinberg, organises practical questions of balance, doubling and the distribution of parts.

Berlioz describes instrumental mechanisms and ranges alongside their dramatic uses, illustrating his claims with passages from actual scores. Rimsky-Korsakov separates problems such as a melody in octaves, sustained harmony and accompaniment, testing how orchestral families combine. Applying either book requires knowledge of the particular player or instrument. A written note has different consequences on a stopped horn, a muted trumpet and an open string, even when the sounding frequency agrees.

Ravel’s Boléro (1928) holds its principal rhythmic ostinato and melodic material relatively constant while changing instrumental presentation and accumulating weight. The first melody arrives on solo flute; later statements exploit instruments including saxophones and trombone. A particularly instructive combination uses horn, celesta and piccolos at different pitch levels to suggest the components of a composite timbre. The harmony’s long stability makes those changes unusually exposed. The work also contains a late tonal disruption.

Doubling has a costAdding instruments can thicken a line without making its articulation clearer. A low, widely spaced chord may project better than the same notes crowded together in the bass, where their partials overlap densely.

Extended techniques change the method of sound production: sul ponticello places the bow near the bridge, col legno uses its wood, and wind multiphonics use specialised fingerings and embouchures to produce several components together. Their notation must specify a reproducible action. The example assigns the same notes to flute and oboe so that timbre is the only intended variable. Its General MIDI playback demonstrates instrumental assignment in isolation from a live player’s breath, attack, dynamics and room.
