How it works

Eighteen short lessons in melody, harmony, rhythm and instrumental sound. Start with a question, play the example, then follow the explanation.

  1. 1 · Independent linesHow can two melodies fit together?Counterpoint: two melodies, one argument
  2. 2 · ImitationWhat happens when the same tune enters again?Fugue: an entrance changes the texture
  3. 3 · A working notationHow can numbers tell a player what to play?Figured bass: numbers above a moving foundation
  4. 4 · TuningWhy can the same notes sound differently tuned?Temperament: where to put the discrepancy
  5. 5 · Tonal directionWhy does one chord seem to need another?Harmony: a chord’s job depends on where it goes
  6. 6 · Large-scale tonalityWhy does a returning tune feel different?Sonata form: the return changes the tonal route
  7. 7 · Altered scale degreesHow do unexpected notes change a familiar harmony?Chromaticism: the spelling records the pressure
  8. 8 · A disputed sonorityWhy does this chord leave us waiting?The Tristan chord: four notes, several readings
  9. 9 · Musical memoryHow can a tune remember a character?Leitmotif: recognition with a changed meaning
  10. 10 · Diatonic alternativesCan the same notes make different scales?Modes: change the centre, change the intervals
  11. 11 · Six equal stepsWhat happens when every scale step is equal?Whole-tone harmony: no privileged semitone
  12. 12 · Eight-note symmetryWhat can a scale of eight notes do?Octatonic harmony: alternating whole tones and semitones
  13. 13 · Without a tonicWhat holds music together without a home key?Atonality: organised intervals without a home key
  14. 14 · Ordered pitch classesCan an ordered set of notes shape music?Twelve-tone writing: a row orders pitch classes
  15. 15 · Grouping timeWhy can a steady beat feel off balance?Rhythm: pulse and accent can diverge
  16. 16 · Instrumental constructionHow does changing instruments change a tune?Orchestration: register, balance and the attack of a note
  17. 17 · Audible processesHow can repetition make something new?Minimalism: repetition makes the change measurable
  18. 18 · Inside the soundCan one sound supply a whole harmony?Spectralism: a timbre becomes harmonic material
Origins

Before Bach

Nine encounters with the ideas, instruments and practices behind Bach’s world. The dates overlap: these traditions developed alongside one another, not as a single line of progress.

  1. 1 · c. 530 BCWhy does a shorter string sound higher?A string divided
  2. 2 · c. 750–1200How can one melody carry a whole service?One line, many functions
  3. 3 · c. 900–1200What happens when chant gains another voice?A second voice above the chant
  4. 4 · c. 1025How did singers learn unfamiliar music?A melody that can be read
  5. 5 · c. 1320–1370Can a rhythm repeat while its notes change?Time can be counted twice
  6. 6 · c. 1400–1700Why do different instruments sustain notes differently?Wood, gut, wind and wire
  7. 7 · c. 1430–1600How can several voices each tell a story?Each voice has a sentence
  8. 8 · c. 1570–1607How did singing become a way to act?The words take command
  9. 9 · c. 1600–1722How can a bass line guide everyone else?The bass becomes a working plan

Notation examples use synthesised sound and distinguish quotations from teaching schematics. Real performances appear where the collection has a relevant passage; the historical path also uses clearly labelled later comparisons.
