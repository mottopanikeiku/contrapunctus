How can a tune remember a character?

Leitmotif: recognition with a changed meaning

The idea in detail

A leitmotif is a recurring musical idea associated with a dramatic person, object, action or concept. The music can recall that association independently of spoken words. In Wagner’s Ring, the orchestra can recall a promise that a character conceals, anticipate an object before it becomes visible, or combine motives whose dramatic meanings are in conflict. A short interval pattern may carry the identity; rhythm, register and instrumentation help the listener recognise it under changing harmonies.

Names as listening aidsMany familiar motive labels were circulated by commentators, notably Hans von Wolzogen. A label identifies a first association, leaving later uses open.

The sword Nothung gives a concrete example of musical memory extending beyond a single scene. Its associations pass through Das Rheingold, Die Walküre and Siegfried, even though the sword’s dramatic condition changes from promise to possession, fragments and reforging. The orchestra can connect those states before a spectator consciously identifies the notes. A change of harmony or a collision with another motive can enrich the association.

The technique has several predecessors. Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique (1830) carries an idée fixe, associated with the beloved, through five movements; in the finale its appearance on E♭ clarinet changes both register and character. Liszt’s thematic transformation similarly preserves a theme’s identity while changing its tempo, metre or harmonic setting. Recurrence, transformation and dramatic association overlap while remaining distinct procedures.

Three kinds of changeTransposition moves a pattern to another pitch level. Augmentation lengthens its durations proportionally. Reharmonisation changes the supporting chords without necessarily changing the melody. Each can preserve different identifying features.

The example uses an invented C–E–G cell. Lowering E changes its mode while preserving the contour; halving the durations changes its pace; doubling them makes the same proportions extend across a barline. A real leitmotif may survive less literal transformations, particularly when an orchestral colour or dramatic context supplies the missing clue. To follow one through an opera, compare its bass and inner voices as well as its melody. A familiar tune above a new bass can disclose a changed allegiance even when its surface rhythm remains intact.

Objects and sources

Shared context

Rackham’s illustration shows Mime at work in the smithy of Siegfried, Act I. The scene belongs to an opera in which the sword’s musical history precedes its successful reforging: the audience has already heard its associations in earlier parts of the Ring. A picture can show the workshop and the broken metal at once; the score can place the sword’s remembered past against the present action.
