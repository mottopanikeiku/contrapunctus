Prelude

Introduction

The Concert Hall plays historical and openly licensed recordings, from plainchant to Boulez’s Le Marteau sans maître, in the order the composers were born. Short notes appear as the music reaches them, and where a score is available, the page moves with the sound. Each of the evenings below can also be played straight through without choosing individual pieces.

Each composer has a chapter. It gives the life, in the composer’s own words where they survive; what the music is made of; the works to start with; short examples to play; and the paintings and poems associated with the music. Every piece in the Hall has a page of its own, with all its recordings and all its notes.

CopyrightRecordings first published in 1963 or later are still protected. The Hall therefore has nothing by Ligeti, Reich and several other recent composers, and its Messiaen, Britten and Boulez come from records issued before 1963. The chapters point to the labels’ and artists’ own recordings.

The chapters overlap, as the periods of music do. In 1909 Rachmaninoff wrote his Third Piano Concerto for his first American tour, and Schoenberg wrote his Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11, which have no home key. The Web of Influence and the Map follow what passed between composers, such as a copied score, a borrowed tune, or two composers in the same city in the same year.

Where a story is legend, the text says whose legend it is. The sources are given on every page.

A first evening

Bach to Ravel in seventy minutes

A starting point for listeners new to the repertoire. Bach’s aria, a Vivaldi spring and Handel’s chorus come first, followed by the great sonata symphonies, a Viennese winter song, a Chopin nocturne and Paris at the turn of the twentieth century. The last piece is built on a single drum rhythm, played 169 times.

Paris, 1888–1913

From Satie’s Gymnopédies to The Rite of Spring

The programme covers twenty-five years in Paris. It includes Satie at the piano in Montmartre, Debussy accompanying Mary Garden, the flute that opened the Faune, Ravel’s harp, the drowned cathedral, and two Stravinsky ballets that the Ballets Russes brought to Paris.

Vienna, from Haydn to Schoenberg

A hundred and twenty years of music in one city

Haydn’s surprise, Mozart’s G minor, the symphony Schubert never finished, the hurdy-gurdy man at the end of Winterreise, Brahms in C minor, Mahler’s Adagietto, and Schoenberg’s night walk and his step into “the air of another planet”.

Composers as performers

Grieg, Debussy, Rachmaninoff, Elgar, Ravel and Stravinsky performing their own music

Every recording here has the composer at the piano or on the podium, from Grieg in Paris in 1903 to Stravinsky in 1960. Each plays at his own tempos and departs from his own written markings where he chooses.

Slow music for late evening

Bach’s alto weeping with Peter, the slow movement of Mozart’s last concerto, the adagio of Schubert’s Quintet, a nocturne, a dream, moonlight, the last page of Fauré’s Requiem and Mahler’s Adagietto.

The first recordings

Grieg to Elgar, 1903–1928

Musicians playing and singing into a recording horn, and from 1925 into a microphone. Grieg, Caruso, Nikisch’s Berlin Philharmonic, Paderewski, Chaliapin, de Pachmann, and Elgar conducting his own march.

Opera

Rossini to Puccini

The programme includes Figaro’s entrance in Seville; the chorus of the Hebrew slaves, which Toscanini conducted when Verdi was buried in 1901; Carmen’s two principal songs; the clown’s aria associated with Caruso; and Tosca’s prayer. The orchestra plays Rossini’s overture and Wagner’s Valkyries on its own.

Music in America

Dvořák in New York to Copland’s Lincoln

Dvořák wrote his Largo in New York in 1893 and told American composers to turn to their own folk songs. The programme continues with three American composers: Ives’s music on the Alcotts’ house in Concord; Gershwin’s Rhapsody in the 1924 recording by Gershwin and Paul Whiteman’s band; and Copland’s fanfare and his portrait of Lincoln, with Carl Sandburg reading Lincoln’s words.

Chapters

Bach to the present in seven chapters

Each chapter opens with a painting from its own years and an account of the changes in harmony, in form and in the instruments themselves.

I1600–1750

The Baroque

A harpsichordist reads a bass line and supplies the chords while a singer tells the story above it. In Vivaldi’s concertos the solo violin breaks away from the orchestra; in Bach’s fugues a tune enters in one voice after another.

II1750–1827

Classical

A melody’s first phrase stops on an unsettled chord, and its answer comes to rest in the home key. Haydn could place a joke at the expected ending, and Mozart gave the piano a singing part among the orchestral winds. In Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony, three trombones enter for the first time in the last movement, having been silent in the movements before it.

III1815–1890

Romanticism

In Schubert’s Erlkönig, one singer takes the parts of a father and his terrified child while the piano plays the horse’s gallop throughout. In Berlioz’s orchestral writing a melody carries a story and returns in a grotesque version. In her piano cycle, Fanny Hensel wrote a piece for each month of the year.

The French Line

VI1908–1945

Modernism

Schoenberg took a soprano beyond the home key; Stravinsky had one repeated chord accented at unexpected moments. Bartók brought village songs home on phonograph cylinders, and Ives set a trumpet’s question over quiet strings. Between the wars, new music acquired its own concert societies and radio orchestras. In 1933 Schoenberg had to leave Germany.

VII1945–present

After 1945

Two pianists repeat a phrase until one moves ahead of the other. A boy’s recorded voice is multiplied through loudspeakers. After 1945 composers worked in the studio as well as at the piano and on manuscript paper. Britten set an opera in a Suffolk fishing village; Saariaho derived chords from the changing sound of a cello. In 1997 Adès wrote a piece in which a symphony orchestra plays the rhythms of a rave.

Lifelines

Composers’ lifespans

Every composer in the atlas on one axis. Bach died the year Haydn turned eighteen; Brahms heard the thirty-year-old Mahler conduct Don Giovanni in Budapest in 1890 and praised it; Rachmaninoff outlived Ravel and Mahler and died two years before Bartók.

Before Bach

Origins

Guido of Arezzo took a book of chants to Rome and taught a pope to sing a tune he had never heard. Six centuries later, Monteverdi gave Orpheus a song to charm the keeper of the underworld. In the centuries between, musicians learned to write rhythm down, to set a second voice against a melody and to write a bass line that left room for a player’s invention.

From the monochord to figured bass
Technique

The Workshop

In a Bach fugue a second voice enters while the first continues. In Ravel’s Boléro the same tune changes in character when the clarinet takes it over from the flute. Eighteen lessons examine details of this kind, using passages from real scores and short, playable examples.
