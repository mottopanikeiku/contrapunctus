Prelude

How to use this atlas

Western art music is a written tradition. A Bach fugue, a Mahler adagio and a Ligeti étude survive because someone wrote them down in a notation precise enough that a stranger, centuries later, can make them sound again. This atlas leans on that fact. Each composer’s page says what the music is made of — which chords, which forms, which instruments in which registers — and shows short examples engraved in the browser, which you can play.

Reading the examplesNotation is rendered from text. Press Play on an example to hear its synthesised pitches and rhythms while the notes light up. Captions distinguish quotations from schematics and identify any reduction or transposition.

The chapters run from the late Baroque to the present in seven movements. They overlap, because styles do: Rachmaninoff was writing lush tonal concertos in the same decade that Schoenberg was abandoning the key signature, and Ravel and Prokofiev were both, in their different ways, neoclassicists looking back at the eighteenth century while being thoroughly modern about it.

Alongside the music sit the other arts — the painting Debussy put on the cover of La Mer, the woodcut behind the funeral march in Mahler’s First, the Böcklin canvas Rachmaninoff saw in black and white and turned into a tone poem. These are not decorations. Composers read, looked at pictures and argued with painters, and many of their works began as a response to something they saw or read.

Where to startIf you know nothing, begin with Origins: from Pythagoras’s monochord to the figured bass Bach inherited. If you know the repertoire but not the theory, The Workshop explains counterpoint, sonata form, the Tristan chord, the octatonic scale and the twelve-tone row with examples.

Every page ends with recordings worth finding, and a lineage: who the composer learned from, and whom they taught or provoked. Follow those threads and the three centuries become one long conversation.

Four portraits

Mahler, Rachmaninoff, Ravel, Prokofiev

Four composers born within thirty-one years of one another, given extended portraits here. A symphonist who wanted the symphony to contain the world; a pianist whose melodies grew out of Orthodox chant and church bells; a precisionist who called himself “artificial by nature”; and a modernist who never stopped writing tunes.

1860–1911

Gustav Mahler

“The symphony must be like the world. It must embrace everything.”
1873–1943

Sergei Rachmaninoff

“I feel like a ghost wandering in a world grown alien. I cannot cast out the old way of writing, and I cannot acquire the new.”
1875–1937

Maurice Ravel

“Does it never occur to these people that I may be artificial by nature?”
1891–1953

Sergei Prokofiev

“It seemed to me that if Haydn had lived in our day he would have retained his own style while accepting something of the new at the same time.”
Seven movements

Three centuries in seven movements

Each chapter opens with a painting from its own years and a short account of what changed in the music — in harmony, in form, in the instruments themselves.

I1600–1750

The Baroque

A bass line with numbers over it — the basso continuo — holds everything up. Above it, voices move in imitation, keys are chosen for their character, and a single mood (an affect) governs a whole movement. The fugue reaches its summit in Bach; opera and the concerto spread from Italy across Europe.

  • Vivaldi
  • Rameau

II1750–1827

Classical

The continuo falls away and melody is set over a lighter accompaniment. Music is now built in balanced phrases and organised by the tension between two keys: the sonata principle, which shapes symphonies, quartets and concertos. Haydn invents its procedures, Mozart makes them sing, Beethoven makes them fight.

  • Bach
  • Haydn
  • Mozart
  • Beethoven

III1815–1890

Romanticism

The composer becomes a hero and the listener a confidant. Harmony grows chromatic, forms stretch to hold a programme or a poem, the piano becomes an orchestra under ten fingers, and national idioms — Polish, Bohemian, Russian — enter the concert hall. From Schubert’s songs to Wagner’s music drama.

IV1880–1918

Fin de siècle

Orchestras of a hundred players and more, symphonies of ninety minutes, harmony stretched to the edge of tonality. Mahler wants the symphony to contain the world; Strauss can make muted, flutter-tongued brass bleat like a flock of sheep; Rachmaninoff builds melodies from chant and bells; Sibelius goes the other way, toward compression and silence.

V1870–1937

The French Line

Chords heard as colour, scales drawn from chant, from Java and from the whole-tone collection; orchestration as a precise art of timbre. Fauré refines, Debussy dissolves, Satie subtracts, and Ravel engineers.

  • Fauré
  • Debussy

VI1908–1945

Modernism

In 1908 Schoenberg writes music without a tonal centre; in 1913 Stravinsky’s Rite of Spring makes rhythm the main event. Then come twelve-tone rows, folk music recorded on wax cylinders by Bartók, neoclassicism, and composers like Prokofiev and Shostakovich writing under the eye of the state.

  • Ives
  • Schoenberg
  • Bartók
  • Stravinsky
  • Webern
  • Berg
  • Prokofiev✦
  • Shostakovich

VII1945–present

After 1945

After the war: serialism extended to every parameter, then Ligeti’s clouds of sound, Feldman’s long quiet, Reich’s pulse and phase, Pärt’s bells, and the spectral music of Saariaho, built from the physics of a single tone. Many styles, many workshops.

Lifelines

Who overlapped with whom

Every composer in the atlas on one axis. Bach died the year Haydn turned eighteen; Brahms heard the thirty-year-old Mahler conduct Don Giovanni in Budapest in 1890 and praised it; Rachmaninoff outlived Ravel and Mahler and died two years before Bartók.

Before Bach

Origins

A vibrating string, a remembered chant, a second voice: the techniques behind Bach’s music were made over two thousand years. Their history runs through Greek mathematics, monastic classrooms, Parisian choir stalls, Italian workshops and the first opera theatres.
