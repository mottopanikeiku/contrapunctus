Prelude

Before you begin

Start with the music. The Concert Hall plays real recordings, from plainchant to Shostakovich’s Fifth Symphony, in the order the composers were born. Short notes appear as the music reaches them, and where we have the score, the page moves with the sound. If you would rather not choose, pick one of the evenings below and let it play.

Then read. Each composer has a chapter: the life, told in their own words where we have them; what the music is made of; the works to start with; short examples you can play; and the paintings and poems that kept it company. Every piece in the Hall has a page of its own, with all its recordings and all its notes.

Where the music stopsMost recordings made since the 1960s are still under copyright, so the Hall cannot play Messiaen, Ligeti or Reich. Their chapters point to the labels’ and artists’ own recordings instead.

The chapters overlap, because music does. In 1909 Rachmaninoff wrote his Third Piano Concerto for his first American tour, and Schoenberg wrote his Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11, with no home key at all. The Web of Influence and the Map follow what passed between composers: a copied score, a borrowed tune, two of them in the same city in the same year.

Where a story is legend, the text says whose legend it is. The sources are on every page.

Rossini to Puccini, overture to last act

The New World

Dvořák in New York to Copland’s Lincoln

Dvořák writes his Largo in New York in 1893 and tells American composers to turn to their own folk songs. Then three American voices: Ives remembering the Alcotts’ house in Concord, Gershwin’s Rhapsody as he and Paul Whiteman’s band recorded it in 1924, and Copland’s fanfare and his portrait of Lincoln, with Carl Sandburg reading Lincoln’s words.

Each chapter opens with a painting from its own years and an account of what changed in the music: in harmony, in form, in the instruments themselves.

I1600–1750

The Baroque

A harpsichordist reads a bass line and supplies the chords while a singer tells the story above it. In Vivaldi’s concertos the solo violin breaks away from the orchestra; in Bach’s fugues a tune enters one voice after another, until the keyboard seems full of people talking.

  • Vivaldi
  • Rameau
  • J. S. Bach

II1750–1827

Classical

A melody’s first phrase stops on an unsettled chord; its answer comes to rest in the home key. Haydn can spring a joke at the expected ending, while Mozart gives the piano a singing part among the orchestral winds. In Beethoven’s Fifth Symphony, the last movement arrives with three trombones that have waited through everything before it.

  • C. P. E. Bach
  • Haydn
  • Mozart

III1815–1890

Romanticism

In Schubert’s Erlkönig, one singer becomes a father and his terrified child while the piano keeps the horse galloping. A melody can carry a story through Berlioz’s orchestra, returning as a grotesque version of itself. At the piano, Fanny Hensel gives each month of the year a piece of its own.

Fin de siècle

V1870–1937

The French Line

A single flute starts Debussy’s Faune; a bell tolls through Ravel’s Le Gibet. Paris makes room for both, alongside Fauré’s songs and Satie’s slow piano dances. Even rival harp manufacturers have a hand in the new music.

VI1908–1945

Modernism

Schoenberg carries a soprano beyond the home key; Stravinsky makes one repeated chord kick at unexpected moments. Bartók brings village songs home on phonograph cylinders, while Ives leaves a trumpet’s question hanging over quiet strings. Between the wars, new music acquires its own concert societies and radio orchestras. In 1933, Schoenberg has to leave Germany.

VII1945–present

After 1945

Two pianists repeat a phrase until one edges ahead. A boy’s recorded voice multiplies through loudspeakers. After 1945, the studio becomes a place to compose, alongside the piano and manuscript paper. Britten fills the opera house with a Suffolk fishing village; Saariaho finds chords inside a cello’s changing sound. By 1997, Adès has a symphony orchestra pounding out the rhythms of a rave.

Lifelines

Who overlapped with whom

Every composer in the atlas on one axis. Bach died the year Haydn turned eighteen; Brahms heard the thirty-year-old Mahler conduct Don Giovanni in Budapest in 1890 and praised it; Rachmaninoff outlived Ravel and Mahler and died two years before Bartók.

Before Bach

Origins

Guido of Arezzo took a book of chants to Rome and taught a pope to sing a tune he had never heard. Six centuries later, Monteverdi gave Orpheus a song to charm the keeper of the underworld. The intervening years filled the page with new possibilities: a rhythm that could be written down, a melody answered by another voice, a bass line that left room for a player’s invention.
