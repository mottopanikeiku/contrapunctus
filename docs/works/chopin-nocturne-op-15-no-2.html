Frédéric Chopin / III. Romanticism

Nocturne, Op. 15 No. 2

1830–33 · Piano

3 min

The recording

  1. LarghettoLuke Faulkner
    3:24

    Luke Faulkner plays a nocturne whose F♯-major song grows increasingly extravagant, then breaks into a quick, murmuring middle. Even the return has fresh ornaments to spare.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A small upward turn

      The melody begins with A♯ nudging up to B, then falls through the notes of a C♯ chord. A deep C♯ in the left hand keeps the phrase waiting; F♯ arrives in the next bar. The little turn already sounds like someone taking breath to sing.

    2. F♯ turns minor

      The opening song has a troubled companion in F♯ minor. Its phrases climb high, then spill down in little runs marked dolcissimo, very sweetly. The bass keeps moving beneath those long upper notes.

    3. Twice as fast

      Doppio movimento: twice the motion, with the piano still speaking sotto voce. A dotted melody rides above quick notes in the same hand while the bass jumps below. The storm starts as a murmur.

    4. A breath before the song

      The quick figures slow and sink towards the middle of the keyboard. A pause makes room for the opening A♯–B turn. The Larghetto song returns, with new curls of small notes woven into the familiar phrase.

    5. Piano and film music

      Faulkner’s training takes him from piano and composition at Chetham’s to music at Oxford, then screen composition at Edinburgh. He graduates there in 2014, having studied recording and engineering as well as writing music for film.

    How it is joined

    1. C♯ beneath the quickened melody

      Repeated A♯s hold up the end of the slow section. The bass then moves to C♯, with E♯, G♯ and B above it: a dominant seventh. Chopin marks the new passage doppio movimento and sotto voce. The right hand now has a dotted tune and quick notes beneath it; the left hand makes wide jumps.

    2. A pause, then A♯–B

      Over a low C♯, the repeated figures slow and die away: molto rallentando, smorzando. The bass falls silent, and a pause separates the last figure from the opening A♯–B upbeat. Marked a Tempo, the melody returns over C♯ and reaches the F♯ bass in the following bar.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. LarghettoIgnacy Jan Paderewski · 14 May 1917, New York
    3:28

    Paderewski records the F♯-major Nocturne in New York in May 1917. His piano belongs to an age of rolled chords and hands that can speak at different moments.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A melody with elbows

      Chopin’s melody curls through quick ornaments while the left hand places bass notes and chords beneath it. Paderewski’s playing often separates the hands’ attacks and spreads chords, giving the tune room to lean ahead of its accompaniment or linger behind.

    2. New York, May 1917

      On 14 May 1917 Paderewski plays this nocturne for Victor in New York. The company issues the first take. A few weeks later he returns to record Chopin’s G♭-major Butterfly Étude.

    3. Quickened, still under the breath

      The central passage starts sotto voce, its dotted tune threaded through quick accompanying notes. Chopin asks for twice the motion. The left hand’s leaps and the insistent dotted patterns gradually turn the murmur into a struggle.

    4. The opening turn returns

      The repeated figures lose speed and sink lower. A pause, then A♯ rises to B: the little turn that begins the nocturne. The returning song keeps its old outline while Chopin fits new ornaments into it.

    5. “What is lost is lost”

      In his 1909 essay on rubato, Paderewski laughs at the rule that time borrowed from one phrase must be paid back in another. “What is lost is lost,” he writes. “For any lawlessness there is, after a certain term, proscription.”

    How it is joined

    1. The bass turns to C♯

      The slow section lingers on repeated A♯s. A C♯ bass starts the middle, supporting a dominant seventh with E♯, G♯ and B. Doppio movimento doubles the motion, and the right hand divides its work between a dotted tune and quick notes underneath. The opening of the passage is marked sotto voce.

    2. The quick figures die away

      Chopin writes molto rallentando, smorzando over repeated figures above a low C♯. The left hand then falls silent. After a pause, A♯ and B introduce the opening tune at its first tempo; the bass moves from C♯ to F♯ beneath the restored melody.

  2. LarghettoEugen d’Albert · 1916
    3:07

    Eugen d’Albert, a pupil of Liszt, records Chopin’s F♯-major Nocturne in 1916. This is the pianist whose singing tone leaves Wilhelm Kempff remembering a hand poised over a single black key.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The singing black key

      A♯ begins the melody, with a flick up to B before the line descends. The left hand travels from a low bass note to chords near the tune. Chopin marks the opening sostenuto: the little notes belong to one sustained phrase.

    2. Liszt’s room in Weimar

      In 1882 d’Albert goes to Weimar to study with Liszt. By July 1884 Tchaikovsky is writing to Nadezhda von Meck about the young pianist he has heard in Moscow: “the legitimate successor of Rubinstein”.

    3. A murmur starts running

      The middle doubles the motion. Inside the right hand, quick notes run beneath a dotted melody; the left hand leaps between widely spaced notes. Sotto voce keeps the beginning hushed before the music gathers force.

    4. The piano takes breath

      Chopin slows the repeated figures and lets them die away. After a pause, the familiar A♯–B turn comes back in the opening tempo. There is room again for the melody’s long breaths and tiny flourishes.

    5. Kempff watches the hand

      In his memoir, Wilhelm Kempff remembers d’Albert’s hand hovering over an A♯. The fading note seems to him to tremble like a human voice. When the passage comes round again, Kempff hears a new sound from the same black key.

    How it is joined

    1. A new tune over C♯

      The lingering A♯s at the end of the slow section lead into a C♯ bass. E♯, G♯ and B fill out a dominant seventh above it. The marking changes to doppio movimento, with sotto voce beneath the right hand: a dotted melody and quick accompanying notes now share that hand.

    2. The bass leaves a silence

      A deep C♯ supports the last repeated figures, marked molto rallentando, smorzando. Then the left hand stops. A pause precedes the A♯–B upbeat of the returning melody; C♯ reappears in the bass, followed by F♯ in the next bar. The first tempo is restored.

  3. Nocturne in F♯ major, Op. 15 No. 2Ignacy Jan Paderewski, piano · 30 January 1937, London
    3:42

    Twenty years after his New York recording, Paderewski returns to this nocturne in London. The F♯-major song and its restless middle are still in his hands in January 1937, just before his appearance on cinema screens in Moonlight Sonata.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A bass below the singer

      The left hand makes a small circuit: low bass, higher note, chord, higher note again. Above it, the right hand’s melody pauses on long notes and slips into quick flourishes. The opening marking is sostenuto, sustained.

    2. Two jobs for one hand

      The right hand now carries a dotted melody and a busy line underneath it. The left hand jumps below both. Doppio movimento doubles the pace; sotto voce asks for a subdued beginning.

    3. Back to the Larghetto

      The quick figures slow down and dwindle. After a pause, the opening tune resumes its old pace. Chopin writes fresh ornaments into the reprise, then sends small notes tumbling towards the final F♯.

    4. Paderewski on the screen

      In February 1937, Moonlight Sonata brings Paderewski into British cinemas. He plays himself in a romance involving a forced landing and a Swedish baroness. The camera also stays with him at the piano for Beethoven’s slow movement and his own Minuet.

    5. The last small notes

      Groups of tiny notes run down through the F♯ harmony. Chopin writes smorzando — dying away — over the final descent. The bass settles on a low F♯, with A♯ left above it.

  4. Nocturne in F♯ major, Op. 15 No. 2Patrizia Prati, piano · 24 November 2015, Madrid
    3:39

    Patrizia Prati plays Chopin’s F♯-major Nocturne in the empty rooms of Madrid’s Museum of Romanticism. Its poised opening melody has a quick, murmuring double life in the middle.

    Notes as it plays
    1. An upbeat takes flight

      Two quick notes, A♯ and B, set the melody moving. The phrase falls, rests on repeated C♯s, then sends a flourish upwards. Chopin keeps adding new little runs to a tune that remains easy to recognise.

    2. Chopin in a Madrid museum

      Prati records at the Museum of Romanticism on 24 November 2015, without an audience: a first concert there had been ruined by coughing and mobile phones. She gives the recordings to Wikimedia Spain, twelve Chopin nocturnes among them.

    3. Dotted notes over C♯

      The quick middle begins over a C♯ bass. The right hand is suddenly crowded: a dotted melody above a running accompaniment, both played sotto voce. The bass makes wide jumps while the tune grows more insistent.

    4. A familiar upbeat

      The middle ends in repeated figures that slow and die away. A pause separates them from the returning A♯–B upbeat. The song comes back in the first tempo, still growing fresh ornaments.

    5. A quiet tumble home

      The closing flourishes tumble down through the notes of F♯ major. Smorzando asks the piano to die away. At the bottom of all that decoration sits the last low F♯.
