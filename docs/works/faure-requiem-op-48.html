Gabriel Fauré / V. The French Line

Requiem, Op. 48

1887–1900 · Op. 48 · Soprano and baritone soloists, chorus, organ and orchestra; several versions

First performed at the Madeleine in 1888, then enlarged in stages. Low strings and organ accompany the prayers; In paradisum ends the work in D major.

7 recordings · 35 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Introït et KyrieChorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    6:23

    Louis Frémaux conducts the Introït et Kyrie, the first movement of the Requiem, Op. 48. A single D sounds before the choir enters, and the movement is built from low strings, long-held chords and a tenor melody that returns as the Kyrie.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening D and choral entry

      Bassoons, horns and low strings play D fortissimo, then fade. The choir enters pianissimo in six parts on a D minor chord. The violins and organ rest.

    2. Origins of the Requiem

      Fauré’s reasons for writing a Requiem are uncertain. His father died in 1885 and his mother on New Year’s Eve 1887, but he had begun the work before she died. In 1902 he wrote to Louis Aguettant that it was composed “for nothing … for pleasure, if I may say so”.

    3. First performance, the Madeleine, 1888

      The first performance was given at a funeral service at the Madeleine in Paris. On 16 January 1888 Fauré conducted five movements at the funeral of Joseph Lesoufaché, an architect. The Offertoire and the Libera me were added later.

    4. Andante moderato, tenor melody

      The marking changes to Andante moderato. Violas and cellos begin to move, and the tenors sing a falling melody over them. The organ enters on soft foundation stops while the horns and bassoons rest.

    5. Wind chord before the sopranos

      Bassoons, horns and trumpets play a brief, strong chord before the soprano prayer. The organ adds reed stops, then returns to its foundation stops. The sopranos sing “Te decet hymnus” over moving violas and cellos.

    6. “Exaudi”, full choir

      At “Exaudi” the whole choir sings fortissimo, and the next phrase is immediately softer. Fauré continues to alternate loud and soft phrases while the violas and cellos keep moving beneath the voices.

    7. Kyrie, return of the tenor melody

      The Kyrie takes up the tenors’ earlier melody. Sopranos, altos and tenors sing it in unison before the writing divides into four parts. The bass line continues to move underneath.

    8. “Christe”, choir and organ

      The first “Christe” is fortissimo, the next piano. The choir repeats this alternation above moving violas and cellos. The organ’s reed stops are added for the louder phrase.

    9. Final Kyrie

      The choir returns to “Kyrie eleison” in short, quiet phrases. Cellos and basses continue beneath. Towards the end a bassoon and a horn play soft solo lines, and the movement closes on a chord of D minor.

    10. The priest at the Madeleine, 1888

      According to a story often retold, after the first performance at the Madeleine in January 1888 the priest called Fauré over and told him, “We have no need of all these novelties; the Madeleine’s repertoire is quite rich enough.”

    Joins

    1. Fermata to the <i>Andante moderato</i>

      A fermata holds the choir’s last chord. At the Andante moderato the tempo quickens and the D minor music continues with violas and cellos in flowing lines. The bass starts moving, and the organ enters on eight-foot foundation stops. The tenors take the melody alone, marked dolce espressivo.

    2. Wind chords before “Te decet hymnus”

      Bassoons, horns and trumpets return with strong chords over the low strings. The organ briefly adds reed stops, then goes back to its quieter foundation stops at rehearsal E. The strings make a diminuendo, and the sopranos enter with “Te decet hymnus”.

    3. Full choir enters at rehearsal F

      The strings crescendo beneath the end of the soprano prayer. At rehearsal F the full choir sings “Exaudi” fortissimo over a D minor chord. Violas, cellos and basses follow with forceful rising figures, and the next choral entry drops at once to piano.

    4. Tenor melody returns as the Kyrie

      The full choir makes a diminuendo at letter G and the wind chords fade. Sopranos, altos and tenors begin the Kyrie in unison on the earlier tenor melody, while the bass holds D beneath them. Violas and cellos resume their flowing accompaniment. The vocal marking is dolce espressivo.

    5. Kyrie into the first Christe

      A crescendo in the Kyrie leads to the first “Christe”, marked fortissimo. The organ adds reeds to its foundation stops (Anches et fonds), and the lower strings rise beneath the choir. A piano repetition follows at once, then a second fortissimo “Christe”, with the calls separated from one another.

    6. From the “Christe” into the final Kyrie

      The “Christe” calls diminish into the final Kyrie. The choir sings softly above moving cellos and basses, and the organ returns to foundation stops. The phrases in the lower strings continue up to the final D minor chord, marked pianissimo.

  2. II. OffertoireBernard Kruysen, baritone; Chorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    7:40

    The Offertoire, the second movement of Fauré’s Requiem, Op. 48, opens with altos and tenors in close imitation in B minor, and Bernard Kruysen sings the baritone solo Hostias. The movement reaches B major only on the last “Amen”.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, violas and cellos

      The violas and cellos rise slowly above the organ and grow louder through the prelude. The marking is Adagio molto. Fauré left the violins, winds and harp out of this movement, so the choir sings with these lower instruments.

    2. Canon for altos and tenors

      Altos and tenors sing “O Domine Jesu Christe” in close canon. The narrow B minor melody begins pianissimo, marked dolcissimo. Each new statement starts a step higher, and the basses join the later entries.

    3. Hostias and Libera me, 1889–1893

      Fauré completed the baritone’s Hostias in the spring of 1889. The Libera me, written for voice and organ in 1877, was added to the Requiem in 1891. On 21 January 1893 Fauré conducted the seven-movement work at the Madeleine, where he was choirmaster.

    4. Fauré’s changes to the Offertory text

      The prayer opens “O Domine Jesu Christe”. The liturgical Offertory begins at “Domine”, so the “O” is Fauré’s addition. He also shortened the next verse and left out the third verse entirely.

    5. Interview with Louis Aguettant, 1902

      In 1902 Fauré told the interviewer Louis Aguettant that, perhaps by instinct, he had wanted to escape the conventional: “for so long I have been playing the organ at funeral services! I have had it up to here. I wanted to do something else.”

    6. Baritone solo, “Hostias”

      The baritone begins “Hostias et preces” (offerings and prayers) on a repeated note. The tempo changes to Andante moderato, in three beats to the bar. Quiet quavers in the violas and cellos accompany him, and the choir rests.

    7. The baritone, Bernard Kruysen

      Bernard Kruysen was a Dutch baritone, born in Montreux in 1933, who studied in Paris with Pierre Bernac. He became one of the leading interpreters of French song. Francis Poulenc accompanied him in recital.

    8. Organ solo before “fac eas”

      The strings stop, and the organ plays a short solo between the baritone’s phrases. The baritone returns with “fac eas, Domine, de morte transire ad vitam” (let them pass from death to life), and his melody now moves more freely.

    9. Return of the opening tempo

      The opening tempo returns. All four choral parts now take up the canon, including the sopranos, who were silent at the start. Their entries overlap above the organ, and the strings play little under the voices.

    10. Letter to René Fauchois, 1921

      In 1921 Fauré wrote to René Fauchois that all the religious illusion he had ever had went into his Requiem, which is “dominated from one end to the other by a very human feeling: confidence in eternal rest”.

    11. Final Amen in B major

      Fauré added “Amen” to the Offertory text. A D♯ turns the closing harmony to B major, and the voices exchange the word. The strings play a last chord, and the choir and organ finish alone.

    Joins

    1. Viola and cello prelude to choral canon

      The rising prelude for violas and cellos makes a diminuendo. Altos and tenors then begin the canon in B minor, pianissimo and marked dolcissimo, with organ accompaniment. The melody passes between the two choral parts at close succession.

    2. Baritone enters on one note

      The choir’s short phrases at the end of the canon break off into rests. The baritone begins “Hostias” as the score changes to 3/4 and Andante moderato. The harmony beneath him is D major. The violas and cellos repeat quiet quavers, and the organ is marked dolce.

    3. Organ solo at rehearsal F

      The strings release their chord at rehearsal F and the organ continues alone, marked Solo. Its upper line rises over a moving bass. The baritone returns on “fac eas” as the cellos rejoin, pianissimo. The triple metre continues unchanged.

    4. The slow canon returns

      The baritone finishes his solo, with its promise of life. I° Tempo, adagio molto restores common time and B minor, and the choir sings “O Domine” again in overlapping entries. After one chord the strings rest, and the organ alone accompanies the four voices.

    5. B minor to B major at “Amen”

      The final petition leads into “Amen”. The key signature changes from two sharps to five, and D♯ replaces D♮. The strings give the new B major chord one quiet stroke, then stop. Choir and organ continue the imitations to the final chord.

  3. III. SanctusChorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    3:10

    The Sanctus is the third movement of Fauré’s Requiem, Op. 48. The sopranos sing a three-note “Sanctus” over harp and divided violas, the brass play only in a single “Hosanna”, and the movement ends as quietly as it began.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening of the Sanctus

      The harp plays rising E♭ major arpeggios while two viola parts move around each other. The sopranos sing “Sanctus” on three notes, and the men reply. The organ holds the harmony.

    2. First performance, Paris, 1900

      Paul Taffanel conducted the expanded Requiem at the Trocadéro in Paris on 12 July 1900, with the Société des concerts du Conservatoire. Before the score was printed, Fauré’s publisher, Julien Hamelle, had advised him to prepare a version for full symphony orchestra.

    3. Violin section, 1900 version

      The church orchestra of 1893 had no violin section. The concert version of 1900 added one. In the Sanctus the first and second violins, muted, play a flowing line above the harp’s arpeggios.

    4. Hosanna, brass fanfare

      The sopranos begin the “Hosanna” softly. As the phrase grows, the harp changes from running notes to chords, and horns and trumpets play a fanfare. Above them the men’s voices sing “Hosanna in excelsis”.

    5. Return of the harp arpeggios

      After the brass fanfare comes a diminuendo. The harp arpeggios return, the violas resume their running figures and the choir sings “Sanctus” again, pianissimo. The altos, who have not sung until now, join for the closing prayer.

    Joins

    1. Longer choral phrases at “Dominus Deus Sabaoth”

      The harp and divided violas continue their flowing figures. Above them, at “Dominus Deus Sabaoth”, the sopranos move beyond the short three-note phrase of “Sanctus”. The men answer the longer line, and the organ sustains beneath both groups.

    2. Sopranos over divided violins

      The men rest while the sopranos sing “Pleni sunt coeli et terra”, marked sempre dolce. Divided violins play their line above the harp, and the cellos hold sustained notes arco. The men return at “gloria tua”.

    3. Running notes become a fanfare

      From rehearsal D the sopranos begin “Hosanna” softly over the continuing viola figures. The harp and strings then change to repeated chords, and the bass beat grows firmer. Horns and trumpets enter forte, and the men sing their “Hosanna” above this fanfare.

    4. Brass diminuendo and return to E♭

      The sopranos’ answering phrase diminishes, and the brass drop to pianissimo. The harp resumes its broken E♭ major chords, and the lower strings change from pizzicato to arco. Over the organ’s sustained harmony the choir sings “Sanctus” once more.

  4. IV. Pie JesuDenis Thilliez, boy soprano; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    3:01

    The boy soprano Denis Thilliez sings the Pie Jesu, the central prayer of the Requiem, Op. 48, which begins with the organ alone. The melody rises higher on its second statement and ends on two alternating notes.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Soprano solo with organ

      The boy soprano begins in B♭ major, marked dolce e tranquille (sweetly and calmly), accompanied by the organ alone. The other instruments rest through the opening prayer, “Pie Jesu Domine, dona eis requiem”: merciful Lord Jesus, grant them rest.

    2. Boys’ voices at the Madeleine

      Women could not sing in the choir at the Madeleine, where Fauré worked. Boys took the upper parts and the Pie Jesu solo. For the concert hall Fauré preferred women in these lines, for their longer breath and adult voices.

    3. Two-note phrase, “sempiternam requiem”

      At “Dona eis” the melody widens. At “sempiternam requiem” (everlasting rest) it narrows to two alternating notes, while muted violas and cellos keep moving beneath the voice. The organ part is marked sempre legato.

    4. Return of the opening melody

      The opening melody returns with woodwind solos around it, marked soft. Its last phrase falls lower than before. At the close the harp plays, the tempo slows (poco rit.), and the voice rises on the final “requiem”.

    5. Sleeve note by Louis Aubert

      Louis Aubert, who signed the sleeve note of this recording, recalled singing the Requiem’s solo soprano part at the Madeleine as a boy of twelve, some months after the first performance. On this recording the solo is sung by Denis Thilliez, of the Neuilly choir school.

    Joins

    1. The orchestral chords and second call

      Muted strings, harp and woodwinds follow the first prayer with soft chords. They then stop, and the soprano and organ begin again at rehearsal A. The marking is un poco più, and the melody rises higher than in its first statement.

    2. The melody narrows to two notes

      At rehearsal B the soprano begins “dona eis” softly over muted lower strings. Above its bass notes the organ plays a moving line marked sempre legato. At the words “sempiternam requiem” the soprano alternates between two pitches, and the accompaniment continues beneath her.

    3. Opening melody returns with woodwind solos

      The two-note figure closes, and “Pie Jesu” begins again on the original melody in B♭ major at rehearsal D. Flute, clarinet and bassoon solos join the soprano. The lower strings are marked espressivo, and the bass moves beneath the returning phrase.

    4. Final phrase and B♭ major close

      “Sempiternam requiem” returns in the lower part of the soprano’s range. The harp plays pianissimo figures over the organ and muted strings. A poco rit. slows the last phrase, which rises at its end, and the instruments close on a B♭ major chord.

  5. V. Agnus DeiChorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    5:49

    The Agnus Dei is the fifth movement of Fauré’s Requiem, Op. 48. The tenors sing the text over a separate melody in the violas; the choir then returns to the music of the work’s opening, and the viola melody comes back to end the movement.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening melody, violins and violas

      Violins and violas begin an F major melody. After six instrumental bars the tenors enter with a different melody above it. The violas continue their line, and the basses play plucked notes underneath.

    2. Recording, Monaco, 1962

      Louis Frémaux and the orchestra recorded the Requiem in Monaco in August 1962. According to the Erato sleeve, the recording took place in the cathedral on 11 August. Canon Henri Carol played the organ.

    3. Full choir enters

      The other voices join the tenors, and the harmony moves towards the minor. The choral chords change chromatically against the melody in the violas and cellos. The strings keep the same walking pace beneath.

    4. Return of the tenor melody

      The tenors sing the prayer alone again. F major returns with them, and the violas resume their flowing melody. The other voices rest.

    5. Soprano C on “Lux”

      The sopranos hold C on “Lux”. The other voices enter beneath it in six parts, and the same note becomes the third of an A♭ major chord.

    6. First performance, Madeleine, 1888

      On 16 January 1888 Fauré conducted the first version of the Requiem at the Madeleine in Paris, for the funeral of the architect Joseph Lesoufaché. It had five movements. The Offertoire and Libera me were added later.

    7. Philippe Fauré on the Requiem

      Fauré’s elder son Philippe wrote that his father composed the Requiem “between two bereavements”. He added that the luceat eis is surely murmured for those who rocked Fauré as a child, and whom Fauré would like to rock in the mysterious beyond.

    8. Return of the Introit, Molto largo

      After a stop, the music resumes at Molto largo. The choir returns to “Requiem aeternam” in D minor, the opening of the whole work, within this movement. The flowing string tune does not return, and low strings hold beneath the voices.

    9. Return of the string melody, D major

      The choir stops. The strings bring back their first melody in D major, a minor third below its original F major. F♯ replaces the F♮ of the Introit, and the organ and pizzicato basses accompany the last phrases.

    Joins

    1. From tenor prayer to full choir

      The F major tenor prayer opens into a full choral petition coloured by minor harmony. Violas and cellos continue the moving tune beneath the choir’s changing chords, and bassoons and horns reinforce the voices. The beat stays steady while the choral parts climb and grow louder.

    2. Tenor reprise in F major

      The full choir makes a diminuendo and stops. The organ plays its opening tune again over a pizzicato bass, and the tenors restate their first melody in F major. Beneath them the violas take over the accompaniment, marked espressivo.

    3. C becomes the third of A♭

      The tenor prayer closes in C major. The sopranos hold its keynote alone on “Lux”. At rehearsal E, A♭ enters in the bass and makes that C the third of an A♭ major chord. Altos, divided tenors and divided basses fill out the harmony, pianissimo, and the strings start to move again.

    4. Fermatas before the Introit reprise

      The strings come to rest on D, and fermatas halt the music. The flowing 3/4 gives way to common time and Molto largo. A forceful D fades as the choir enters pianissimo on “Requiem aeternam”. The D minor harmony and slow tempo of the Introit return.

    5. D minor opens into D major

      F♯ turns the choir’s final chord into D major. The key signature changes to two sharps, the metre returns to 3/4, and the score is marked I° Tempo. Violins and violas take up their flowing melody again over a plucked bass. The choir rests and the instruments play the rest of the movement alone.

  6. VI. Libera meBernard Kruysen, baritone; Chorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    4:54

    Bernard Kruysen sings over a steady pizzicato in the cellos and basses. This movement contains the Requiem’s brief passage on judgement, with faster music, brass and the choir singing “Dies irae”.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Baritone solo over pizzicato ostinato

      Cellos and basses play two pizzicato crotchets, rest, then pick up the next bar. Over this repeated pattern the baritone sings “Libera me, Domine” (deliver me, Lord) in a wide-ranging melody with sudden leaps.

    2. Libera me for baritone, 1877

      In 1877 Fauré wrote Libera me as a separate piece for baritone. In the same year he became choirmaster at the Madeleine. The first Requiem was heard there eleven years later, and the earlier music was added to it in later revisions.

    3. Choral entry, “Tremens factus sum ego”

      The choir sings “Tremens factus sum ego” (I am made to tremble), its four parts moving together, pianissimo. The lower strings change from pizzicato to arco, and the baritone rests.

    4. Dies irae, Più mosso

      The marking changes to Più mosso, and the horns enter fortissimo. The choir sings “Dies illa, dies irae” in chords, and trombones join the brass. Fauré sets the words in six beats to the bar.

    5. Dies irae text, Aubert’s sleeve note

      Fauré left out the long Dies irae sequence of the Requiem Mass, but its words appear in this movement. Louis Aubert, who sang in the Madeleine choir as a boy, wrote on this recording’s sleeve that it passes “as an indispensable episode”.

    6. Requiem aeternam, rehearsal F

      After the day of wrath the choir sings “Requiem aeternam”, piano. The 6/4 metre continues, and the strings keep moving underneath.

    7. Unison return of the baritone melody

      The opening melody returns in the whole choir, sung in unison by sopranos, altos, tenors and basses. Kruysen sang it alone at the start. The repeated bass pattern is back beneath it.

    8. Return of the baritone, Kruysen

      The baritone sings the opening words once more over quiet repeated chords, and the choir follows pianissimo. In the last bar the strings play arco again and hold a D minor chord under a fermata.

    Joins

    1. Baritone solo to choral “Tremens”

      The baritone finishes and the plucked bass pattern stops. At C the cellos take up the bow beneath the choir’s pianissimo “Tremens”. The four choral parts move together above sustained lower strings, and the organ continues underneath.

    2. Horns enter at Più mosso in 6/4

      The choir ends on “ventura ira” and stops. At Più mosso the metre changes from common time to 6/4, and the horns begin the next passage fortissimo. The choir enters with “Dies illa, dies irae”, and the trombones join with forceful chords in the strings.

    3. The choir drops to piano

      The day-of-wrath chords make a diminuendo into “Requiem aeternam”, marked piano at rehearsal F. The six-beat metre continues. Violins and violas keep up a moving accompaniment above the low strings, and the brass play more softly as the choir does.

    4. The bass pattern returns first

      Moderato restores common time and the opening bass rhythm. The strings play pizzicato again, with timpani reinforcing the rhythm. Altos and basses are still finishing “luceat eis” when the bass rhythm returns, and the opening melody comes back only after this.

    5. One melody for the whole choir

      Above the restored bass pattern, the choir sings the baritone’s opening D minor melody in unison, marked piano dolce. The strings repeat their chords. Horns and trombones add quiet harmony, and the timpani play intermittently, with gaps between their notes.

    6. The baritone returns to D minor

      The choir makes a diminuendo, and the baritone returns alone on “Libera me” over repeated string and organ chords. The choir follows pianissimo. The music stays in D minor through the closing bars, and the strings bow the final chord under a fermata.

  7. VII. In paradisumChorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    3:35

    “In paradisum” is the seventh and last movement of the Requiem, Op. 48, and sets a prayer that angels may lead the departed into paradise. The sopranos sing above quick broken chords on the organ, accompanied by muted strings and, later, a harp.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, organ and muted strings

      The organ plays quick broken chords in D major. Muted strings hold long notes above it, and the sopranos begin a rising line. There are no brass or woodwinds in this final movement.

    2. Tenor and bass entries on “Jerusalem”

      The divided tenors and basses join the sopranos on “Jerusalem”, entering pianissimo and growing louder together. The organ continues its broken chords beneath the longer choral notes.

    3. Harp entry and soprano phrase

      The harp adds rising semiquavers to the organ’s movement. Above them the sopranos sing “Chorus angelorum te suscipiat”, marked dolce. The strings remain muted.

    4. Fauré’s state funeral, Madeleine, 1924

      In 1924 the Requiem was performed in its full orchestral version at Fauré’s state funeral in the Madeleine. He had served there for years as choirmaster and organist.

    5. Final chord in D major

      The other voices join the sopranos on “requiem”, pianissimo. Organ and harp continue beneath them as the harmony moves to D major. The last choral chord is held under a fermata.

    6. Interview with Louis Aguettant, 1902

      In 1902 Fauré told the interviewer Louis Aguettant that someone had called his Requiem “a lullaby of death”. He said that this was how he felt death, as “a happy deliverance, an aspiration towards happiness beyond”.

    Joins

    1. Tenors and basses enter at “Jerusalem”

      Divided tenors and basses enter on “Jerusalem”, pianissimo, beneath the sopranos. Double basses join the low strings. The voices crescendo together, and the organ continues its quick broken chords at the same pace.

    2. A harp joins the organ

      “Jerusalem” subsides, and the lower voices rest. The muted strings settle as the harp begins rising semiquavers beside the organ’s running figure. The sopranos start “Chorus angelorum”, marked dolce.

    3. Lazarus in the soprano line

      The sopranos continue with “Et cum Lazaro quondam paupere” (and with Lazarus, once a poor man). Organ and harp continue their broken chords, and the muted strings crescendo. The vocal line climbs towards “aeternam”, eternal.

    4. The choir closes in D major

      The soprano line descends from “aeternam” towards “requiem”. The remaining voices make a quiet entry, and the strings drop to pianissimo. Organ and harp continue beneath the sustained choir as the harmony resolves to D major and the final word is prolonged.
