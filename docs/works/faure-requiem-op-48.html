Gabriel Fauré / V. The French Line

Requiem, Op. 48

1887–1900 · Op. 48 · Soprano and baritone soloists, chorus, organ and orchestra; several versions

First heard at the Madeleine in 1888, then enlarged in stages. Low strings and organ support the prayers; In paradisum brings the ending into D major.

7 recordings · 35 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Introït et KyrieChorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    6:23

    A single D sounds, and the choir answers almost under its breath. Louis Frémaux conducts an opening built from low strings, long-held chords and a tenor melody that returns as the Kyrie.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One note, six voices

      Bassoons, horns and low strings sound D, fortissimo, then let it fade. The choir answers pianissimo in six parts on a D minor chord. The violins are silent. Even the organ waits.

    2. The Madeleine, January 1888

      The first performance belongs to a funeral service at the Madeleine in Paris. On 16 January 1888 Fauré conducts five movements for the funeral of Joseph Lesoufaché, an architect. The boy soprano is Louis Aubert, later a composer himself.

    3. The tenors find a melody

      The marking changes to Andante moderato. Violas and cellos begin to move, and the tenors sing a gently falling melody over them. The organ joins with its soft foundation stops; the horns and bassoons rest.

    4. Winds before the sopranos

      Bassoons, horns and trumpets sound a brief, strong chord before the soprano prayer. The organ adds reed stops, then withdraws to its foundations. “Te decet hymnus” — praise is due to you — unfolds over gently moving violas and cellos.

    5. A cry to be heard

      “Exaudi” — hear — brings the whole choir to fortissimo. The answer is immediately softer. Fauré keeps alternating the loud appeal and the quiet reply while violas and cellos press ahead beneath the voices.

    6. The tenor tune shared

      The Kyrie uses the tenors’ earlier tune. Sopranos, altos and tenors now sing it together in unison, before it opens into four-part harmony. The bass line keeps walking underneath.

    7. Christe, then an echo

      The first “Christe” is fortissimo, the next piano. The choir repeats this call and echo above restless violas and cellos. The organ’s reed stops add bite to the louder appeal.

    8. A quiet final Kyrie

      The choir returns to “Kyrie eleison” in short, quiet phrases. Cellos and basses continue beneath it; towards the end, a bassoon and a horn add soft solo lines. The last chord is D minor.

    How it is joined

    1. The held chord begins to walk

      A fermata holds the choir’s last chord. Andante moderato then quickens the D minor prayer: violas and cellos begin their flowing lines, the bass starts moving and the organ enters with eight-foot foundation stops. The tenors take the melody alone, marked dolce espressivo.

    2. Winds announce the next prayer

      Bassoons, horns and trumpets return with strong chords over the low strings. The organ briefly adds reed stops, then restores its quieter foundations at rehearsal E. The strings diminish, leaving room for the sopranos’ “Te decet hymnus”.

    3. The whole choir calls out

      The strings crescendo beneath the end of the soprano prayer. At rehearsal F the whole choir calls “Exaudi” fortissimo over a D minor chord. Violas, cellos and basses answer with forceful rising figures; the next choral appeal drops immediately to piano.

    4. The tenor melody becomes the Kyrie

      The full choir diminishes and the wind chords fade. Sopranos, altos and tenors begin the Kyrie in unison on the earlier tenor melody, with D still beneath them in the bass. Violas and cellos recover their flowing accompaniment; the vocal marking is dolce espressivo.

    5. The choir strikes Christe

      The Kyrie grows into the first “Christe”, marked fortissimo. The organ adds reeds to its foundation stops, and the lower strings climb beneath the choir. A piano repetition follows straight away, then another fortissimo call: the new words arrive in sharply separated appeals.

    6. The last Kyrie settles

      The “Christe” calls diminish into the final Kyrie. The choir sings quietly above moving cellos and basses, with the organ back on foundation stops. The lower-string phrases continue towards the final D minor chord, marked pianissimo.

  2. II. OffertoireBernard Kruysen, baritone; Chorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    7:40

    Altos and tenors begin a close pursuit through B minor; Bernard Kruysen answers them with the baritone’s Hostias. Fauré saves B major for the last “Amen”.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Low strings climb

      Violas and cellos rise slowly above the organ, growing louder through the prelude. The marking is Adagio molto. Fauré leaves the violins, winds and harp out of this movement; the choir will sing among these low instruments.

    2. Two voices in pursuit

      Altos and tenors sing “O Domine Jesu Christe” in canon: one line follows closely behind the other. The narrow B minor tune begins pianissimo, marked dolcissimo. Each new appeal starts a step higher; the basses reinforce the later calls.

    3. A prayer added later

      The Hostias joins the Requiem in 1889. Fauré then enlarges the Offertoire and adds the older Libera me. On 21 January 1893 he conducts the seven-movement work at the Madeleine, where he serves as choirmaster.

    4. One voice offers Hostias

      The baritone begins “Hostias et preces” — offerings and prayers — on a repeated note. The pace is now Andante moderato, in three beats to the bar. Quiet quavers in the violas and cellos accompany him; the choir rests.

    5. The organ answers alone

      The strings stop, and the organ has a small solo between the baritone’s phrases. He returns with “fac eas, Domine, de morte transire ad vitam”: let them pass from death to life. His melody moves more freely now.

    6. The sopranos join the prayer

      The opening pace returns. This time all four choral parts take up the canon, including the sopranos who were silent at the start. Their entries overlap above the organ; the strings leave the voices room.

    7. Amen turns to major

      Fauré adds “Amen” to the Offertory text. D♯ brightens the closing harmony into B major, and the voices pass the word between them. The strings sound one last chord, leaving the choir and organ to finish.

    How it is joined

    1. Low strings make room for voices

      The rising viola and cello prelude diminishes. Altos and tenors begin the B minor canon pianissimo, marked dolcissimo, while the organ supports them. The melody passes from one choral part to the other in close succession.

    2. The baritone begins on one note

      The choir’s short phrases break off into rests. The baritone begins “Hostias” as the score changes to 3/4 and Andante moderato. D major opens beneath him; the violas and cellos repeat quiet quavers, and the organ is marked dolce.

    3. The organ takes the next phrase

      The strings release their chord at rehearsal F and the organ continues alone, marked Solo. Its upper line rises over a moving bass. The baritone returns on “fac eas” as the cellos rejoin, pianissimo; the triple metre continues unchanged.

    4. The slow canon returns

      The baritone finishes his promise of life. I° Tempo, adagio molto restores common time and B minor, and the choir begins “O Domine” again in overlapping entries. After one chord the strings rest, leaving the organ to accompany the four voices.

    5. B minor brightens to B major

      The last plea fades into “Amen”. D♯ replaces D♮ as the key signature changes from two sharps to five. The strings give the new B major chord one quiet stroke, then stop. Choir and organ continue the imitations to the final chord.

  3. III. SanctusChorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    3:10

    The sopranos’ three-note “Sanctus” floats over harp and divided violas. Fauré keeps the brass in reserve for one splendid “Hosanna”, then returns to the quiet with which he began.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Three notes for Sanctus

      The harp rises through an E♭ major chord while two viola parts weave around each other. The sopranos sing “Sanctus” on just three notes, and the men answer. The organ holds the harmony underneath.

    2. Two hundred and fifty performers

      Paris, 12 July 1900: Paul Taffanel conducts the expanded Requiem at the Trocadéro during the Exposition universelle. There are 250 performers. The publisher, Julien Hamelle, has encouraged Fauré to prepare the work for large concert halls.

    3. Hosanna finds its brass

      The soprano “Hosanna” begins softly. As the phrase grows, the harp exchanges its running notes for firm chords, and horns and trumpets sound a fanfare. The men’s voices declare “Hosanna in excelsis” above them.

    4. Back to the harp

      The brass fanfare subsides. Harp arpeggios return, the violas resume their running figures and the choir sings “Sanctus” again, pianissimo. The altos, silent until now, join the closing prayer.

    How it is joined

    1. The voices stretch their phrases

      The harp and divided violas keep their flowing figures. Above them, “Dominus Deus Sabaoth” carries the sopranos beyond the small three-note phrase of “Sanctus”. The men answer the longer line, with the organ sustaining beneath both groups.

    2. Sopranos over divided violins

      The men rest while the sopranos sing “Pleni sunt coeli et terra”, marked sempre dolce. Divided violins add their line over the harp; the cellos sustain with the bow. The men return with “gloria tua”.

    3. Running notes become a fanfare

      The sopranos begin “Hosanna” softly over the continuing viola figures. The harp and strings then gather into repeated chords, with a firmer bass beat. Horns and trumpets enter forte, and the men sing their “Hosanna” above this fanfare.

    4. The fanfare returns to E♭

      The sopranos’ answer diminishes, and the brass sink to pianissimo. The harp resumes its broken E♭ major chords; plucked lower strings return to the bow. The choir sings “Sanctus” once more over the organ’s sustained harmony.

  4. IV. Pie JesuDenis Thilliez, boy soprano; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    3:01

    Denis Thilliez, a boy soprano, sings the Requiem’s central prayer, beginning with the organ alone. The melody reaches higher on its second appeal, then settles into the simplest possible plea: two alternating notes.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A voice above the organ

      The boy soprano begins in B♭ major, marked dolce e tranquille — sweetly and calmly. Only the organ accompanies him. The orchestra waits through the opening prayer, “Pie Jesu Domine, dona eis requiem”: merciful Lord Jesus, grant them rest.

    2. Boys at the Madeleine

      At the Madeleine, where Fauré works, women cannot sing in the choir. Boys take the upper parts and the Pie Jesu solo. In the concert hall Fauré prefers women for these lines, with their longer breath and adult voices.

    3. Two notes for rest

      “Dona eis” expands the melody, then “sempiternam requiem” — everlasting rest — narrows it to two alternating notes. Muted violas and cellos keep moving beneath the voice. The organ part is marked sempre legato.

    4. The first prayer returns

      The opening melody returns, now surrounded by soft woodwind solos. Its last plea sinks lower than before. At the end the harp plays quietly, the pace eases, and the voice turns upwards on the final “requiem”.

    How it is joined

    1. The second prayer reaches higher

      Muted strings, harp and woodwinds answer the first prayer with soft chords. They then withdraw, leaving the soprano and organ to begin again at rehearsal A. The marking is un poco più, and the melody reaches higher than its first statement.

    2. The melody narrows to two notes

      The soprano begins “dona eis” softly at rehearsal B over muted lower strings. The organ carries a moving line above its bass notes, marked sempre legato. As the words reach “sempiternam requiem”, the singer settles into two alternating pitches while the accompaniment continues beneath.

    3. The first melody returns with winds

      The two-note plea closes, and “Pie Jesu” begins again on the original melody in B♭ major. Flute, clarinet and bassoon solos join the soprano. The lower strings are marked espressivo, and the bass moves beneath the returning phrase.

    4. The last requiem turns upwards

      “Sempiternam requiem” returns in a lower part of the soprano’s voice. The harp plays pianissimo figures over the organ and muted strings. A poco rit. eases the last phrase, which turns upwards before the instruments settle on B♭ major.

  5. V. Agnus DeiChorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    5:49

    The tenors sing their prayer while the violas carry a tune of their own. Fauré lets that string melody have the last word, after the choir has remembered the dark opening of the Requiem.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The strings have a tune

      Violins and violas begin a flowing F major melody. When the tenors enter, they sing a different tune above it. The violas keep going: two melodies share the prayer, with plucked bass notes underneath.

    2. The whole choir pleads

      The other voices join the tenors and the harmony turns towards the minor. Their chords shift uneasily against the viola and cello melody. The strings keep the same walking pace beneath this more urgent appeal.

    3. The tenor prayer returns

      The tenors have the prayer to themselves again. F major returns with them, and the violas resume their flowing tune. The other voices rest.

    4. Light on a held C

      The sopranos hold C on “Lux” — light. As the other voices gather beneath it, the same note becomes the third of an A♭ major chord. Six choral parts spread out from that single thread of sound.

    5. A funeral at the Madeleine

      Paris, 16 January 1888: Fauré conducts the first version of the Requiem at the Madeleine, for the funeral of the architect Joseph Lesoufaché. There are five movements. The Offertoire and Libera me join the work later.

    6. The opening prayer remembered

      The music stops, then begins again at Molto largo. The choir returns to “Requiem aeternam” in D minor: the opening of the whole work, recalled inside this movement. The flowing string tune is gone; low strings hold beneath the voices.

    7. The strings finish the prayer

      The choir falls silent. The strings bring back their first tune in D major, a minor third below its original F major. F♯ has replaced the F♮ of the remembered Introit; the organ and plucked basses accompany the last phrases.

    How it is joined

    1. More voices, darker chords

      The F major tenor prayer opens into a full choral petition coloured by minor harmony. Violas and cellos continue the moving tune beneath the choir’s changing chords, with bassoons and horns reinforcing the voices. The beat stays steady as the choral parts climb and grow louder.

    2. F major and the tenors

      The full choir diminishes and rests. Over the organ’s returning tune and a plucked bass, the tenors begin their first melody again in F major. The violas take up the accompaniment beneath them, marked espressivo.

    3. C becomes the third of A♭

      The tenor prayer settles on C major. The sopranos hold its keynote alone on “Lux”; at rehearsal E, A♭ arrives in the bass and turns that C into the third of a new chord. Altos, divided tenors and divided basses fill out the harmony, pianissimo, while the strings begin to move again.

    4. A pause before the Introit

      The strings settle over D, and fermatas stop the music. Common time and Molto largo replace the flowing triple metre. A forceful D fades under the choir’s pianissimo “Requiem aeternam”, restoring the D minor harmony and slow tread of the Introit.

    5. D minor opens into D major

      F♯ brightens the choir’s final chord into D major. The score restores triple metre and the opening pace, and violins and violas recover their flowing melody over a plucked bass. The choir rests for the remainder of the movement.

  6. VI. Libera meBernard Kruysen, baritone; Chorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    4:54

    Bernard Kruysen sings over the steady tread of plucked cellos and basses. This movement contains the Requiem’s brief vision of judgement: faster music, brass, and a choir crying “Dies irae”.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A tread beneath the baritone

      Cellos and basses pluck two crotchets, rest, then pick up the next bar. Above this repeated pattern the baritone sings “Libera me, Domine” — deliver me, Lord — in a melody that ranges widely, with sudden leaps.

    2. Eleven years before the funeral

      In 1877 Fauré writes Libera me as a separate piece for baritone. That year he becomes choirmaster at the Madeleine. Eleven years later the first Requiem is heard there; this older music joins it during the subsequent revisions.

    3. The choir begins to tremble

      “Tremens factus sum ego” — I am made to tremble — belongs to the choir. Its four parts move together, pianissimo. The lower strings put down their plucking fingers and take up the bow; the baritone rests.

    4. The day of wrath

      The marking changes to Più mosso, and horns break in fortissimo. The choir’s “Dies illa, dies irae” follows in heavy chords, with trombones joining the brass. Fauré sets the words in six beats to the bar.

    5. Rest after the outcry

      The choir asks for eternal rest, piano, after the day of wrath. The six-beat bars continue, and the strings keep moving underneath. The same voices that cried out now sing “Requiem aeternam”.

    6. The solo prayer becomes everyone’s

      The opening melody returns in the whole choir, sung in unison. The repeated bass pattern is back beneath it. What Kruysen sings alone at the start now passes through sopranos, altos, tenors and basses together.

    7. Kruysen returns alone

      The baritone sings the opening words once more over quiet repeated chords. The choir answers pianissimo. In the last bar the strings return to the bow and hold a D minor chord under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. Plucked basses take up the bow

      The baritone finishes and the plucked bass pattern stops. Cellos take up the bow beneath the choir’s pianissimo “Tremens”. Four choral parts move together above sustained lower strings, while the organ continues quietly underneath.

    2. Horns announce a faster tread

      The choir’s “ventura ira” settles and falls silent. Più mosso changes common time to 6/4, and fortissimo horns begin the next passage. The choir enters with “Dies illa, dies irae”; trombones and forceful string chords reinforce the appeal.

    3. The choir drops to piano

      The day-of-wrath chords diminish into “Requiem aeternam”, marked piano at rehearsal F. The six-beat metre continues. Violins and violas keep a moving accompaniment above the low strings, and the brass soften with the choir.

    4. The bass pattern comes back first

      Moderato restores common time and the opening bass rhythm. The strings pluck again, with timpani reinforcing the tread. Altos and basses are still finishing “luceat eis”; the opening melody returns only after this preparation.

    5. One melody for the whole choir

      Above the restored bass pattern, the choir begins the baritone’s opening D minor melody in unison, marked piano dolce. The strings repeat their chords. Horns and trombones add quiet harmony, while the timpani leave gaps between their notes.

    6. The baritone returns to D minor

      The choir diminishes, and the baritone returns alone on “Libera me” over repeated string and organ chords. The choir answers pianissimo. D minor holds through the closing bars; the strings bow the final chord and a fermata prolongs it.

  7. VII. In paradisumChorale Philippe Caillard; Orchestre national de l’Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Louis Frémaux · 1962
    3:35

    The Requiem ends with a welcome: may angels lead you into paradise. Sopranos sing above the organ’s quick broken chords, with muted strings and, later, a harp.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The organ keeps shining

      The organ turns over quick broken chords in D major. Muted strings hold long notes above it, and the sopranos begin a rising line. There are no brass or woodwinds in this final movement.

    2. Voices gather on Jerusalem

      The divided tenors and basses join the sopranos on “Jerusalem”, entering pianissimo and growing together. The organ keeps its broken chords running beneath the longer choral notes.

    3. A harp for the angels

      The harp adds rising semiquavers to the organ’s movement. Above them the sopranos sing “Chorus angelorum te suscipiat” — may the choir of angels receive you — marked dolce. The strings remain muted.

    4. Back to the Madeleine

      In 1924 the Requiem returns to the Madeleine for Fauré’s own state funeral. The church where he spent years as choirmaster and organist hears the work in its full orchestral version.

    5. The last word is rest

      The other voices join the sopranos on “requiem”, pianissimo. Organ and harp continue beneath them as the harmony settles into D major. The last choral chord is held under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The sopranos acquire a choir

      Divided tenors and basses enter on “Jerusalem”, pianissimo, beneath the sopranos. Double basses join the low strings. The voices crescendo together while the organ continues its quick broken chords, unchanged in pace.

    2. A harp joins the organ

      “Jerusalem” subsides, and the lower voices rest. The muted strings settle quietly as the harp begins rising semiquavers beside the organ’s running figure. The sopranos start “Chorus angelorum”, marked dolce.

    3. Lazarus in the soprano line

      The sopranos continue with “Et cum Lazaro quondam paupere” — and with Lazarus, once a poor man. Organ and harp maintain their broken chords while the muted strings crescendo. The vocal line climbs towards “aeternam”, eternal.

    4. The choir settles into D major

      The soprano line descends from “aeternam” towards “requiem”. The remaining voices join quietly, and the strings drop to pianissimo. Organ and harp carry on beneath the sustained choir as the harmony finds D major and the final word is prolonged.
