Sergei Rachmaninoff / IV. Fin de siècle

Polka de V.R. (Polka de W. R.)

1911 · Piano

4 min

The recording

  1. AllegrettoSergei Rachmaninoff, piano · April 1919
    4:09

    Rachmaninoff turns a polka his father liked to play into a small keyboard comedy: the tune keeps its dancing bass while the hands grow ever busier around it. This is the composer’s 1919 Edison recording.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The bass starts dancing

      A few bars of semiquavers slow and fade before the tune arrives at pp. The left hand alternates a bass note with a chord: the polka’s oom-pah. Above it, the right hand slips little triplets between the melody notes.

    2. His father’s favourite polka

      Vasily Rachmaninoff likes to play this tune, and his son gives the arrangement his father’s initials. The melody belongs to Franz Behr’s Scherzpolka, Op. 303. Rachmaninoff moves it from F to A♭ major and dedicates his 1911 version to Leopold Godowsky.

    3. The dance catches its breath

      Meno mosso slows the dance after the staccato chords. A slurred melody curls over left-hand chords; a tempo restores the pace, then another Meno mosso holds it back. Rachmaninoff keeps changing his mind about how quickly to go.

    4. Ten sides for Edison

      In April 1919 Rachmaninoff, newly arrived in America and in need of money, agrees to record for Edison. Edison wants ten sides at once, before any contract is signed, and issues all three takes of each. The RCA booklet that reissues them calls the sound “distant and disappointing”.

    5. A joke in E major

      The tune turns up in E major, marked scherzando — playfully. Short accented chords and quick inner notes jostle against the bass. The change from four flats to four sharps makes a new corner of the keyboard feel like home.

    6. Two tunes in A♭

      A♭ major returns with the opening tempo. Rachmaninoff adds his own countermelody to Behr’s tune, giving the little dance another voice. The hands have more to say even while the bass keeps its familiar step.

    7. The last soft chord

      A quiet run climbs through the high notes. Meno mosso and a ritardando slow the final bars, and the piece ends on a soft chord under a fermata. The polka takes a small bow.

    How it is joined

    1. Both hands strike short chords

      Poco più mosso quickens the dance. In A♭ major, both hands play short, accented chords, beginning at mf and growing through f to ff. Rests cut the chord pairs apart while the bass keeps leaping.

    2. A run softens the chords

      A descending right-hand figure and a ritardando draw the music away from the ff chords. At Meno mosso, the right hand plays a slurred melody above moving inner notes and a held left-hand chord. The marking drops to mf.

    3. A quick run reaches E major

      Poco a poco rallentando slows the curling melody. A run marked veloce starts softly, swells and descends; the left hand falls silent beneath its last notes. At the double bar, E major and Tempo precedente restore the dancing pace, with scherzando written above the tune.

    4. Four flats and the old tune

      The E major passage ends with a change back to four flats. Forte sextuplets sweep above left-hand broken chords, then a ritardando eases into Tempo I. The A♭ major tune returns at p, with a second melodic line running beside it.

    5. The quick hands come to rest

      A long pp run rises across the keyboard before Meno mosso. The left hand holds a low octave, the right hand’s line slows through a ritardando, and a final high chord completes the A♭ major ending at pp, under a fermata.
