George Frideric Handel / I. The Baroque

Water Music, Suite No. 2 in D, HWV 349

1717 · HWV 348–350 · Orchestra with horns, trumpets and woodwinds

About fifty musicians played from a barge during George I’s Thames excursion in July 1717. The king asked for the music three times on the outward and return journeys.

5 recordings · 13 min

In the Hall

  1. I. AllegroMarine Chamber Orchestra of the United States Marine Band; Major Michelle Rakers, conductor · 29 January 2017
    2:05

    Trumpets begin this Water Music suite with a fanfare, and the horns answer over a running bass. Handel keeps the instruments apart until the whole orchestra finally takes up one tune.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Trumpets call, horns answer

      The trumpets make the first call. Oboes and strings answer with quick semiquavers; then the horns take their turn, with bassoon and bass running beneath them. Handel leaves whole groups silent while another speaks.

    2. Everyone has the tune

      The closing passage brings trumpets, horns, oboes, second violins and violas together on a dotted tune. First violins race around it in semiquavers, and the bass keeps moving.

    3. Six chords for strings

      After the brass finish, Handel adds a small Adagio for strings and continuo. Six chords slow the music to a walk. A♯ and D♯ appear in the first violins: a suddenly private ending to all that public splendour.

    How it is joined

    1. A pause after the horns

      The horns finish their fanfare over the moving bassoon and bass. Rests open a gap before the orchestra returns with short D major chords and rising figures. Trumpets and horns now join the oboes and strings in the same phrase.

    2. The trumpets begin another round

      The short chordal exchange ends with another rest. Trumpets lead the next phrase; oboes and violins answer with D major arpeggios while the bass rests. Horns then take over, with bassoon and continuo moving beneath them.

    3. The answers grow shorter

      The horns finish over running bass quavers. Trumpets, oboes and upper strings answer with a short turning figure, which passes straight back to the horns and bass. The exchanges shrink to a bar apiece; the Allegro pulse continues through the handovers.

    4. The trumpet phrase comes back

      The trumpet-led passage returns in D major. Oboes and violins again run through arpeggios while the bass rests, then the horns answer over bassoon and continuo. The music revisits the same order of speakers, with the quick string figures between them.

    5. Horns, then the whole orchestra

      Horns play above the running bassoon and continuo while the trumpets, oboes and strings rest. Then the whole orchestra joins the dotted D major tune. First violins add semiquavers; the bass keeps its running line beneath the gathered brass.

    6. The strings have the last word

      The D major tutti ends with short chords separated by rests. Handel marks the remaining string-and-continuo passage Adagio: six chords spread across three bars. Trumpets, horns and oboes are silent. The first violins introduce A♯ and D♯ into the slow-moving harmony.

  2. II. Alla hornpipeMarine Chamber Orchestra of the United States Marine Band; Major Michelle Rakers, conductor · 29 January 2017
    3:49

    Handel gives the hornpipe tune to oboes and strings, then lets trumpets and horns answer in turn. The brass fall silent for the middle section, making their return in D major all the more welcome.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Oboes and strings first

      The hornpipe strides in three broad beats, with oboes and strings carrying the tune. Trumpets and horns wait ten bars. When their turns come, each pair plays alone: the trumpets first, then the horns.

    2. The dance starts again

      The first section repeats, from the oboe-and-string tune to the final D major chord. The brass have their own separate turns again. Handel makes the same melody sound like three groups answering across a room.

    3. Fifty musicians afloat

      On 17 July 1717 about fifty musicians play beside George I’s barge on the Thames. The king likes Handel’s music so much that he asks for it three times. Supper in Chelsea begins at one in the morning.

    4. Thirty-five bars without brass

      The middle section belongs to oboes, bassoon and strings. Trumpets and horns rest for all thirty-five bars. The violins now have room for running figures beneath and around the wind tune.

    5. Back to the first tune

      Da capo: back to the beginning. The D major hornpipe returns, with the oboes and strings first and the two brass pairs taking their turns. This time the opening section plays once and stops at the mark Fine.

    How it is joined

    1. The D major chord, then the tune

      The full orchestra reaches a held D major chord, with D in the bass. The repeat returns to the opening oboe-and-string tune; trumpets and horns rest again for ten bars. Their separate entries will follow in the same order.

    2. A middle section without brass

      The repeated first section closes on D major. Trumpets and horns stop, leaving oboes and strings with a new phrase above the bassoon and continuo. The first violins soon add running quavers; the brass remain silent through the entire middle section.

    3. The winds leave the strings

      Oboes and bassoon stop. Violins continue the running figures above a sustained bass, with viola and second violin filling the harmony. These eight string-and-continuo bars lead to the written da capo, which sends the orchestra back to D major and the opening tune.

    4. Da capo brings back D major

      The strings finish the middle section and the da capo returns to the opening D major tune. Oboes and bassoon rejoin the strings; trumpets and horns wait their turn. The opening section is played once more, stopping at its D major cadence and the mark Fine.

  3. III. LentementMarine Chamber Orchestra of the United States Marine Band; Major Michelle Rakers, conductor · 29 January 2017
    2:16

    The slow dance of the D major Water Music suite gives trumpets and horns long notes beneath the oboes and violins. A middle strain leaves the brass silent before the opening returns.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Long notes under the tune

      Lentement means slowly. Oboes and violins carry a tune in three beats while trumpets and horns take turns holding long notes beneath it. The bass moves steadily, with figures telling the continuo player which chords to supply.

    2. A phrase without brass

      The second strain begins with oboes and bassoon; the strings join their phrase. Trumpets and horns have fifteen bars of rests. The familiar long brass notes disappear, leaving the woodwinds and strings to carry the dance.

    3. The first strain comes home

      Da capo brings back the opening, with the brass and their long notes. The first strain plays once more. Its last D major chord bears a fermata, and the mark Fine makes this return the ending.

    How it is joined

    1. A fermata before the repeat

      The first strain ends on D major, with a fermata over every part. The repeat sign sends the players back to the upbeat. Oboes, strings and bass begin the same tune again, with the trumpets and horns retaining their long notes.

    2. The brass rest after D major

      The repeated strain finishes on its D major fermata. Oboes and bassoon begin the next phrase while the strings wait, then join them. Trumpets and horns have no notes in this fifteen-bar strain; the woodwinds now carry the opening by themselves.

    3. Da capo restores the brass

      The second strain closes with the oboes, strings and bass together. Da capo sends the orchestra back to the first upbeat and the D major opening. Trumpets and horns return; the strain proceeds to its fermata chord, where Fine ends the dance.

  4. IV. BourréeMarine Chamber Orchestra of the United States Marine Band; Major Michelle Rakers, conductor · 29 January 2017
    1:16

    Twelve bars are enough for this brisk D major bourrée: a short strain, a longer answer, and both repeated. Michelle Rakers and the Marine Chamber Orchestra play the whole dance three times.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. An upbeat and twelve bars

      One upbeat launches the dance. A four-bar phrase is repeated, then an eight-bar answer is repeated too. The tune moves in quick, even notes, with the bass keeping its own steady stride underneath.

    2. A catch before the cadence

      The dance makes its third circuit. At the end of the longer strain, a note hangs over the bass before dropping a step: the little 4–3 suspension written beneath the stave. Then the chord settles on D major.

    How it is joined

    1. D major, then the upbeat again

      A 4–3 suspension delays the last cadence of the eight-bar strain. The bass reaches D and the upper parts hold the D major chord. The upbeat then starts the dance again, with no linking passage between the two rounds.

    2. One more round of the dance

      The second round closes on the same D major chord. The upbeat immediately recalls the opening, and the short and long strains make their last circuit. Both retain their repeats and the bass returns to D at the final cadence.

  5. V. MinuetMarine Chamber Orchestra of the United States Marine Band; Major Michelle Rakers, conductor · 29 January 2017
    3:15

    This D major minuet closes the Marine Chamber Orchestra’s Water Music programme with twenty-four bars of ceremonial dance. After playing both strains with their repeats, the orchestra takes the whole minuet once more.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A dance on repeated notes

      The melody begins by repeating F♯, three crotchets in a bar. Below it, the bass steps through D major. Short runs and dotted rhythms enliven the upper parts, but the three-beat tread keeps the dance stately.

    2. Three bars for the strings

      Handel begins the second strain with the brass resting. Oboes and strings carry three bars on their own before trumpets and horns join them. The bass keeps moving through the handover.

    3. Arnold prints the whole score

      In London in 1788, Samuel Arnold publishes Water Music in full score, seventy-one years after the royal river party. He ends with this D major minuet. Beneath its final chord stands one word: Finis.

    4. The minuet once more

      The orchestra returns to the beginning and plays the two strains once each. The repeated F♯ notes come back, then the longer answer carries the dance to its last D major chord.

    How it is joined

    1. A moving bass beneath held chords

      The upper parts hold D major while the bass outlines the chord in crotchets, D–F♯–A. After the repeated first strain, Handel starts the longer answer with oboes, strings and bass; trumpets and horns rest for three bars before rejoining.

    2. The D major dance returns

      The sixteen-bar strain finishes on a held D major chord, with D in the bass. The orchestra returns to the opening repeated F♯ notes and the three-beat bass. This final traversal takes each strain once.

    3. The longer answer closes the dance

      The first strain ends with the upper parts holding D major over the moving bass. The second strain follows directly, without a repeat of the first. Its opening rests the trumpets and horns for three bars; the full scoring returns and the bass reaches D at the close.
