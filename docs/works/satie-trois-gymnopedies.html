Erik Satie / V. The French Line

Trois Gymnopédies

1888 · Piano

A low bass note, then a chord, then room for the melody: three pieces built from wonderfully little. Debussy orchestrated the first and third.

3 min

The recording

  1. I. Lent et douloureuxRobin Alciatore · c. 2008
    3:04

    A low bass note, a chord on the second beat, and a melody that seems in no hurry to move. Satie’s first Gymnopédie makes a small change of note at the end feel enormous.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Bass, chord, then melody

      The bass sounds on beat one, the chord on beat two. G major seventh alternates with D major seventh, softly and evenly. Four bars pass before the melody enters; its first phrase comes to rest on an F♯ held across four more bars.

    2. One E, changing chords

      The melody drops to a low E and stays there for three bars. Beneath it, E minor becomes E minor seventh, then D minor. The bass still sounds on the first beat and the chord on the second; a single held note sees three different harmonies pass beneath it.

    3. The first ending

      The closing phrase rises to G, then falls to F♯. After the repeated bass notes, two full chords bring the first half to rest: A minor seventh, then D major. That F♯ gives the ending its gentle brightness.

    4. The opening returns

      The low G returns. For four bars the piano has only its bass notes and softly struck chords, just as at the beginning. The melody starts again with the same F♯ and A; the changed ending is still some way off.

    5. F♮ darkens the ending

      The closing phrase reaches its G again. This time it falls to F♮, and the bass stays on E beneath the altered melody. The final chord is D minor. One white note has changed the ending of a piece that began among so many sharps.

    How it is joined

    1. The melody drops to E

      The bass leaves its alternating G and D for F♯, B and E. Above the E-minor chord, the melody drops to a low E and holds it through three bars. G major over E follows, then D minor under the same melody note. The slow three-beat accompaniment keeps its familiar rhythm.

    2. D major, then the low G

      A minor seventh closes onto D major, with F♯ in the upper chord. Then the low G restarts the accompaniment: B–D–F♯ enters on beat two, making G major seventh. D major seventh follows. The melody waits four bars before returning.

    3. An E bass holds its ground

      The repeated passage ends on D seventh. E minor follows, with G in the melody, as at the first ending. Now F♮ replaces the earlier F♯, and D-minor notes sound above an E bass that persists for six bars. The last two chords are A minor seventh and D minor.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. I. Lent et douloureux — guitar arrangementMichael Laucke, guitar and arrangement · 23 July 2001
    2:52

    Michael Laucke fits the first Gymnopédie onto six guitar strings. The bass, the softly plucked chords and the long melody all belong to one pair of hands.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Satie on six strings

      Laucke’s guitar carries the low bass, the accompanying chords and the melody. Satie spaces them apart: bass on beat one, chord on beat two, then long melody notes above. Each pluck begins to fade while the next note is being made.

    2. A lower melody note

      The melody drops lower and holds a single note while the chords beneath it change. The accompaniment keeps its slow three-beat step. Satie can darken a phrase without making it hurry.

    3. The bass starts again

      The first ending has settled in major. Now the opening bass-and-chord pattern returns, with space above it before the melody comes back. The same tune makes a second circuit.

    4. The darker ending

      The closing melody changes one of its long notes: the major third falls a semitone. Satie’s second ending closes in minor, leaving the same bass destination with a different chord above it.

    How it is joined

    1. A held note crosses changing chords

      The melody drops to a lower held note, and the bass leaves its opening alternation. In the piano score, E minor becomes E minor seventh, then D minor under the held E. Laucke carries this change into the guitar arrangement, keeping the slow bass-on-one, chord-on-two rhythm.

    2. The accompaniment starts again

      The first ending closes in major. The accompaniment then starts afresh, with a low bass note followed by a seventh chord. Four bars of this pattern prepare the melody. Satie leaves the upper line empty while the bass finds its familiar step.

    3. The third falls a semitone

      The repeated melody takes Satie’s second ending. Its long major third is flattened, and the accompanying chords change with it. In the piano original, F♮ replaces F♯ over a repeated E bass; the last chord closes in D minor. The guitar carries the same major-to-minor turn in its transposed key.
