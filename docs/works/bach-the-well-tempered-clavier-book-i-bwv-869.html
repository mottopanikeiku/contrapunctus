Johann Sebastian Bach / I. The Baroque

The Well-Tempered Clavier, Book I · BWV 869

1722 · BWV 846–869 · Keyboard

Twenty-four prelude-and-fugue pairs, ascending from C major and C minor to B major and B minor. The first prelude began as a lesson for Wilhelm Friedemann.

8 min

The recording

  1. Fugue in B minorKimiko Ishizaka, piano
    8:02

    Kimiko Ishizaka plays the fugue that closes Book I of The Well-Tempered Clavier. Its subject touches all twelve notes of the chromatic scale, twisting through semitones before four voices gradually crowd the keyboard.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Alto, all twelve notes

      After a short rest, the alto begins F♯–D–B. Wide leaps connect pairs of notes only a semitone apart. Before this long subject finishes, it has touched all twelve notes of the chromatic scale; the line settles on F♯, ready for an answer.

    2. The soprano arrives last

      The subject reaches the highest voice after its turns in alto, tenor and bass. Under the soprano’s F♯-minor answer, the bass takes a slower descending line. Each part inherits a job another voice has just finished.

    3. The bass steps down

      The bass descends through short four-note patterns. Above it, soprano and tenor pass a quicker figure between them, holding notes across the beat. The alto briefly remembers the subject’s first three notes, then leaves the other voices to continue.

    4. The tenor has the subject

      B–G–E: the tenor begins the subject in E minor. The bass sounds E and falls silent, leaving room around the new entry. Above it, the soprano descends in slower notes while the tenor works through the familiar semitone pairs.

    5. A singer in the household

      On 3 December 1721 Bach marries Anna Magdalena Wilcke, a court singer at Köthen. He is Prince Leopold’s music director. The next year, the date on Book I of The Well-Tempered Clavier, he begins looking for a new post.

    6. Four entries, one bar apart

      The soprano begins in B minor; a bar later the alto starts in E minor. The bass follows in A major, then the tenor in D major. The voices cut across one another before the subject can finish: this crowding of entries is called stretto.

    7. The tenor in F♯ minor

      The tenor begins C♯–A–F♯, carrying the subject into F♯ minor. Its winding continuation needs B♯ and even F𝄪. The soprano moves in longer notes above it, giving the restless tenor something slow to lean against.

    8. The bass stays silent

      Soprano and alto exchange the episode’s quick figures. The bass voice rests, so the tenor supplies the line beneath them, stepping down through the same small patterns heard earlier. The music is gathering itself for the final low-voice entries.

    9. F♯ holds beneath the alto

      The bass holds F♯ while the alto gives one last F♯–D–B, the subject’s opening. The low note is a dominant pedal, delaying the arrival on B. Above it, a sustained B rubs against the harmony before resolving to A♯.

    10. D♯ in the last chord

      The four voices meet on B major. D♯ replaces the D♮ of the home key: the raised final third known as a Picardy third. After all those twisting lines, the score ends with a held chord and a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The bass descends beneath tied notes

      After the soprano’s answer, the bass descends in repeating four-note groups. Soprano and tenor trade tied notes and semiquavers above it. The soprano’s E hangs over a bass D before resolving down; at the end of the sequence, A♯ in the bass rises to B and the alto restores the complete B-minor subject.

    2. D♯ leads the bass to E

      The bass’s descending sequence ends on D♯, which rises to E. Then the bass stops. The tenor begins B–G–E, the complete subject in E minor, while the soprano supplies its slower descending companion. A three-note hint in bar 28 has become a full statement.

    3. Running notes prepare F♯ minor

      The D-major entries lead into running semiquavers. The soprano climbs from D, and the alto answers with a similar figure from E. E♯ then appears in the alto, pointing towards F♯ minor. The bass descends A–G♯–F♯ and stops; the tenor begins the next subject later in the same bar.

    4. The tenor carries the low line

      With the bass voice silent, the tenor takes the descending sequence. Soprano and alto exchange the earlier episode’s tied notes above it. The tenor’s final A♯ rises to B before recalling the B-minor subject; the bass then enters underneath in E minor, so the two low voices overlap.

    5. The last chord turns major

      The bass climbs chromatically to F♯ and holds it beneath the alto’s last subject fragment. The soprano’s B resolves to A♯, then rises to B as the bass moves F♯–B. In the alto, E falls to D♯. That raised third makes the final chord B major, held beneath a fermata.
