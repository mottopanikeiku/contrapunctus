Franz Liszt / III. Romanticism

Piano Sonata, S. 178

1853 · S. 178 · Piano

A few themes sustain one continuous movement, including a slow section and a fugato. The manuscript preserves the loud ending that Liszt replaced with a quiet close.

28 min

The recording

  1. Piano Sonata in B minor, S. 178Jorge Bolet · 1960
    27:36

    A hammered piano figure becomes a singing melody, then a fugue subject: Liszt builds his B minor Sonata from these transformations. Jorge Bolet plays the complete work in this 1960 recording, from the first low notes to the quiet ending.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Low Gs and silence

      Two low Gs, separated by rests, then a descending scale. Liszt marks the piano sotto voce, under the breath. The scale falls twice, with different intervals the second time; the sonata has begun without settling into B minor.

    2. A leap, then hammer blows

      The Allegro energico introduces two short ideas: a sudden octave leap with a tumbling triplet, then a repeated note struck like a hammer. Both will return in disguises. The repeated note will even learn to sing.

    3. A manuscript in black and red

      Liszt’s manuscript is written on green-blue paper, first in black ink. He adds articulation, dynamics and fingerings in red, then makes later changes in red crayon and pencil. Its many corrections and paste-overs show how long the Sonata occupied him.

    4. Grandioso in D major

      The Grandioso opens out in D major: broad piano chords and a melody with room to stride. Underneath, the repeated quavers recall the hammering heard earlier.

    5. The hammer learns to sing

      The piano softens into dolce con grazia, sweet and graceful. Soon the hammering figure becomes a melody marked cantando espressivo. Its notes last twice as long, and flowing triplets support them. The same little pattern now sounds tender.

    6. Peace to compose in Weimar

      At Weimar Liszt is court Kapellmeister, and his relationship with Princess Carolyne zu Sayn-Wittgenstein gives him peace to compose. The final versions of his two piano concertos and the Faust and Dante symphonies come from the same years.

    7. Agitato, then incalzando

      After the singing tune the music grows restless. The score marks the tune agitato, then drives on incalzando, pressing forward, faster, forceful and boisterous, until the slow Andante arrives.

    8. A first version in 1849

      Liszt allegedly presents an early version to a private Weimar circle in the summer of 1849, and the first surviving sketch dates from that year. Most of the work is done in 1852–53; the autograph is dated 2 February 1853.

    9. A dedication sent to Schumann

      The Sonata appears in 1854 with a dedication to Robert Schumann, who has dedicated his C major Fantasy to Liszt fifteen years earlier. By the time Liszt’s score reaches the Schumann household that May, Robert is in the sanatorium at Endenich.

    10. Clara’s diary

      Liszt sends the dedication copy to Clara Schumann on 21 May 1854. On 25 May she writes in her diary: “Brahms played them to me, but I became completely wretched. … It is nothing but blind noise.”

    11. A slow F♯-major song

      The Andante sostenuto settles into F♯ major and three beats to the bar. A quiet melody moves over soft piano chords. The earlier singing tune and the Grandioso will return within this slow section, gathering towards a broad F♯-major climax.

    12. Blank lines in the autograph

      In the autograph Liszt leaves a few blank lines before the Andante sostenuto, and again before the later Allegro energico fugato. Ernst Herttrich identifies them as a slow movement and a scherzo inside the single span.

    13. Wagner hears it from Klindworth

      On 5 April 1855 Karl Klindworth plays the Sonata to Wagner in London. That evening Wagner writes to Liszt: “beautiful beyond anything, grand and sweet, deep and noble, sublime as you are yourself”. “The London misery was forgotten all at once.”

    14. A climax, and the song returns

      The slow section reaches its own climax on a broad F♯-major chord. As it rings, a rising scale in the left hand leads the slow theme back, now sweet and lush, with the left hand flowing up and down the scale.

    15. No programme, but Faust

      Liszt gives the Sonata no written programme. Numerous commentators nonetheless read it as another musical version of the Faust tale, which attracted many Romantic composers; Liszt used the same techniques in his Faust Symphony, begun in 1854.

    16. Where does the fugato belong?

      A fugato is coming, and scholars disagree about its place. Alan Walker calls it “both a third movement and an extended leadback into the recapitulation”. Kenneth Hamilton calls it development, part of a non-tonic recapitulation or a scherzo, “or not, depending on your reaction to this stroke of genius”.

    17. Two figures become a fugue

      In B♭ minor, the piano sets the leaping figure and the repeated-note figure running through a fugato. One voice begins; another answers while the first keeps going. The opening’s outbursts now fit together as counterpoint.

    18. Bülow takes it to Berlin

      On 22 January 1857, Hans von Bülow gives the Sonata its first public performance, at a trio evening in Berlin’s Englisches Haus. He has studied with Liszt since 1851. Later that year he marries Liszt’s daughter Cosima, with her father at the wedding.

    19. The opening Allegro comes home

      The fugato leads back to B minor and the opening Allegro’s main theme. Liszt compresses the return: the Grandioso and the singing melody reappear in a much shorter span. The piano still has a Prestissimo and a final grand statement ahead.

    20. A pupil of a pupil

      Jorge Bolet studied with Moriz Rosenthal, a renowned pupil of Liszt. In 1960, the year of this recording, he supplies the piano soundtrack for the film Song Without End, in which Dirk Bogarde plays Liszt.

    21. Prestissimo, then silence

      An exuberant Prestissimo drives towards a triumphant return of the Grandioso melody and the last climax. Alan Walker stresses that the silence after that climax must be long enough.

    22. Liszt crosses out the flourish

      After the grand climax, the piano returns to Andante sostenuto. Liszt’s manuscript contains a louder ending, crossed out. In its place come quiet recollections: the repeated-note figure, the leap and finally the falling scale, reversing the order in which they first appeared.

    23. “Highly delighted”

      Liszt answers Wagner from Weimar on 2 May 1855: “Your letter about the sonata has highly delighted me, and you must excuse me for not having thanked you at once.”

    How it is joined

    1. The piano breaks into octaves

      The low Gs and falling scales of Lento assai lead to Allegro energico at bar 8. The piano leaps in octaves, tumbles through a triplet and then strikes a repeated note. B minor becomes firmly established later, at bar 32.

    2. Falling bass scales open D major

      The opening’s descending scale returns in the piano’s bass beneath the hammering figure. Repetition drives towards D major, the relative major of B minor. At Grandioso, broad chords carry the new melody while the left hand continues in repeated quavers.

    3. Hammer blows become a melody

      After the D-major Grandioso, the piano recalls the principal theme and softens into dolce con grazia. At the later cantando espressivo, Liszt doubles the repeated-note figure’s note values. Triplets in the accompaniment support the new singing line.

    4. Three slow beats in F♯ major

      At Andante sostenuto, the piano turns to F♯ major and 3/4. The right hand carries a quiet melody over gently moving chords, with low notes supporting each change. The slow section will bring back both the singing theme and the Grandioso.

    5. Quiet music starts to chase itself

      The slow section leads to Allegro energico in B♭ minor. The piano’s lower voice takes up the leaping and repeated-note figures; another voice answers above it. Short staccato notes turn the old hammer blows into a running fugue subject.

    6. The fugato reaches B minor

      The fugato leads back to B minor. At bar 533 the piano restores the principal theme first heard at bar 32, reuniting its leaping octaves and repeated-note figure. The Grandioso and singing melody follow in shortened versions.

    7. A silence before the final Andante

      The grand piano climax at bar 710 is followed by silence, then Andante sostenuto. The quiet melody returns before Liszt recalls the opening’s repeated-note figure, octave leap and descending scale. Those three ideas now arrive in reverse order.
