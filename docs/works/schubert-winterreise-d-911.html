Franz Schubert / III. Romanticism

Winterreise

1827 · D. 911 · Voice and piano; twenty-four poems by Wilhelm Müller

Schubert set twelve poems in February, then added twelve more in the autumn. The walking chords of Gute Nacht are eventually reduced to a hurdy-gurdy’s bare fifth in Der Leiermann.

4 recordings · 16 min

In the Hall

  1. 1. Gute NachtHans Duhan, baritone; Ferdinand Foll, piano · April–May 1928
    4:09

    Gute Nacht, the first song of Winterreise, D. 911, follows a man leaving his lover’s house in the snow, walking in step with the piano. In Hans Duhan’s recording the first verse goes straight to the third, and the final verse turns briefly to the major as he closes the door.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Piano introduction

      Mässig, in gehender Bewegung — moderate, with a walking motion. The piano plays repeated quaver chords before the voice enters.

    2. Opening couplet, Gute Nacht

      “Fremd bin ich eingezogen, / Fremd zieh’ ich wieder aus.” (I came here a stranger; a stranger I leave.) Müller placed the same word at the start of both lines. Schubert’s melody falls over the piano’s steady steps.

    3. Third verse, second omitted

      Duhan goes straight to the third verse and leaves out the second. That verse contains the line “Die Liebe liebt das Wandern” (love loves to wander).

    4. Joseph von Spaun’s recollection

      Joseph von Spaun recalled that Schubert asked his friends to come to Schober’s, where he would sing them “a cycle of horrifying songs”. Schubert then sang the whole of Winterreise in an emotional voice, and his friends were dumbfounded by its sombre mood.

    5. Final verse in D major

      At bar 71 the last verse moves from D minor to D major. So as not to wake her, he closes the door softly and writes goodnight on the gate. The piano keeps up its quaver chords underneath.

    6. First complete recording, HMV, 1928

      Hans Duhan’s Winterreise was the earliest complete recording, made for HMV in 1928, the centenary of Schubert’s death. He recorded nineteen songs with Ferdinand Foll on 27 April, 2 May and 4 May, and the other five with Lene Orthmann in July.

    7. Piano postlude in D minor

      After an un poco ritard. in bar 98, the a tempo at bar 99 restores the walking pace and the music returns to D minor. The voice’s final note gives way to six bars of piano alone, bars 100–105.

    Joins

    1. Voice enters after six-bar introduction

      The piano plays six bars of minor-key chords in steady quavers. The singer enters with a pickup at the end of bar 7 and a descending melody. Under the voice the piano continues its quavers at the same tempo.

    2. Third verse follows the first

      Five bars of piano lead into the third verse over the same minor-key walking chords. Duhan left out the repeat that would carry the second stanza, so the song moves straight from the stanza about remembered love to the stanza asking why he should stay.

    3. D minor to D major, bar 71

      The piano plays five bars alone, and at bar 71 the last stanza begins pianissimo in the major. Schubert changes the key from D minor to D major, raising F to F♯. The quaver chords continue at the same pace. In this stanza the singer leaves without waking his beloved.

    4. D minor returns under the last vocal note

      The last sung phrase is marked un poco ritardando. At a tempo the original pulse returns, and D minor returns under the voice’s final note. The piano then plays alone for six bars and ends on the D minor chord.

  2. 5. Der LindenbaumHans Duhan, baritone; Ferdinand Foll, piano · April–May 1928
    4:16

    “Der Lindenbaum” is the fifth song of Schubert’s Winterreise, D. 911, and concerns a linden tree by a well, linked with sweet dreams and with words carved into its bark. The piano depicts the rustling branches as the traveller passes the tree in the dark and is tempted by its offer of rest.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Piano introduction

      The piano plays fast figures for seven bars in E major before the singer enters. Schubert marks them pianississimo. Related piano figures return in the stanza about the cold wind.

    2. Linden tree by the well

      “Am Brunnen vor dem Tore”: by the well outside the gate. A linden tree stands there, and the traveller has many memories of it. He dreams in its shade, carves loving words into its bark and returns to it in joy and in sorrow.

    3. Third stanza in E minor

      The key signature changes to E minor at bar 25, and the third stanza begins at bar 28. The traveller passes the tree at night and closes his eyes, though it is already dark. The piano’s rustling figure, heard earlier in the major, is now in the minor.

    4. Schober on “Der Lindenbaum”

      After Schubert sang the cycle to his friends, Schober said that of all the songs only “Der Lindenbaum” had pleased him. Schubert answered: “I like these songs more than all the others, and you will come to like them too.”

    5. Fifth stanza, bars 45–53

      At bar 45 a low C♮, marked fz, takes the piano away from E major. In the fifth stanza the cold wind blows into his face and blows his hat off. He sings “Ich wendete mich nicht” (I did not turn back) as the piano plays chromatic figures.

    6. Final verse, major key

      The major-key tune returns at bar 58 for the final verse. The wanderer is now hours away, but he still hears the branches tell him “Du fändest Ruhe dort”, that he would find rest there. Schubert repeats this closing line through bar 76.

    7. Piano postlude, bars 77–82

      In the postlude (bars 77–82) the piano brings back the material of its introduction. It closes pp on a final chord of E major.

    Joins

    1. Rustling figure returns in E minor

      The first two stanzas end in E major. At bar 25 the key signature changes to E minor, and the piano’s rustling figure returns with G♮ in place of G♯. The piano plays alone for three bars. The voice re-enters with a pickup in bar 28, as the traveller passes the tree in darkness.

    2. Return to E major, fourth stanza

      The voice goes straight on into the next stanza. At bar 37 the four-sharp signature returns, and the piano plays B–D♯–F♯, the dominant of E, as it sets the tree’s invitation. In Müller’s fourth stanza the branches offer the traveller rest.

    3. Low C at the cold-wind stanza

      In bars 45–53 the piano plays a low C♮, marked forzando (fz) in bars 45 and 47, against the E-major context. G♮ also enters the piano’s rapid figures. The voice moves from the smooth tune to the harsher phrases of the cold-wind stanza and takes up C♮.

    4. Piano interlude over a low B

      The voice stops, and the piano plays a four-bar interlude over a low B in the bass (bars 53–58). The rapid figures diminish under a diminuendo. The last stanza begins pianississimo, with the opening tune back in E major, as the traveller remembers the distant tree.

    5. Final vocal E and piano postlude

      The voice ends its repeated invitation to rest on E. The piano then takes up the fast figures of the introduction again, marked pianissimo, and plays them through to the final dominant–tonic cadence in E major, from a B-major chord to E major (bars 76–82).

  3. 11. FrühlingstraumHans Duhan, baritone; Ferdinand Foll, piano · April–May 1928
    4:19

    In Frühlingstraum, the eleventh song of Schubert’s Winterreise, D. 911, a dream of May flowers and a lover’s embrace ends at a cockcrow. Hans Duhan and Ferdinand Foll perform it.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. First stanza, A major

      The piano begins pp in A major and 6/8, marked Etwas bewegt. The singer dreams of bright flowers, green meadows and noisy birds. Schubert sets this imaginary May morning to an easy, dance-like step.

    2. Second stanza, Schnell

      “Da war es kalt und finster”: it was cold and dark. A cock’s crow has woken the wanderer, and ravens shriek on the roof. Schubert marks the passage Schnell (fast) and replaces the swaying piano figure with short chords separated by rests.

    3. Third stanza, Langsam

      At bar 27 the tempo changes to Langsam in 2/4. The piano repeats chords over a sustained A while the singer looks at the leaves painted by frost on his window, the flowers of his dream. He imagines people laughing at a man who sees flowers in winter.

    4. Return of the opening music

      At bar 44, marked Wie oben, the piano brings back the opening 6/8; the voice enters at bar 48. In this stanza the dream contains a young woman, kisses and love returned.

    5. Frühlingstraum, longest side, 1928

      At 4:11, Frühlingstraum is the longest song in Duhan and Foll’s 1928 set, which HMV issued as twelve records with each song on one side. A 12-inch shellac side held a little over four minutes.

    6. Second awakening, bar 58

      At bar 58, marked Schnell, the cockcrow returns with the same fast, clipped accompaniment. At the first awakening his eye opens; now his heart wakes. He sits alone and thinks about the dream.

    7. Final stanza

      “Noch schlägt das Herz so warm” — the heart still beats so warmly. The singer closes his eyes and asks when the leaves on the window will turn green and when he will hold his beloved again. Schubert repeats the last question and ends in the minor.

    Joins

    1. Piano introduction to first vocal entry

      The piano plays soft broken chords in 6/8. The voice enters on the last quaver of bar 4 in the same pulse. Schubert’s accompaniment stays in A major as the singer begins the dream of flowers.

    2. The change to Schnell

      The last note of the dream section is followed directly by the marking Schnell. The piano changes from flowing broken chords to short, pointed strokes with rests between them. The voice tells of the cockcrow, the cold room and ravens on the roof.

    3. Slow chords at the window

      At bar 27 the marking changes from Schnell to Langsam and the metre from 6/8 to 2/4. Through bars 27–30 the piano plays repeated chords, pp, around a held note while the singer looks at the window. In Schubert’s original key the A is held in both hands and the bass begins on A.

    4. Return to the opening tempo

      At bar 44 the marking Wie oben restores the opening tempo and 6/8. The piano plays four bars of the A-major music from the opening. The voice re-enters at bar 48 with Müller’s fourth stanza, a dream of kisses and returned love.

    5. The clipped chords return

      Schnell returns at bar 58. The piano resumes its short chords and rests, as it did at the end of the first dream, and they break off the second dream. In the fifth stanza Müller gave the awakening to the heart.

    6. The slow chords return

      Langsam and 2/4 return with the piano’s quiet repeated chords (bars 71–88). The singer closes his eyes. The question about holding his beloved is repeated, and the final chord turns Schubert’s opening A major to A minor.

  4. 24. Der LeiermannHans Duhan, baritone; Ferdinand Foll, piano · April–May 1928
    3:16

    “Der Leiermann” is the twenty-fourth and last song of Schubert’s Winterreise, D. 911. An old man stands barefoot on the ice and turns his hurdy-gurdy, with an empty plate at his feet. The piano holds a bare fifth, and the traveller asks whether they might go on together.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Introduction, open-fifth drone

      The piano holds a bare fifth in the bass. Above it, a short right-hand figure repeats, imitating the hurdy-gurdy’s wheel and drones. The singer enters after eight bars.

    2. The hurdy-gurdy man on the ice

      The old man sways barefoot on the ice, and his small plate stays empty. Müller gave him frozen fingers, no audience and no earnings. The piano repeats its tune throughout.

    3. Third stanza, bars 31–38

      “Keiner mag ihn hören, / Keiner sieht ihn an”: nobody cares to hear him, nobody looks at him. The dogs growl around the player. The same bare fifth continues under the voice.

    4. Final stanza, bars 53–58

      “Wunderlicher Alter” (strange old man). The traveller addresses the hurdy-gurdy man directly for the first time and asks whether he may go with him and whether the hurdy-gurdy will accompany his songs. The voice rises an octave on the last question, in bars 56–57, and the piano holds its drone.

    5. Piano postlude, bars 58–61

      The voice rests from bar 59. The piano repeats the opening figure and ends on a minor chord over the same drone. Müller’s poem ends with the traveller’s questions.

    Joins

    1. The voice enters over the drone

      The voice enters after eight piano bars. The left hand keeps its open fifth, and the right hand’s turning figure fills the spaces between the vocal phrases. In Schubert’s original key of A minor the drone is A–E, with no third.

    2. Piano interlude into the third stanza

      After the repeated lines about the empty plate, the piano plays four bars alone. At bar 31 the voice returns with the same melody and the words about nobody listening. The bass fifth continues across the join.

    3. Piano interlude after the fourth stanza

      The fourth stanza ends, and the voice rests for four bars (bars 49–52). The piano repeats the interlude from bars 27–30. Its small right-hand figure turns above the open fifth while a short line moves beneath it in the left hand.

    4. The singer addresses the old man

      The singer returns over the drone in bars 53–58 with repeated notes and speaks to the old man directly. The last line climbs an octave as he asks the old man to accompany his songs. The bass stays fixed beneath the rising voice.

    5. Closing bars: the opening figure returns

      In bars 58–61, the piano begins its opening figure again while the singer holds his last note. The voice then stops, and the piano is marked piano in bar 59. The same bass fifth continues as a drone beneath the final minor chord.
