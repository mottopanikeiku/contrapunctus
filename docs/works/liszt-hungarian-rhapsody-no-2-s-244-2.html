Franz Liszt / III. Romanticism

Hungarian Rhapsody No. 2, S. 244/2

1851 · S. 244/2 · Piano

A slow lassú gives way to the quick friss. The piano imitates a band’s flourishes, pauses and gathering speed.

13 min

The recording

  1. Hungarian Rhapsody No. 2, S. 244/2Martha Goldstein
    13:06

    Liszt’s Second Hungarian Rhapsody begins with a grave piano summons and ends in a rush of F♯-major octaves. Martha Goldstein plays the whole piece, including the long slow half that makes the dance feel so headlong.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Major first, then minor

      The piano opens on a C♯-major chord, although C♯ minor is the slow section’s home. Within six bars the minor has taken hold. This is the Lassan, the rhapsody’s slow half, with the freedom of a speaker who can stop in the middle of a sentence.

    2. A tune in Liszt’s notebook

      In 1846 Liszt writes down the opening tune after hearing someone play it. Five years later it appears at the head of this rhapsody, published by Bartholf Senff and dedicated to Count László Teleki.

    3. Minor and major

      The Lassan keeps turning from C♯ minor towards C♯ major and E major. Its grave phrases have bright, capricious neighbours. Liszt saves the steady dancing pulse for the second half.

    4. Songs played by Romani bands

      Liszt’s rhapsodies bring the songs and dances played by Romani bands to the concert piano. The musicians improvise on melodies that include published popular songs. Liszt even intends his book about Romani music in Hungary as a preface to the rhapsodies.

    5. The dance starts quietly

      The Friska begins in F♯ minor, quietly, on a C♯-major chord. A tune remembered from the Lassan acquires a dancing step. Tonic and dominant chords alternate beneath it as the music gathers speed towards the main tune in F♯ major.

    6. A pupil gets another ending

      In his last years Liszt writes a cadenza and a new coda for his pupil Toni Raab. For Lina Schmalhausen he supplies further additions. Even this much-played rhapsody remains something he can reshape at the piano with a pupil beside him.

    7. Room for the pianist

      Near the end, F♯ minor returns and the dance pauses over a recollection of the Lassan. Liszt writes Cadenza ad libitum: the pianist may add a cadenza here. Rachmaninoff and d’Albert both take up the invitation and write their own.

    8. Octaves across the keyboard

      The final Prestissimo is in F♯ major. Octaves climb and plunge across almost the whole keyboard, with a crescendo driving them towards the closing chords. The slow opening’s C♯ has become the dominant of this new home.

    How it is joined

    1. C♯ becomes the dance’s dominant

      The Lassan’s C♯-minor world opens into F♯ minor. The Friska starts quietly on C♯ major, now the dominant chord, and recalls a tune from the slow section. Repeated piano figures establish the dance while tonic and dominant alternate underneath.

    2. The Lassan interrupts the dance

      Before the final Prestissimo, the piano turns back to F♯ minor and recalls another tune from the Lassan. The dance relaxes into this slower recollection; Liszt then leaves space for a cadenza with the marking Cadenza ad libitum.

    3. Prestissimo in F♯ major

      After the space for a cadenza, Liszt restores F♯ major and marks the piano Prestissimo. Rising and falling octaves fill the keyboard. The crescendo continues into the final chords in F♯ major.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. Opening disc side onlySergei Rachmaninoff, piano · 1919
    4:09

    Rachmaninoff plays the opening of Liszt’s Second Hungarian Rhapsody in his first year of making gramophone records, 1919. This is the first side of Edison Diamond Disc 82169, with the slow Lassan’s shifts between major and minor.

    Notes as it plays
    1. C♯ major opens the door

      Liszt begins on a C♯-major chord, then settles into C♯ minor. The piano seems to speak in the slow Lassan, taking time over its phrases. The fast Friska belongs to the later part of the complete rhapsody.

    2. Major beside minor

      Within the Lassan, Liszt turns repeatedly towards C♯ major and E major. The piano’s grave opening has lighter, capricious companions.

    3. Ten sides for Edison

      In 1919 Rachmaninoff begins recording for Edison. The contract allows ten released sides, and he plays a Lauter concert grand made in Newark. He asks to approve his performances before release; Edison nevertheless issues different takes of the same pieces.

    4. Rachmaninoff meets the Philadelphia sound

      Rachmaninoff later records his own Second Piano Concerto with Stokowski and the Philadelphia Orchestra, in 1924 and again in 1929.

  2. Opening disc side onlyPhiladelphia Orchestra, Leopold Stokowski (arr. Müller-Berghaus) · 1936
    4:11

    Stokowski and the Philadelphia Orchestra play Liszt’s Second Hungarian Rhapsody in Karl Müller-Berghaus’s orchestral arrangement. This opening side of their 1936 recording turns the piano’s slow declamation into a public speech.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A slow orchestral summons

      The Lassan, Liszt’s slow first half, begins with a brief introduction before settling into its minor key. Grave phrases alternate with lighter turns. In this arrangement, the orchestra takes over the changing characters of the piano original.

    2. Three scores, three keys

      The piano original’s home key is C♯ minor. Liszt’s orchestral version, made with Franz Doppler, moves it up to D minor; Müller-Berghaus takes it down to C minor.

    3. Stokowski puts down the baton

      Late in the 1929–30 season, Stokowski begins conducting without a baton. In Philadelphia he experiments with seating, bowing and even the lights: at one concert only his head and hands are illuminated in the darkened hall.

  3. Opening disc side onlyNational Symphony Orchestra of London, Walter Goehr · 1945
    4:55

    Walter Goehr brings Liszt’s Second Hungarian Rhapsody to Kingsway Hall with the National Symphony Orchestra of London in 1945. This opening disc side turns the piano’s calls and replies into an orchestral conversation.

    Notes as it plays
    1. The slow opening speaks

      Liszt begins with the Lassan, the rhapsody’s slow half. Grave phrases stand beside capricious little turns, long before the fast dance takes over. Goehr has an orchestra to shape these changes of character, where Liszt’s original has one pianist.

    2. Schoenberg’s pupil in London

      Goehr studies with Schoenberg in Berlin and works for Berlin Radio. Forced as a Jewish musician to seek work outside Germany, he moves to London to conduct for His Master’s Voice. His record labels sometimes call him George Walter.

    3. Liszt writes down a tune

      The rhapsody’s opening melody appears in Liszt’s sketchbook in 1846, noted down after he hears it played. He publishes the piano work in 1851. Later, working with Franz Doppler, he makes an orchestral version in D minor.
