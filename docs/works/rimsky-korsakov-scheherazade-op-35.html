Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov / III. Romanticism

Scheherazade

1888 · Op. 35 · Orchestra with prominent solo violin

Across four movements drawing on The Thousand and One Nights, the solo violin keeps returning to the storyteller. The orchestral themes migrate between episodes, so a recognised melody may arrive in quite unfamiliar circumstances.

4 recordings · 39 min

In the Hall

  1. I. The Sea and Sinbad’s ShipSan Francisco Symphony Orchestra, Pierre Monteux; Naoum Blinder, violin · 3–4 March 1942
    8:19

    A stern phrase for brass and strings, answered by one violin over a harp: the Sultan and Scheherazade are already in the room. Pierre Monteux conducts the San Francisco Symphony, with Naoum Blinder as the storyteller.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The Sultan’s first word

      Brass, low woodwinds and strings deliver a descending phrase in octaves. Largo e maestoso — broad and majestic. The strings press heavily into their notes, and pauses break up the phrase before the winds answer softly.

    2. One violin answers

      Scheherazade’s violin winds above the harp. The score marks it Recitativo: a singer’s freedom, with no words. After the orchestra’s heavy opening, Naoum Blinder has room to turn a single phrase into a tale.

    3. The strings make waves

      Allegro non troppo. The strings begin their rolling accompaniment, and the Sultan’s stern phrase becomes the broad sea tune. Its long notes ride over the quicker motion below; the same melody has put out to sea.

    4. Three years at sea

      At twelve, Rimsky-Korsakov enters naval college. He begins his First Symphony before a three-year voyage, then returns to a life divided between music and the navy. For years his jobs include inspecting naval bands.

    5. A story buys another night

      In The Thousand and One Nights, Scheherazade keeps her husband waiting for the end of each story. The Sultan postpones her execution to hear what happens next. Rimsky-Korsakov gives this woman one violin against a whole orchestra.

    6. San Francisco, March 1942

      Monteux and the San Francisco Symphony make this recording at the War Memorial Opera House on 3–4 March 1942. Naoum Blinder plays the violin solos. The complete suite fills Victor’s 78-rpm album DM-920.

    7. The sea tune returns

      The broad sea tune returns over the rolling strings. Brass and strings enlarge a melody that began as the Sultan’s solitary command. The accompaniment keeps rising and falling beneath its long notes.

    8. E major, at rest

      The closing pages settle into E major. Small solo phrases remain among the strings and winds; at the very end a cello’s rising and falling figure rests beneath held wind chords. The strings finish with soft plucked notes.

    How it is joined

    1. Wind chords leave room for violin

      The heavy opening stops. Soft wind chords, separated by fermatas, prepare the violin’s Lento recitative. Harp chords replace the orchestral bass, and the violin climbs into its winding, espressivo line.

    2. The strings begin to roll

      A cadenza and held violin note end the recitative. At Allegro non troppo, the key signature changes to E minor. Violas and cellos begin their rolling figures; the violins take up the broad tune above them, with winds sustaining the harmony.

  2. II. The Story of the Kalender PrinceSan Francisco Symphony Orchestra, Pierre Monteux; Naoum Blinder, violin · 3–4 March 1942
    10:26

    Scheherazade’s violin introduces a bassoon with a tale of its own. Its winding B-minor melody passes to the oboe and strings, while brass fanfares keep interrupting.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The violin returns

      The solo violin begins another winding recitative over harp chords. Scheherazade’s voice is familiar from the first movement. Her cadenza slows before a quite different soloist takes over.

    2. Bassoon and four double basses

      A bassoon begins in B minor, dolce ed espressivo — sweetly and expressively. Four muted double basses hold a quiet foundation beneath it. The tune mixes little dancing figures with longer notes, as though its teller keeps stopping to remember a detail.

    3. A violinist who taught Stern

      The solo violin is Naoum Blinder, concertmaster of the San Francisco Symphony. He reaches the United States in 1928 with the banker Felix Warburg’s sponsorship and teaches many violinists, Isaac Stern among them.

    4. The tune in new colours

      The bassoon’s tune returns again and again in new colours. Strings shimmer in tremolo, a thrumming accompaniment turns beneath it, and sharper sounds prick the texture.

    5. The professor teaches himself

      In 1871 Rimsky-Korsakov accepts a professorship at the Saint Petersburg Conservatory. He is twenty-seven. Alongside teaching, he sets about learning the harmony and counterpoint his own training has left out.

    6. The brass breaks in

      The long melody meets short, fierce brass calls. The new idea grows out of the Sultan’s phrase from the first movement. Horns, trumpets and trombones turn it into a march.

    7. Not the Sultan

      The unison phrase that opened the suite for the stern Sultan returns in this movement’s brass. Rimsky-Korsakov says it serves here as musical material alone: in the Kalender’s tale, “there cannot, however, be any mention of Sultan Shakhriar.”

    8. Clarinet over plucked strings

      A clarinet stretches the tune into a free solo. The strings pluck repeated chords beneath it, quickening and slowing with the soloist. The score asks the clarinet to begin lento, lungo — slowly, at length — before accelerating.

    9. A fanfare that sails on

      The sharp fanfare for muted trumpet and trombone first sounds in this movement. Rimsky-Korsakov notes that it returns in the finale “in the delineation of the wrecking ship”, though that episode has no connection with the Kalender’s tale.

    10. The bassoon’s second tale

      The bassoon returns in a cadenza, answering the earlier clarinet solo. Its familiar tune opens the movement’s last large section. The same little figures now have the brass march behind them.

    11. Towards the B-major chord

      Short woodwind phrases prepare the final rush. The last page is marked Animato: strings race beneath brass chords, with timpani reinforcing the bass. A sharp final chord brings the B-minor tale to a B-major end.

    How it is joined

    1. The violin leaves a bassoon

      The violin’s cadenza slows, marked ritardando assai. At Andantino, the bassoon begins its B-minor tune above four muted double basses. The violin’s high ending hangs briefly over the new, much lower voice.

    2. The Sultan’s phrase becomes a march

      A figure first plucked by the low strings grows into brass fanfares. Its kinship with the Sultan’s motto links the interruption to the first movement. Horns, trumpets and trombones exchange short calls while the sustained woodwinds and strings support them.

    3. Plucked strings under a free clarinet

      The orchestral chord stops at a fermata. At Moderato assai, a solo clarinet takes up a free recitative. The strings pluck short repeated chords, following the soloist’s slowing and acceleration. Their soft attacks leave the clarinet’s long notes exposed.

  3. III. The Young Prince and the Young PrincessSan Francisco Symphony Orchestra, Pierre Monteux; Naoum Blinder, violin · 3–4 March 1942
    8:16

    The two violin sections sing a long G-major melody, then the woodwinds turn it into a dance with a snare drum. Scheherazade’s own solo violin slips into the lovers’ scene before the end.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A song for the violins

      The violins begin alone with the lower strings, singing in G major. Long notes open into small runs, then settle again. The marking is Andantino quasi allegretto: the tune has a gentle walking pace, even when its decorations hurry.

    2. A summer’s work

      Rimsky-Korsakov writes Scheherazade in the summer of 1888. That November he conducts its first performance in Saint Petersburg. The composer who once divided his time between a ship and a symphony now stands before the whole orchestra.

    3. A composer in the navy

      In 1856, aged twelve, Rimsky-Korsakov is taken to St Petersburg to enter the naval school. By 1861 Balakirev has him writing a symphony. In 1862 the new midshipman sails on the clipper Almaz; the voyage, he recalls, lasts two years and eight months, and music is “wholly forgotten”.

    4. The tune starts dancing

      Flute and clarinet shorten the long melody into little dancing phrases. The strings pluck beneath them, and the snare drum gives the beat a crisp edge. The flute is marked ben marcato staccatissimo: very short notes, clearly marked.

    5. “Fairy-tale wonders”

      In My Musical Life, published in 1909, Rimsky-Korsakov says he wanted the hearer to carry away “an oriental narrative of some numerous and varied fairy-tale wonders”. The prince and princess belong to that imagined world; the music leaves their story open.

    6. A theme in another story

      Rimsky-Korsakov has no patience with listeners who pin one meaning on each theme. “In vain do people seek in my suite leading motives linked unbrokenly with ever the same poetic ideas and conceptions,” he writes. The Princess’s clarinet theme returns at the Baghdad festival, where the Arabian Nights has no place for her.

    7. The broad song returns

      A trumpet-led phrase stretches the dance, then the opening tempo returns. The violin sections recover their long G-major tune. The woodwinds leave more space around it, and the strings can sing in longer breaths again.

    8. Scheherazade interrupts her tale

      One violin takes over, with a few harp chords beneath it. The score says Orchestre tacet: the orchestra falls silent. Scheherazade’s winding recitative returns, then becomes a cadenza before the woodwinds begin to answer.

    9. The strings rejoin

      Allargando assai — broadening greatly. The violin sections take over from the soloist, and the horns remove their mutes. The melody opens into a full orchestral phrase before the solo violin decorates it again.

    10. San Francisco, March 1942

      Pierre Monteux conducts the San Francisco Symphony in the War Memorial Opera House on 3 and 4 March 1942. Victor issues the recording as the 78-rpm album DM-920.

    11. A handful of small sounds

      The closing music breaks into little woodwind runs above plucked strings and soft percussion. On the last page, flute, oboe and bassoon pass the quick notes downwards. The strings end with harmonics, small bright sounds over the final G.

    How it is joined

    1. Plucked strings and a snare drum

      The broad string melody shortens into the Allegretto dance. Flute and clarinet take the tune, with the flute marked staccatissimo. Muted strings pluck beneath them, and the snare drum taps out its rhythm. The bass leaves space between its notes.

    2. The violins recover their long tune

      A broad trumpet-led phrase stretches the dance. At Come prima, the first tempo returns, with the G-major melody in both violin sections. The lower strings sustain their notes again while woodwinds hold quiet chords above.

    3. The orchestra stops for one violin

      The string song thins into an oboe phrase over muted horns, then stops at a fermata. Orchestre tacet: only solo violin and harp remain. The violin begins its Lento recitative, recalling the voice that opened the suite.

    4. The horns take off their mutes

      The solo violin’s phrase gathers the woodwinds around it. At Allargando assai, the violin sections rejoin with their bows, the horns remove their mutes, and timpani support the expanded phrase. A fermata holds it open before the solo violin starts decorating again.

    5. The song breaks into little runs

      The long orchestral phrase releases into short woodwind figures. Plucked strings keep the bass light; triangle, tambourine and snare drum add soft taps around the melody. The final runs pass down the winds before the strings settle in G major.

  4. IV. Festival at Baghdad — The Sea — The Ship Breaks against the CliffSan Francisco Symphony Orchestra, Pierre Monteux; Naoum Blinder, violin · 3–4 March 1942
    11:41

    A crowded festival spills into a storm at sea, and the suite’s old melodies collide. After the wreck, Rimsky-Korsakov leaves Scheherazade’s violin suspended high above a quiet E-major chord.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The orchestra challenges the violin

      Oboes, clarinets, bassoons and strings launch the Sultan’s phrase. It stops at a silence, leaving the solo violin to answer. The next orchestral interruption brings in brass and percussion; Scheherazade answers that one too.

    2. The Sultan reaches for the bass drum

      Rimsky-Korsakov has saved his heaviest percussion for this movement. Across the suite he adds timpani first, then cymbals and triangle, then snare drum and tambourine. Now the Sultan, gruffly impatient, gets the first whack on the bass drum; the tam-tam waits for the wreck.

    3. Flute above the dancers

      At Vivo, the strings begin a quick repeated pulse. A flute runs above it, starting the festival dance. The tune keeps moving while other instruments enter around it: clarinet, bassoon, then the bright tap of the triangle.

    4. The lovers join the crowd

      The lighter dance from the prince-and-princess movement returns in the festival. Its familiar little phrases fit into the faster music. The finale keeps finding room for people and tunes from the earlier tales.

    5. Guests from other tales

      Rimsky-Korsakov points out that the Kalender’s principal tune and the Princess’s clarinet theme return as secondary themes of the Baghdad festival. Yet “nothing is said in The Arabian Nights about these persons taking part in the festivities.”

    6. A summerhouse full of fingerings

      In 1873 Rimsky-Korsakov is appointed Inspector of Music Bands of the Navy. That summer he obtains a trombone, a clarinet and a flute and works out their fingerings at his summerhouse in Pargolovo, playing them “for all the neighbours to hear”. The post is abolished in 1884.

    7. The Kalendar’s brass calls

      The Kalendar’s fanfares enter the festival music. Short brass calls that once interrupted a bassoon’s tale now jostle with the quick dance. The strings keep their feet moving beneath them.

    8. The sea engulfs the festival

      The sea music from the first movement returns, broad and heavy, over the rolling strings. The marking becomes Allegro non troppo maestoso. Woodwind runs and harp sweeps cross the waves while brass and percussion gather towards the shipwreck.

    9. The ship shudders and breaks

      The scene cuts like a film to Sinbad’s storm-tossed ship. Its theme stutters in the basses, and the ship shudders and breaks on a stroke of the tam-tam. A stunned calm follows.

    10. One thousand and one nights

      Scheherazade keeps the Sultan waiting for the end of each story. By the thousand-and-first night, he has fallen in love with her and abandons his plan to kill her. The suite’s last pages give his once-threatening phrase a quiet voice.

    11. After the wreck

      The epilogue makes room for quiet solo voices. A clarinet traces the waves above soft strings; later, violin and harp return to Scheherazade’s recitative. The final cadenza rises away from the orchestra.

    12. Bob Varney’s transfer

      Bob Varney transferred Victor’s 78-rpm album DM-920 to digital, and the recording reached the Internet Archive in 2009. The Wikimedia Commons file describes it as restored from the original set.

    13. A violin above E major

      The violin reaches a high E and hangs above the orchestra. Woodwinds, horns and harp hold the quiet harmony beneath it. The last string chords are plucked; the Sultan’s heavy opening has become an E-major farewell.

    How it is joined

    1. The violin releases the dance

      The solo violin’s second recitative slows over held cellos and basses. At Vivo, horns, percussion and strings establish a quick pulse. The flute enters above it with the festival tune, while repeated viola notes keep the dance moving.

    2. The dance opens onto the sea

      The short dance figures give way to Allegro non troppo maestoso. Violins and cellos recover the first movement’s rolling sea tune, with the other strings repeating their accompaniment beneath it. The winds and brass sustain broad chords; percussion gives the returning waves their weight.

    3. The brass fades after the wreck

      A tam-tam stroke crowns the shipwreck. Low strings and timpani continue beneath the brass, whose held chords are marked morendo — dying away. At Poco più tranquillo, the clarinet takes up the wave figure over soft strings and a sustained bass.

    4. The violin settles above E major

      The violin’s cadenza climbs into the high register. At Alla breve, Tempo come I, its held E floats over the strings’ quiet recollection of the sea. Woodwinds and horns join the closing E-major harmony, and plucked string chords leave the violin hanging above them.

  1. Opening disc side onlyPhiladelphia Orchestra, Leopold Stokowski · 12 November 1934
    3:48

    Leopold Stokowski and the Philadelphia Orchestra begin the young lovers’ movement with a long G-major violin tune, threaded with quick woodwind runs. This 1934 recording preserves the opening disc side.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Two violin sections sing

      The violins begin in G major above held lower strings. Their tune alternates long notes with small, quick runs. Rimsky-Korsakov marks it Andantino quasi allegretto, giving the lovers’ music a gentle walking pace.

    2. Philadelphia, November 1934

      On 12 November 1934, Stokowski records Scheherazade with the Philadelphia Orchestra. This side contains the beginning of the third movement. Its label gives the tempo, Andantino quasi allegretto, and calls it ‘Part 1’.

    3. Small notes around a song

      The string melody leaves room for quick woodwind decorations. A run can rush past while the tune holds one note, then the longer phrase takes over again. The instruments move at different speeds within the same gentle pulse.

    4. A story across disc sides

      The recording reaches listeners in 1935, divided into short sides. This one stops before the movement’s ending. The lovers’ music continues on the next side, towards the return of Scheherazade’s solo violin and the quiet close.
