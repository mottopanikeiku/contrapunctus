Ludwig van Beethoven / II. Classical

String Quartet No. 14, Op. 131

1826 · Two violins, viola and cello

41 min

The recording

  1. I–VII. Complete quartet — seven linked movementsLéner String Quartet: Jenö Léner and Joszef Smilovits, violins; Sándor Roth, viola; Imre Hartman, cello · 11, 21 and 22 February; 25 August 1924, London
    40:42

    A solitary violin begins the Léner Quartet’s performance of Beethoven’s seven linked movements. Before the final C♯-major chords come a dance, a shared song and a Presto played close to the bridge, where the strings turn wiry and strange.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One violin begins alone

      The first violin starts a slow fugue in C♯ minor. Each of the other players takes up its tune, while the earlier voices keep moving. Four people gradually fill the silence; the cello has a melody of its own.

    2. A dedication for Karl’s protector

      In March 1827 Beethoven asks Schott to put Baron Joseph von Stutterheim’s name on the quartet. Stutterheim has accepted Beethoven’s nephew Karl into his regiment. The dedication carries an uncle’s gratitude into print.

    3. A semitone into daylight

      The fugue gathers its voices onto a quiet C♯. The next movement steps up to D major and a quick 6/8 dance. The first violin has the tune now, buoyed by the other three players.

    4. Eleven bars of speech

      The dance gives way to short, almost spoken phrases. They pass down through the quartet before the first violin breaks into running notes above held chords. Beethoven needs only eleven bars to reach the next movement.

    5. A song between two violins

      The violins share an A-major melody, one picking up where the other leaves off. The viola holds a quieter line between them, and the cello plucks the accompaniment. This small song supplies the quartet’s longest movement: a set of variations.

    6. A joke for Schott

      Schott asks whether the quartet is new. In August 1826 Beethoven sends the engraver’s copy with a joke written on it: “Stolen together from this and that.” He then has to assure the publisher that the music is brand new.

    7. The cello sets off

      A short cello figure launches the E-major Presto. The other three players answer with clipped notes; phrases race ahead, then trip over rests. Later Beethoven asks for sul ponticello — bowing close to the bridge — to give the same strings a thin, metallic edge.

    8. Twenty-eight bars to breathe

      The viola begins a slow G♯-minor melody. After the Presto’s quick strokes, the bow can stay on the string and the phrases can stretch. This Adagio lasts just twenty-eight bars; a first-violin flourish carries it into the finale.

    9. Four players strike together

      C♯ minor returns. All four players attack the finale’s opening gestures together, then the first violin sings above insistent offbeats. The slow fugue’s separate entrances have given way to an Allegro with clenched fists.

    10. Three C♯-major chords

      E♯ brightens the final tonic chord into C♯ major. After the last violin leap, the quartet plays three separated chords, fortissimo. The players stop together under the final fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. C♯ releases into D major

      The fugue’s four lines gather onto quiet C♯ octaves, held under a fermata. The cello then steps up to D and the first violin begins a D-major tune. Allegro molto vivace replaces the Adagio, with two dancing beats in each 6/8 bar.

    2. The dance gives way to speech

      The D-major dance thins to pianissimo notes and rests. Beethoven adds G♯ to the signature and changes 6/8 to common time. Chords punctuate short phrases passed from violin to violin, then viola and cello. The marking is Allegro moderato; an Adagio follows within this eleven-bar recitative.

    3. A flourish becomes a shared song

      The first violin runs across held chords, then the quartet settles towards A major. Andante ma non troppo e molto cantabile brings a new tune in 2/4. The two violins share it, the viola holds an inner line, and the cello changes to pizzicato, placing little groups of quavers beneath the song.

    4. The cello breaks the quiet

      The variations close quietly in A major, with plucked lower strings beneath a violin note held under a fermata. The cello switches back to the bow and starts the E-major Presto alone. A bar of silence follows its little figure; then all four players take up the clipped, quick music in cut time.

    5. The viola slows the quartet

      Ordinary bowing returns after the Presto’s near-bridge passage, and the quartet closes firmly in E major. The viola starts the G♯-minor Adagio while the other players wait to enter beneath it. Three slow beats replace the Presto’s two; the cello joins with sustained notes.

    6. A violin flourish into C♯ minor

      The first violin decorates the last phrase of the G♯-minor Adagio while the lower strings sustain its harmony. The key signature loses one sharp. C♯ minor returns at Allegro, in cut time, with a fortissimo C♯ struck in octaves by all four players.
