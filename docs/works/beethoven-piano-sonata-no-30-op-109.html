Ludwig van Beethoven / II. Classical

Piano Sonata No. 30, Op. 109

1820 · Piano

3 recordings · 20 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Vivace, ma non troppo — Adagio espressivoArtur Schnabel · 1932
    3:51

    Artur Schnabel plays the first movement of Beethoven’s Piano Sonata No. 30, Op. 109, marked Vivace, ma non troppo — Adagio espressivo. Eight bars of quick, quiet music are followed by a slow passage in the manner of an improvisation, and Beethoven built the whole movement from these two tempos.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening Vivace and first Adagio

      The hands play quick figures in alternation, softly, in E major. After eight bars Beethoven changes to Adagio espressivo, and broad arpeggios and diminished-seventh chords interrupt the opening tune. The arpeggios cover the whole keyboard.

    2. Return of Tempo I

      The Adagio reaches its cadence in B major. At Tempo I the interlocking figures return, now rising in repeated phrases. Accents cut into the legato, and the melody expands into octaves.

    3. Return of the opening theme in octaves

      E major and the opening theme return, with the right hand in octaves. Beethoven soon brings the dynamic back down to piano. The hands are still exchanging their short figures when the next Adagio espressivo begins.

    4. Return of the Adagio

      The slow arpeggios return in E major, then move to C major. Runs descend from the top of the piano into the bass and rise again. Beethoven extends the ending with a diminuendo, a ritardando and a fermata.

    5. Coda, from bar 66

      Tempo primo restores the quick exchanges between the hands. In the coda Beethoven also brings them together in a short passage of quiet chords. The movement ends on a chord of E major, held under the pedal.

    6. Schnabel’s recording, Abbey Road, 1932

      On 22 March 1932 Schnabel recorded Op. 109 for HMV in Abbey Road’s Studio No. 3. The sonata filled three shellac discs, issued as DB 1822–24.

    Joins

    1. Adagio to Tempo I in B major

      The opening eight bars stop before their B-major cadence is complete. The Adagio extends the cadence across arpeggios until bar 15. Tempo I then restores 2/4 and the quick exchanges between the hands, beginning in B major. The left hand takes the melodic figure and the right hand plays above it.

    2. Rising octaves lead to E major

      The piano rises through repeated figures under a series of crescendos. At the top of the rise the opening theme returns in E major, doubled in octaves, and the left hand plays below it. The Vivace tempo continues. A piano marking follows soon after, and the hands return to the closer spacing of the opening.

    3. The Adagio espressivo returns

      The metre changes from 2/4 to 3/4 and the marking to Adagio espressivo. The piano’s chords are spread as arpeggios across both hands. This second Adagio begins in E major. The sharps are then cancelled from the key signature for a passage in C major, with low octaves held beneath the runs.

    4. A fermata before Tempo primo

      The Adagio’s runs descend into the bass, then climb to a fermata, with a diminuendo and ritardando marked. At bar 66 Tempo primo restores 2/4 and the quick figures in E major. The right hand starts in a high register and the left hand enters after it.

  2. II. PrestissimoArtur Schnabel · 1932
    2:20

    Artur Schnabel plays the Prestissimo, the E-minor middle movement of the Piano Sonata No. 30, Op. 109. Running quavers give way to exchanges between the hands, and then the opening music returns.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, left hand <i>ben marcato</i>

      The movement is in E minor, Prestissimo, and begins fortissimo after the quiet end of the first movement. Beneath the running tune the left hand is marked ben marcato (strongly marked). The second theme, in B minor, keeps the restless character of the first.

    2. Middle section, from the bass

      The middle section is built from the opening bass line. Repeated Bs, then Cs, sound beneath slow-moving notes above. The running quavers stop, and the hands alternate softly with the soft pedal down.

    3. Return of the opening, E minor

      The opening returns fortissimo in E minor. After passages marked una corda and sempre più piano, Beethoven writes tutte le corde, so the pianist releases the soft pedal and the hammers strike all their strings again. The first bass octaves are restored at full strength.

    4. Letter to Maximiliane Brentano, 1821

      In December 1821 Beethoven sent the sonata to Maximiliane Brentano in Frankfurt, daughter of his friends Franz and Antonie Brentano. “A dedication!” he wrote. “But not one of those that so many people use and abuse.”

    Joins

    1. Into the development of the opening bass

      After the B-minor second theme, Beethoven develops the opening bass. The piano holds a repeated B, the dominant of E minor, while the right hand moves into longer notes. The bass then rises to C. The Prestissimo marking remains in force, and the running quavers in both hands gradually drop away.

    2. Soft pedal, then fortissimo

      The repeated bass stops. With the soft pedal down, the hands exchange phrases marked sempre più piano and pause under a fermata. A few soft chords follow. At tutte le corde and fortissimo the opening E-minor tune returns above its bass octaves.

    3. Closing bars of the Prestissimo

      In a quiet closing phrase the hands move apart. The bass steps down while the right hand plays rising chords. A crescendo leads to three short chords in E minor, marked forte staccato.

  3. III. Gesangvoll, mit innigster EmpfindungArtur Schnabel · 1932
    14:04

    Artur Schnabel plays the third movement of the Piano Sonata No. 30, Op. 109, Gesangvoll, mit innigster Empfindung: six variations on a quiet theme in E major, with quick counterpoint and trills in both hands. The movement ends with the original sixteen bars of the theme.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Theme in E major

      Beethoven headed the movement Gesangvoll, mit innigster Empfindung (songfully, with the deepest feeling). The melody is in E major, and its stress falls on the second beat of the three-beat bar. The theme runs to sixteen bars, and each half is repeated.

    2. Variation I

      The first variation places the melody an octave higher and adds short ornaments. The left hand plays a bass note followed by a chord, in the manner of a slow dance. Beethoven marks the tune molto espressivo.

    3. Dedication to Maximiliane Brentano

      The sonata is dedicated to Maximiliane Brentano. Beethoven had known her since her childhood, and in 1812 he gave the young pianist a short trio to encourage her playing. Nine years later, when he sent her this sonata, he wrote: “I see you all before me.”

    4. Variation II

      Leggieramente (lightly). The hands pass short figures between them, with brief rests. In the next passage two right-hand voices imitate one another above steady left-hand quavers, and Beethoven then combines the two kinds of writing.

    5. Variations III to V

      The third variation is marked Allegro vivace and is in 2/4, with two fast lines. The fourth is slower, in 9/8. The fifth is a fugato in several voices, and the key stays E major throughout these changes of tempo.

    6. Final variation, repeated B

      The final variation returns to the theme’s slow tempo, with repeated Bs above the melody. The crotchets divide into quavers, then triplets, then shorter values. The repeated notes become trills, and the melody continues in the middle register.

    7. Trills in both hands

      Long trills run through the final variation. Runs pass above them, then move into the left hand while the right hand continues to trill. The pianist must sustain a singing line within the quick notes.

    8. Return of the theme, E major

      The trills end, and the original theme returns in E major, marked cantabile, with its plain chords and dotted rhythm. This time each half is played once, without repeats. A quiet chord closes the sonata.

    Joins

    1. A repeated B above the song

      The fast fugato of the fifth variation closes, and Tempo primo del tema brings back the tempo of the theme. The metre changes from cut time to 3/4. The key stays E major. The piano plays the melody in slow notes, with a repeated B above it and a bass that moves once per beat. Later the repeated notes turn into trills.

    2. From the trill to the theme

      The right-hand trill moves down into the middle of the keyboard as the left hand’s arpeggios grow quieter. Beethoven writes più dimin., then pianissimo. The trill stops, the bass holds E, and the original melody returns above it, cantabile, with its plain chords and dotted rhythm.
