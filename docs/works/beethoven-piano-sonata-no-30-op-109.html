Ludwig van Beethoven / II. Classical

Piano Sonata No. 30, Op. 109

1820 · Piano

3 recordings · 20 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Vivace, ma non troppo — Adagio espressivoArtur Schnabel · 1932
    3:51

    Eight bars of quick, quiet piano music, then a slow improvisation seems to break in. Artur Schnabel plays the opening of Op. 109, where Beethoven makes a whole movement out of those two ways of speaking.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Eight bars, then Adagio

      The hands fit quick figures between one another, softly, in E major. After eight bars Beethoven changes to Adagio espressivo: broad arpeggios and diminished-seventh chords interrupt the little tune. The pianist suddenly has the whole keyboard to cross.

    2. The quick music returns

      The Adagio finally reaches its B-major cadence. Tempo I brings back the interlocking figures, now climbing in repeated phrases. Accents puncture the legato, and the tune grows into octaves.

    3. The tune in octaves

      E major and the opening tune return, with the right hand playing in octaves. Beethoven soon takes the sound back down to piano. The hands are still exchanging their little figures when the next Adagio interrupts.

    4. The Adagio ranges farther

      The slow arpeggios return in E major, then veer into C major. Runs tumble from the top of the piano into the bass and climb back. Beethoven draws out the ending with a diminuendo, a ritardando and a fermata.

    5. Quiet chords between the figures

      Tempo primo restores the quick exchanges between the hands. In this coda Beethoven also lets them settle into quiet chords, briefly singing together. The movement ends with an E-major chord held under the pedal.

    6. Schnabel at Abbey Road

      On 22 March 1932 Schnabel sits down in Abbey Road’s Studio No. 3 to record Op. 109 for HMV. The sonata fills three shellac discs, issued as DB 1822–24.

    How it is joined

    1. B major releases the quick tune

      The opening eight bars leave their B-major cadence unfinished. The Adagio stretches it across sweeping arpeggios until bar 15. Tempo I then restores 2/4 and the quick exchanges between the hands, beginning in B major. The left hand takes the melodic figure while the right answers above it.

    2. Climbing octaves reach E major

      The piano climbs through repeated figures and crescendos. At the top of this climb the opening tune returns in E major, doubled in octaves, with the left hand answering below. The Vivace pace continues. A piano marking soon brings the hands closer to their quiet opening conversation.

    3. The arpeggios interrupt again

      The metre changes from 2/4 to 3/4 and the marking to Adagio espressivo. Arpeggios spread the piano’s chords across both hands. This second Adagio begins in E major, then strips the sharps from the signature for a forceful passage in C major, with low octaves held beneath the runs.

    4. A fermata before Tempo primo

      The Adagio’s runs descend into the bass, then climb towards a fermata. A diminuendo and ritardando slow their progress. Tempo primo restores 2/4 and the quick figures in E major; the right hand begins high while the left waits to answer.

  2. II. PrestissimoArtur Schnabel · 1932
    2:20

    Artur Schnabel plays the fierce E-minor middle movement of Op. 109. Running quavers give way to whispered exchanges between the hands before the opening bursts back.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A bass with teeth

      The left hand is marked ben marcato — strongly marked — beneath the running tune. E minor, fortissimo, Prestissimo: the quiet end of the first movement is a long way away. Even the second theme, in B minor, keeps the restless character of the first.

    2. The bass takes over

      The middle passage grows from the opening bass line. Repeated Bs, then Cs, support notes that move slowly above them. The running quavers eventually stop; the hands answer one another softly, with the soft pedal down.

    3. All the strings again

      The opening comes back fortissimo in E minor. Beethoven writes tutte le corde: the pianist releases the soft pedal, allowing the hammers to strike all their strings again. After the whispered exchanges, those first bass octaves have a startling force.

    How it is joined

    1. The opening bass comes forward

      After the B-minor second theme, Beethoven develops the opening bass. The piano settles over repeated B, the dominant of E minor, while the right hand stretches into longer notes. The bass then rises to C. The Prestissimo marking stays in force as the hands gradually shed their running quavers.

    2. Soft pedal, then fortissimo

      The repeated bass stops. With the soft pedal down, the hands exchange phrases marked sempre più piano, quieter and quieter, and pause under a fermata. A few hushed chords remain before tutte le corde and fortissimo restore the opening E-minor tune above its bass octaves.

    3. The bass drops, the chords climb

      A quiet closing phrase sets the hands moving apart: the bass steps down while chords climb in the right hand. The crescendo ends in three clipped chords, marked forte staccato, with E minor firmly under the fingers.

  3. III. Gesangvoll, mit innigster EmpfindungArtur Schnabel · 1932
    14:04

    Six variations take a quiet E-major song through quick counterpoint and trills that fill both hands. At the end, Artur Schnabel has only the original sixteen bars left to play.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A song in three

      Beethoven asks for Gesangvoll, mit innigster Empfindung — songfully, with the deepest feeling. The E-major melody leans on the second beat of its three-beat bar. Each half repeats: there is time to get to know these sixteen bars.

    2. An octave higher

      The first variation lifts the melody an octave and gives it little flourishes. Below, the left hand places a bass note and then a chord, like the steps of a slow dance. Beethoven marks the tune molto espressivo.

    3. Maximiliane at the piano

      The sonata bears Maximiliane Brentano’s name. Beethoven has known her since childhood; in 1812 he gives the young pianist a short trio to encourage her playing. Nine years later, sending this sonata, he writes: “I see you all before me.”

    4. One hand answers another

      Leggieramente: lightly. The hands toss short figures between them, leaving tiny rests. A tender passage follows in which two right-hand voices imitate one another above steady left-hand quavers. Then Beethoven fits the two ways of playing together.

    5. Two hands in pursuit

      The third variation breaks into Allegro vivace, two racing lines in 2/4. The next slows into 9/8; the fifth gathers several voices into a fugato. E major holds through these changes of pace.

    6. B begins to tremble

      The final variation starts at the theme’s slow tempo, with repeated Bs above the tune. Crotchets become quavers, then triplets, then smaller notes still. The repetition turns into trills, while the melody keeps singing through the middle of the piano.

    7. Trills in both hands

      Long trills bind the final variation together. Runs sweep above them, then pass into the left hand while the right keeps trilling. The pianist has to sustain a singing line inside all those quick notes.

    8. The song comes home

      The trills subside, and the original theme returns in E major, marked cantabile. Its chords and dotted rhythm are plain again. This time the two halves go by once; a quiet chord closes the sonata.

    How it is joined

    1. A repeated B above the song

      The fifth variation’s fast fugato closes, and Tempo primo del tema restores the theme’s pace. Cut time changes to 3/4. E major remains, but the piano now sings in slow notes, with repeated B above the melody and a bass that moves once per beat. Those repeated notes will grow into the trills.

    2. The trill releases the original theme

      The right-hand trill drops into the middle of the keyboard as the left hand’s arpeggios grow quieter. Beethoven writes più dimin., then pianissimo. The trill ends; the bass settles on E and the original melody returns above it, cantabile, with its plain chords and dotted rhythm.
