Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart / II. Classical

Requiem, K. 626

1791 · K. 626 · Soloists, chorus, orchestra and organ

Bassoons and basset horns open the work in their low register. Süssmayr completed the unfinished score; later editors have offered other solutions.

4 recordings · 18 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Introitus: Requiem aeternam — AdagioLisa della Casa, soprano; Wiener Staatsopernchor; Wiener Philharmoniker; Franz Sauer, organ; Bruno Walter, conductor · 26 July 1956, Small Festival Hall, Salzburg; live
    5:38

    The Introitus of the Requiem in D minor, K. 626, marked Adagio, opens with bassoons and basset horns before the choir enters. Bruno Walter conducts this Salzburg performance, with Lisa della Casa singing the soprano psalm verse.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, bassoon and basset horn

      A bassoon begins, and a basset horn enters before the bassoon’s phrase is over. The basset horn is a low member of the clarinet family. Beneath the long wind notes the strings play short, uneven steps, with rests cutting into the beat.

    2. Choral entries, bars 8–12

      The basses begin the prayer for eternal rest, and the tenors, altos and sopranos follow in turn with the phrase first heard in the winds. The violins play falling leaps in detached pairs of notes, with a rest after each pair.

    3. Mozart’s death and the unfinished score

      Mozart died in Vienna on 5 December 1791, leaving the Requiem unfinished. He had fully scored this opening movement. The rest of Walter’s performance used Süssmayr’s completion.

    4. Soprano solo in B♭ major

      The choir stops. A short wind link leads to the soprano’s psalm verse in B♭ major, with running lines in the violins. Mozart leaves the organ out under this solo.

    5. Count Walsegg’s copy of the score

      Count Franz von Walsegg commissioned the Requiem in memory of his wife. Before conducting it in December 1793, he copied out the score and headed it “Requiem composed by Count Walsegg”.

    6. Return of “Requiem aeternam”, bars 34–43

      The basses bring back “Requiem aeternam” while the altos sing a quicker line around them on “Dona eis” (grant them). The two texts are sung together. The sopranos then take up the quicker line.

    7. Closing fermata on A major

      The last request for light is sung piano. In bars 46–48 choir and orchestra hold A major, the dominant of D minor, under a fermata. In the complete work the Kyrie follows, a fugue in D minor marked Allegro.

    Joins

    1. Choir enters with the wind phrase

      The brass play before the choir enters. The basses begin the D-minor prayer, and the tenors, altos and sopranos follow in turn with the opening wind phrase. The violins change from short off-beat notes to falling pairs, with a rest between each pair. The instrumental bass continues its broken tread under the voices as they enter one after another.

    2. Organ stops before the soprano psalm

      The opening choral section closes in B♭ major. A short phrase for basset horns and bassoons links it to the soprano psalm. The organ stops, marked senza Org. in the score, and cellos and running violin figures accompany the solo voice. The tempo stays Adagio (bars 17–25).

    3. The basses return to “Requiem aeternam”

      After the psalm, bars 32–43 begin with a quiet wind link that recalls the opening. The basses then return to “Requiem aeternam” in D minor, and the altos add a quicker curling phrase on “Dona eis”. The violin leaps return around them. The two vocal ideas then pass through the choir together.

    4. Closing A-major chord before the Kyrie

      The choir drops to piano for its last petition for light. The bass moves to A, and the voices hold an A-major chord under a fermata. This chord is the dominant of D minor, the key of the fugue that opens the Kyrie.

  2. III. Dies irae — Allegro assaiWiener Staatsopernchor; Wiener Philharmoniker; Franz Sauer, organ; Bruno Walter, conductor · 26 July 1956, Small Festival Hall, Salzburg; live
    1:53

    The Dies irae, marked Allegro assai, is the third section of the Requiem in D minor, K. 626, in which the choir sings the text of the Day of Judgement over rapid string figures. Bruno Walter conducts the Vienna forces in Süssmayr’s completion.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening of the Dies irae

      The four choral parts enter together in D minor on “Dies irae”, day of wrath, the opening of the Latin Requiem Sequence. The violins play repeated notes and the bass moves in broken chords. Trumpets and timpani mark the beat.

    2. “Quantus tremor”, choral basses

      The choral basses sing “Quantus tremor” — how great the trembling — on repeated notes set against the beat, beginning in the second half of bar 40. Sopranos, altos and tenors reply with the words of the day of wrath. The violins keep up their fast figuration throughout.

    3. Instrumental close

      The choir stops after the first beat of bar 65, and bars 66–68 are for orchestra alone. Violins, bassoons and the bass line keep moving until the final D minor chord cuts them off.

    Joins

    1. Choir halts on A; basses lead on

      The choir stops on A major, the dominant of D minor. The basses then sing a repeated figure alone, and the other three parts follow in longer notes. Bassoons double the bass line. The violins play quick repeated notes and then climb through the reply of the upper voices. The tempo remains Allegro assai.

    2. Full choir at “Quantus tremor”

      All four choral parts take up “Quantus tremor”, with their repeated notes now sung together, and the orchestral bass resumes its running broken chords. Trumpets and timpani punctuate the return. The violins continue the fast pulse through the final judgement and the short D-minor postlude.

  3. VIII. LacrimosaWiener Staatsopernchor; Wiener Philharmoniker; Franz Sauer, organ; Bruno Walter, conductor · 26 July 1956, Small Festival Hall, Salzburg; live
    3:25

    The Lacrimosa is the eighth movement of the Requiem in D minor, K. 626; two bars for the violins open it before the choir enters with a rising line. Bruno Walter conducts the Vienna choir and orchestra in a live recording made in Salzburg in 1956.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening bars, upper strings

      Violins and violas play alone for two bars, in short phrases separated by rests. The choir and lower instruments then enter over the same accompaniment. The metre is 12/8, with four slow beats in each bar, each divided into three.

    2. Bars 5–8, rising choral line

      At the words about rising from the ashes, in bars 5–8, the choir climbs step by step. Separated syllables become longer notes, and the phrase grows to forte over a crescendo. The violins continue beneath it.

    3. Eight-bar fragment in Mozart’s manuscript

      In Mozart’s manuscript the Lacrimosa breaks off after eight bars. After his death, Constanze arranged for the unfinished Requiem to be completed. Franz Xaver Süssmayr supplied the rest of this movement in the version performed here.

    4. Eybler’s declaration, 21 December 1791

      On 21 December 1791 Joseph Eybler signed a declaration that Constanze had entrusted Mozart’s unfinished Requiem to him, and promised to finish it by the middle of Lent. His additions ran from the Dies irae to this Lacrimosa, where he wrote two soprano bars and stopped. Süssmayr took over the task.

    5. Return of the opening melody, bar 22

      At bar 22 the opening melody returns in D minor, now set to “Dona eis requiem” — grant them rest. The choir sings it forte, with basset horns and bassoons alongside. The violin figures still fall between the beats.

    6. Final Amen, plagal cadence

      The final Amen extends over two held chords, a plagal cadence from G minor to D major with F♯ in the last chord. The violins complete their short falling figures and stop with the voices.

    Joins

    1. Choir at piano for “Huic ergo”

      At bar 15 the choir drops to piano for “Huic ergo”. Basset horns, bassoons, trumpets and timpani rest. The upper voices repeat notes while the choral bass steps down and back up beneath them, and short rests continue to divide the string accompaniment. The harmony moves back to D minor and cadences at “Domine”.

    2. The violin figure returns

      Two instrumental bars bring back the violins’ rising-and-falling figure. The low strings resume their short, separated notes, and at bar 22 the choir returns forte in D minor with “Dona eis requiem”. Basset horns and bassoons reinforce the voices; trumpets and timpani punctuate the phrase.

    3. G minor to D major at the Amen

      In bars 29–30 the choir holds a G-minor chord on the first syllable of Amen while the violins finish their falling figures. The bass moves from G to D. An F♯ in the final chord makes the close D major, and the strings sustain their notes with the singers.

  4. XIV. Communio: Lux aeternaLisa della Casa, soprano; Wiener Staatsopernchor; Wiener Philharmoniker; Franz Sauer, organ; Bruno Walter, conductor · 26 July 1956, Small Festival Hall, Salzburg; live
    7:04

    The Communio, Lux aeterna, is the final movement of the Requiem in D minor, K. 626, and opens with Lisa della Casa singing the Introitus psalm melody to a prayer for eternal light. The choir then repeats the Kyrie fugue, which ends on a bare D and A.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Soprano solo, Lux aeterna

      A short wind phrase leads into the soprano’s prayer for eternal light. Her melody comes from the Introitus psalm verse, in B♭ major. Beneath her longer notes the strings continue their quick figures.

    2. Full choir entry, bars 59–60

      Tenors and basses enter, followed by altos and sopranos. The strings continue their quick figures throughout. Basset horns and bassoons return as the voices enter.

    3. Return of the Introitus

      The Communion prayer brings back the words “Requiem aeternam”. The Introitus music returns, combining its long opening theme with quicker phrases on “Dona eis”, which pass from altos to sopranos and then through the lower voices.

    4. Süssmayr’s letter, 8 February 1800

      In a letter of 8 February 1800 to the publishers Breitkopf & Härtel, Süssmayr explained that in his completion he repeated the Kyrie fugue “in order to give the work more unity”.

    5. Constanze Mozart’s account, 1799

      Ten months before Süssmayr’s letter, Constanze told the same publishers that Mozart, seeing death near, had asked Süssmayr to repeat the first fugue in the final piece, “as is customary anyway”. In his letter, Süssmayr said he had allowed himself the repetition.

    6. Return of the Kyrie fugue

      The Kyrie fugue returns at Allegro, now set to “Cum sanctis tuis” (with your saints). It has two themes, one in longer notes and the other in semiquavers. The basses begin the first and the altos the second, and both pass through the other voices of the choir.

    7. Salzburg, July 1956

      On 26 July 1956 Bruno Walter conducted the Requiem in Salzburg’s Small Festival Hall. Lisa della Casa sang the soprano part and Franz Sauer played the organ. The same concert included Mozart’s G-minor Symphony No. 25.

    8. Brahms on Süssmayr

      Brahms edited the Requiem for the old Mozart Complete Edition. Of Süssmayr’s work he said: “He copied Mozart’s framework painstakingly and completed it with equal amounts of diligence and piety.”

    9. Closing Adagio and open fifth

      The fugue ends with a sudden broadening to Adagio. The running voices come together on the last words, “Quia pius es”. The final chord contains only D and A, a bare fifth.

    Joins

    1. The lower voices take over

      As the B♭-major soprano passage ends, tenors and basses enter with the chorus. Altos follow, then sopranos. Basset horns and bassoons return forte, and the strings continue their quick figures without a break through the change of voices. Beneath the overlapping entries the bass line becomes busy again.

    2. Held A major to the Kyrie fugue

      The choir holds an A-major chord under a fermata, with the bass on A. At the Allegro the choral basses and bassoons begin the Kyrie’s D-minor subject. The altos enter with a running line against it, doubled by second violins and basset horns. The Kyrie fugue is sung here to the words of the Communion.

    3. Final Adagio and open fifth

      In bars 130–133 the last semiquaver runs stop, and at the Adagio all four choral parts move together. A fermata falls before “Quia pius es”. The bass descends towards D, and the final chord is an open fifth on D and A, with no third.

    Open in the Concert Hall
