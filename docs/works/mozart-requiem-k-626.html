Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart / II. Classical

Requiem, K. 626

1791 · K. 626 · Soloists, chorus, orchestra and organ

Bassoons and basset horns begin the work in a low, sombre conversation. Süssmayr completed the unfinished score; later editors have offered other solutions.

4 recordings · 18 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Introitus: Requiem aeternam — AdagioLisa della Casa, soprano; Wiener Staatsopernchor; Wiener Philharmoniker; Franz Sauer, organ; Bruno Walter, conductor · 26 July 1956, Small Festival Hall, Salzburg; live
    5:38

    The Requiem begins with bassoons and basset horns winding around one another, before the choir takes up their prayer. Bruno Walter conducts this Salzburg performance, with Lisa della Casa singing the soprano psalm verse.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Low reeds, restless strings

      A bassoon begins; a basset horn answers before the phrase is over. The basset horn is a low member of the clarinet family. Beneath these long wind notes, the strings take short, uneven steps, with rests cutting into the beat.

    2. The choir builds upwards

      The basses begin the prayer for eternal rest. Tenors, altos and sopranos follow in turn, taking up the phrase heard first in the winds. Around them the violins make sharp, falling leaps, each pair of notes separated from the next by a rest.

    3. Mozart’s finished first movement

      Mozart dies in Vienna on 5 December 1791, leaving the Requiem unfinished. This opening movement is already fully scored. The completion used for the rest of Walter’s performance is Süssmayr’s.

    4. One voice in B♭

      The choir falls silent. A brief wind link leads to the soprano’s psalm verse in B♭ major, with running violin lines around it. Mozart leaves the organ out beneath this solo.

    5. Count Walsegg copies the score

      Count Franz von Walsegg commissions the Requiem in memory of his wife. Before conducting it in December 1793, he copies out the score and heads it “Requiem composed by Count Walsegg”.

    6. Two pleas at once

      The basses bring back “Requiem aeternam”, while the altos thread a quicker line around them on “Dona eis” — grant them. The prayer for rest and the act of asking unfold together. Soon the sopranos take up the running line too.

    7. A held breath on A

      The last request for light grows quiet. Choir and orchestra settle on A major, the chord that points back towards D minor, and hold it under a fermata. The Kyrie’s D-minor fugue follows in the complete work.

    How it is joined

    1. The wind phrase finds words

      Brass announce the choir, whose basses begin the D-minor prayer. Tenors, altos and sopranos follow with the opening wind phrase. The violins change from short off-beat notes to falling pairs, leaving a rest between each pair. The instrumental bass keeps its interrupted tread as the voices accumulate above it.

    2. The organ falls silent

      The first choral prayer subsides into B♭ major. A short phrase for basset horns and bassoons bridges the gap before the soprano psalm. The organ falls silent; cellos and running violin figures accompany the solo voice. The marking remains Adagio.

    3. The basses recover the prayer

      After the psalm, a quiet wind link recalls the opening. The basses recover “Requiem aeternam” in D minor, and the altos add a quicker curling phrase on “Dona eis”. The violin leaps return around them. Two vocal ideas now travel through the choir together.

    4. A major holds the door open

      The choir softens to piano for its last request for light. The bass settles on A, and the voices hold an A-major chord under a fermata. This dominant chord prepares the D-minor fugue of the following Kyrie.

  2. III. Dies irae — Allegro assaiWiener Staatsopernchor; Wiener Philharmoniker; Franz Sauer, organ; Bruno Walter, conductor · 26 July 1956, Small Festival Hall, Salzburg; live
    1:53

    The choir shouts the Day of Judgement over racing strings, and even its pauses feel breathless. Bruno Walter conducts the Vienna forces in Süssmayr’s completion.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Day of wrath

      Four choral parts strike together in D minor: “Dies irae”, day of wrath. The violins shake on repeated notes, the bass runs through broken chords, and trumpets and timpani hammer out the beat. Mozart leaves almost no space between the threats.

    2. Trembling in the basses

      The choral basses take “Quantus tremor” — how great the trembling — on repeated notes tugging against the beat. Sopranos, altos and tenors answer with the day of wrath again. The violins race on through the exchange.

    3. After the last syllable

      The choir has finished, but the orchestra still has three bars to go. Violins, bassoons and the bass line keep moving until the final D-minor chord cuts them short.

    How it is joined

    1. The basses break away

      The choir stops on A major, the dominant of D minor. Its basses break away with a repeated trembling figure; the other three parts answer in longer notes. Bassoons double the low voice, and violins beat out quick repeated notes before climbing through the reply. The Allegro assai continues.

    2. Four voices tremble together

      All four choral parts take up “Quantus tremor”. Their repeated notes now move together, while the orchestral bass resumes its running broken chords. Trumpets and timpani punctuate the return; the violins keep the fast pulse going through the final judgement and the short D-minor postlude.

  3. VIII. LacrimosaWiener Staatsopernchor; Wiener Philharmoniker; Franz Sauer, organ; Bruno Walter, conductor · 26 July 1956, Small Festival Hall, Salzburg; live
    3:25

    The Lacrimosa turns two sighing violin bars into a choral ascent that seems to run out of breath. Bruno Walter conducts the Vienna choir and orchestra, recorded live in Salzburg in 1956.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Two bars of sighs

      Violins and violas begin alone, their short phrases caught between little silences. The choir takes up the lament over the same faltering accompaniment. The metre is 12/8: four slow beats, each divided into three.

    2. Rising from the ashes

      At the words about rising from the ashes, the choir itself climbs, step by step. Separated syllables grow into longer notes and the phrase swells to forte. The violins keep catching their breath beneath it.

    3. Eight bars in Mozart’s hand

      In Mozart’s manuscript the Lacrimosa breaks off after eight bars. After his death, Constanze arranges for the unfinished Requiem to be completed. Franz Xaver Süssmayr supplies the rest of this movement in the version performed here.

    4. The same melody asks

      The opening melody returns in D minor, now carrying “Dona eis requiem” — grant them rest. The choir sings it forte, with basset horns and bassoons alongside. The violin sighs are still there, between the beats.

    5. Amen turns to major

      The final Amen stretches over two held chords. G minor settles into D major, with F♯ brightening the last chord. The violins finish their little falling figures and come to rest with the voices.

    How it is joined

    1. The prayer drops to piano

      The choir drops to piano for “Huic ergo”. Basset horns, bassoons, trumpets and timpani rest. The upper voices repeat notes while the choral bass steps down and back up beneath them; little rests still divide the string accompaniment. The prayer works its way back to D minor.

    2. The violin sighs return

      Two instrumental bars bring back the violins’ rising-and-falling figure. The low strings resume their short, separated notes, and the choir returns forte in D minor with “Dona eis requiem”. Basset horns and bassoons reinforce the voices; trumpets and timpani punctuate the phrase.

    3. G minor settles into D major

      The choir holds a G-minor chord on the first syllable of Amen while the violins finish their falling figures. The bass moves from G to D. An F♯ in the final chord turns the close to D major, and the strings finally sustain their notes with the singers.

  4. XIV. Communio: Lux aeternaLisa della Casa, soprano; Wiener Staatsopernchor; Wiener Philharmoniker; Franz Sauer, organ; Bruno Walter, conductor · 26 July 1956, Small Festival Hall, Salzburg; live
    7:04

    Lisa della Casa’s soprano brings back the Introitus psalm melody, now asking for eternal light. The choir closes the Requiem with the earlier Kyrie fugue, its racing lines finally stripped down to a bare D and A.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Light from one voice

      A short wind phrase opens onto the soprano’s prayer for eternal light. Her melody comes from the Introitus psalm verse, in B♭ major. The strings keep their quick figures moving beneath her longer notes.

    2. The choir joins the prayer

      Tenors and basses take up the prayer, followed by altos and sopranos. The strings continue their quick figures through the handover. Basset horns and bassoons return alongside the growing choir.

    3. The old words return

      The Communion prayer makes room for the old words, “Requiem aeternam”. The returning Introitus combines its long opening theme with quicker phrases on “Dona eis”; these move from altos to sopranos, then through the lower voices.

    4. Süssmayr explains the return

      In his letter to the publishers Breitkopf & Härtel on 8 February 1800, Süssmayr explains his completion: he repeats the Kyrie fugue “in order to give the work more unity”.

    5. Two themes run together

      The Kyrie fugue returns at Allegro, now carrying “Cum sanctis tuis” — with your saints. One theme strides in longer notes; the other runs in semiquavers. Basses begin the first, altos the second, and the two keep finding new partners throughout the choir.

    6. Salzburg, July 1956

      On 26 July 1956, Bruno Walter conducts the Requiem in Salzburg’s Small Festival Hall. Lisa della Casa sings the soprano part and Franz Sauer plays the organ. The same concert includes Mozart’s G-minor Symphony No. 25.

    7. Only D and A

      The fugue ends with a sudden broadening to Adagio. The running voices come together on the last words, “Quia pius es” — for you are merciful. The final chord contains only D and A: all that crowded singing ends on a bare fifth.

    How it is joined

    1. The lower voices take over

      As the B♭-major soprano passage ends, tenors and basses begin the choral prayer. Altos follow, then sopranos. Basset horns and bassoons return forte, while the strings carry their quick figures straight through the handover. The bass line becomes busy again beneath the overlapping voices.

    2. A major releases the fugue

      The choir holds an A-major chord under a fermata, the bass resting on A. Then Allegro: the choral basses and bassoons begin the Kyrie’s D-minor subject. Altos enter with its running companion, doubled by second violins and basset horns. The old fugue carries the Communion words.

    3. The voices stop running

      The last semiquaver runs reach a halt, and Adagio brings all four choral parts together. A fermata suspends the phrase before “Quia pius es”. The bass descends towards D; the final chord leaves D and A alone, with no third between them.
