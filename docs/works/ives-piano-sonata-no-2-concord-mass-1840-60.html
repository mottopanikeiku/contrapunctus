Charles Ives / VI. Modernism

Piano Sonata No. 2, ‘Concord, Mass., 1840–60’

c. 1916–19; revised 1920s–40s · Piano; optional additional flute and viola passages

Four New England literary portraits, with quotations from Beethoven’s Fifth running through them. In Hawthorne the pianist uses a felt-covered board to play clusters on the black keys; in Thoreau a flute may join the pianist.

4 recordings · 45 min

In the Hall

  1. I. EmersonEric Xi Xin Liang, piano · 2024
    15:38

    ‘Emerson’ is the first movement of Ives’s Piano Sonata No. 2, ‘Concord, Mass., 1840–60’, and is his portrait of Emerson. Ives set out seven themes on the first page and developed them through the movement, in which Beethoven’s four-note motif returns repeatedly.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Seven themes, opening page

      Seven themes appear on the opening page, some only a few notes long. They interrupt and overlap in the piano, and they include fragments of the melody that returns plainly in The Alcotts.

    2. Ives on Emerson and Thoreau

      At the opening of his Essays Before a Sonata, Ives called the first and last movements, Emerson and Thoreau, “composite pictures or impressions”. They give no programme of either man’s life or of any one of his works.

    3. Copies sent out, January 1921

      In January 1921 Ives mailed two hundred copies of the Concord Sonata, with a book of essays, to people in the music world. He had paid for the printing himself. Ives was an insurance executive.

    4. Human faith melody, both halves combined

      After the opening page the dynamic drops, and both halves of the sonata’s main tune sound together. The right hand plays the first half, varied, in C, and the left hand plays the second in A♭. Ives called the tune the “human faith melody”.

    5. Unfinished Emerson Concerto, 1910–11

      The movement grew out of an unfinished concerto from 1910–11. In it Ives cast the soloist as Emerson and the orchestra as the crowd responding to him. In the sonata a single pianist plays both parts.

    6. Carlyle on Emerson’s paragraphs

      In his Essays Before a Sonata (1920), Ives recalled that Carlyle had told Emerson some of his paragraphs did not cohere. Ives located Emerson’s plan in “the large unity of a series of particular aspects of a subject”. Kyle Gann has written that the movement mirrors that “grand, nonlinear chaos of interrelated ideas”.

    7. Lyrical theme marked “verse”

      A lyrical theme enters in the piano. Ives labels it verse in the score, for Emerson’s poetry beside his prose. It leads to an apparent return of the opening and then a large climax based on Beethoven.

    8. Fugue in Four Keys, Yale

      At Yale, Ives showed Horatio Parker a Fugue in Four Keys, with all four keys sounding at once. Parker curtly asked him to bring no more such experiments. Ives turned to counterpoint, harmony and form, and in four years, Jan Swafford writes, he learned a great deal about the shaping forces of music.

    9. Central episode

      A high melody descends while figures rise beneath it in the bass. The melody returns several times, with more notes and thicker chords at each return. The episode begins and ends softly, with a large crescendo between.

    10. Central “nature” episode

      Kyle Gann called this central episode “nature”. The lines rising from the bass approximate a harmonic series, a common sign of nature for composers, and Emerson wrote a famous essay called Nature. The melody gains force with each repetition, a little like Ravel’s Boléro.

    11. Song episode and fugal entries

      The Emerson theme returns in a song-like passage. Fugue-like entries follow, drawn largely from the earlier verse theme, with each line beginning before the previous one has finished.

    12. Revisions, 1921–1947

      Ives had begun revising the sonata by 1921, and Emerson received particular attention. A substantially revised edition was printed in 1947. He went on changing the notes for more than twenty years after mailing the score.

    13. Recapitulation and second C minor climax

      Music from the opening episodes returns. Beethoven’s repeated-note figure again builds to a C minor climax, the second after the large outburst in the movement’s first third.

    14. First performances, Cos Cob and New York

      On 28 November 1938 John Kirkpatrick played the whole sonata from memory at a private concert in Cos Cob. The public première followed at New York’s Town Hall on 20 January 1939, and the New York Tribune called it “the greatest music composed by an American”.

    15. Closing section, Beethoven figure

      The Emerson theme is broken into fragments. Beethoven’s four-note figure returns in A major and E♭ major, keys a tritone apart. At the end the figure is played slowly in the low bass.

    16. Emerson essay, closing paragraph, 1920

      Ives ended his Emerson essay with the four notes of Beethoven’s Fifth. He placed their message “above the relentlessness of fate knocking at the door”, as “the Soul of humanity knocking at the door of the Divine mysteries”.

    Joins

    1. Entry of the verse theme

      The overlapping piano phrases give way to a quiet lyrical theme, labelled verse. Its clear upper line begins another episode, which later builds to the first large C minor outburst on Beethoven’s repeated-note figure.

    2. High melody above rising bass figures

      After the Beethoven climax, a descending melody begins softly in the piano’s high register, with rising figures in the bass beneath it. The melody repeats with added notes and thicker chords. The passage forms a long arch and ends softly, at the dynamic of its opening.

    3. Emerson theme returns as song

      The repeated high melody dies away. The piano then plays the Emerson theme in a song-like version, and after it the earlier verse theme provides much of the fugue-like writing. The new lines take up tunes already heard among the louder chords.

    4. C minor climax to distant-key echoes

      The later Beethoven climax in C minor begins to break up. The Emerson tune breaks apart, and the repeated-note figure sounds in A major and E♭ major. These distant echoes grow quieter, and the slow final statement falls to the piano’s bass.

  2. II. HawthorneEric Xi Xin Liang, piano · 2024
    12:42

    ‘Hawthorne’ is the second movement of Ives’s Piano Sonata No. 2, ‘Concord, Mass., 1840–60’, a scherzo drawn from Hawthorne’s stories, with frost on a window, a train to heaven and a hymn interrupted by a circus. It ranges from clusters on the black keys to ragtime played with the fists.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening arpeggios

      The piano opens with rapid arpeggios. Ives linked the opening to the children in Hawthorne’s The Three Golden Apples, waking to frost on the window. The arpeggios soon lead into railway music.

    2. Felt-covered board clusters

      In the first slow episode, the score calls for a felt-covered board, fifteen inches long, to press neighbouring black keys high on the piano. The clusters sound softly while a slower melody moves below.

    3. Ives on the Hawthorne movement

      In his essay, Ives wrote that any full picture of Hawthorne rests on the influence of sin upon the conscience. He described the movement as an “extended fragment” drawn from Hawthorne’s wilder, fantastical adventures into half-childlike, half-fairylike realms, and it does not attempt that fuller picture.

    4. Fast rag

      The slow melody gives way to a fast rag. Accents fall just before the beat, in ragtime syncopation. Ives keeps changing the tunes while the accents remain displaced.

    5. Hawthorne’s story, 1843

      Hawthorne’s The Celestial Rail-Road, published in 1843, turns Bunyan’s arduous pilgrimage into a comfortable train ride, and the passengers can stow their sins with the luggage. Ives’s scherzo takes the bustle of the train and the irreverence of the story.

    6. Hymn tune <i>Martyn</i>

      The rag is interrupted by the hymn Martyn, associated with the words “Jesus, lover of my soul”. Its shape resembles the sonata’s recurring melody. The rag returns, and the hymn interrupts it a second time.

    7. Hawthorne essay, 1920

      In the “Hawthorne” essay of 1920, Ives described an old hymn heard in a churchyard while a circus parade came down Main Street. In his account, the hymn sheltered the people among the graves from the noise of the parade.

    8. March and second ragtime

      A march breaks off from the ragtime, with piano clusters imitating drums. It slows to a pause, and a longer rag begins. This passage builds to the climax of fist clusters.

    9. Childhood drum lessons

      Ives liked to tell how his father found him, aged five, banging out his band’s drum parts on the piano with his fists. George Ives told Charles that this was all right if he knew what he was doing, and sent him down the street for drum lessons.

    10. Phrases of Columbia, left hand

      After the cluster climax, the piano starts again slowly. The left hand plays phrases of Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean.

    11. Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean

      Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean becomes more recognisable as its phrases accumulate. Fragments of the tune appear across the piano before the tune is heard whole, which reverses the usual order of theme and variations.

    12. Ives’s essay on Hawthorne, 1920

      At the end of his Hawthorne essay Ives listed what the music may recall: frost on a window, Feathertop, the Seven Vagabonds and Phoebe’s Garden. He then qualified the list with the words “not something that happens, but the way something happens”.

    13. Triplet figures and final bars

      Rapid triplet figures run across the keyboard and carry fragments of familiar tunes. Near the end, Columbia and the hymn appear for the last time, followed by a final gesture across the whole piano.

    Joins

    1. Black-key cluster above a slow melody

      The rapid piano figures stop. In the slow episode, neighbouring black keys sound softly high on the keyboard, pressed down together with a felt-covered board. A languid melody moves beneath the cluster at a different pace from the opening arpeggios.

    2. Cluster passage to fast rag

      The soft high clusters and slow lower melody give way to fast ragtime. Accented notes fall before the beat, which displaces the accents. Quick, sharply articulated phrases replace the held black-key chords.

    3. Martyn quotations interrupt the rag

      The piano’s ragtime breaks off for a quiet statement of the hymn Martyn. The hymn’s longer notes resemble the sonata’s recurring melody. The rag then resumes, and a second quiet statement of Martyn follows it.

    4. From the rag into a march

      The third continuation of the rag breaks off abruptly into a march. Clusters in the piano imitate drum strokes beneath the march. The march slows to a pause, and a longer ragtime passage follows, building to the fist-cluster climax.

    5. Fist clusters to <i>Columbia</i> phrases

      The long ragtime passage builds to clusters played with the fists. After them the piano starts again slowly, and the left hand plays phrases of Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean. The song is introduced a phrase at a time.

    6. Left-hand fragments of <i>Columbia</i> lengthen

      The scraps of Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean in the left hand are developed into longer, more recognisable phrases. As the tune becomes clearer, the surrounding texture increases in speed and force. Interruptions continue to break up its course.

    7. Triplet figuration after <i>Columbia</i>

      After the passage built on Columbia, complex triplet figures take over the piano. Fragments of tunes pass through the running notes. The triplet motion continues up to the last recalls of the patriotic song and the hymn.

  3. III. The AlcottsEric Xi Xin Liang · 2024
    5:47

    “The Alcotts” is the third movement of Ives’s Piano Sonata No. 2, “Concord, Mass., 1840–60”. Ives took the music heard at the Alcotts’ piano, hymns and Scottish songs along with Beethoven’s Fifth, and made from it the broad C major melody at the centre of the Concord Sonata.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Hymn melody in B♭ over A♭

      The piano plays a hymn-like melody in B♭ over bass chords in A♭. The tune is close to complete here. Fragments of this melody appear throughout the Concord Sonata.

    2. Ives on the Orchard House spinet, 1920

      In his 1920 essay, Ives pointed to a “little old spinet-piano” at Orchard House. His account combined the Alcott family with Little Women. In it, Beth played Scottish airs and tried her hand at Beethoven’s Fifth.

    3. Beethoven’s Fifth and <i>Missionary Hymn</i>

      The short-short-short-long figure of Beethoven’s Fifth sounds through the piano chords. A second borrowed tune, Missionary Hymn, enters, and its repeated notes also fall by a third. Ives sets the two against each other in different keys.

    4. Middle episode, parlour music

      The middle episode is a song-like piano passage in the manner of parlour music. Its turns follow the idiom of Scottish folk song, and twice it quotes the wedding march from Wagner’s Lohengrin.

    5. Public première, Town Hall, 1939

      On 20 January 1939 John Kirkpatrick gave the complete sonata its public première at New York’s Town Hall. He had already played it from memory at a private concert in Cos Cob on 28 November 1938. By then the score had been in circulation for eighteen years.

    6. Chromatic transition

      The Scottish-song turns are extended into a chromatic passage. Piano chords cluster around the melody and lead to a much broader statement of the sonata’s recurring tune.

    7. Full melody in C major

      The whole melody is played in broad C major chords, with both hands. Its rising do–re–mi and Beethoven’s repeated-note figure now form one continuous tune.

    8. Final C major cadence

      Near the end the music briefly returns to B♭. The piano then closes on a C major chord.

    Joins

    1. Quiet restatement, then the parlour episode

      After the Beethoven climax, the recurring melody returns at a quiet level over dissonant piano chords. In the next episode, the parlour episode, a song-like upper line is set apart from its accompaniment. Scottish turns and a brief allusion to Wagner’s wedding music replace the pounding repeated notes.

    2. Parlour tune leads to C major melody

      The piano keeps the Scottish turns of the parlour tune and alters them with chromatic notes. The chords grow more crowded and the plain accompaniment is disturbed. The harmony becomes ambiguous. The song idiom changes gradually, and this passage forms the link to the broad C major melody.

    3. Chromatic passage into C major melody

      The chromatic piano passage moves into the complete recurring melody in C major. Both hands play broad chords beneath its do–re–mi opening and the repeated notes taken from Beethoven. The fragments heard in the earlier movements are joined here in a single phrase.

    4. B♭ before the final C major chord

      The broad C major chords grow quieter as the theme dies away. B♭, the key of the opening, returns briefly before the closing C major chord. The last change of harmony falls within the slowing piano phrase.

  4. IV. ThoreauEric Xi Xin Liang, piano · 2024
    10:31

    ‘Thoreau’ is the fourth movement of Ives’s Piano Sonata No. 2, ‘Concord, Mass., 1840–60’, and depicts a walk around Walden Pond. After a restless passage the music settles over a repeated A–C–G in the bass, and the Concord Sonata ends on high chords.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening chords in two keys

      Rising chords in the piano sound in two keys at once. Their small upward steps return near the end of the movement.

    2. Ives’s 1920 essay on Thoreau

      In his 1920 essay Ives placed Thoreau beside Walden Pond on an autumn day, “a shadow of a thought at first, colored by the mist and haze over the pond”. In his account the mist lifts, and Thoreau grows restless and starts walking.

    3. Three walking episodes

      A plain G major chord begins the first of three walking episodes. Each one quickens and grows louder, then falls back. The last builds to a bell-like climax.

    4. Thoreau’s walks in the Essays

      In Ives’s imagined day Thoreau walks the white-pebbled eastern shore, the “bolder northern” and western shores, and the railroad track “where the Aeolian harp plays”. Each quicker stride is still short of the “tempo of Nature”. Ives wrote that Thoreau’s freedom lay “in his submission to her”.

    5. A–C–G ostinato in the bass

      A–C–G rises repeatedly in the bass as an ostinato. Above it the piano plays an independent melody in another key.

    6. The Concord bell

      In Ives’s account, the evening is interrupted only by “the faint sound of the Concord bell”, because it is prayer-meeting night in the village. Kyle Gann hears the bell in a series of soft, dissonant chords, after which the music moves towards its climax.

    7. Repeated chords and climax

      The piano repeats chords that grow more dissonant. Earlier in this passage a dotted-rhythm figure appears that is related to the sonata’s recurring melody. The repetition leads to a climax.

    8. Performance for Max Smith, 1912

      In a memo of 1913 Ives noted that the year before he had played the Concord Sonata for his friend Max Smith, a music critic, with Thoreau still rather improvised.

    9. Return of the A–C–G ostinato

      Converging chords follow the climax. The A–C–G ostinato and its melody return, and then figures from the opening page come back.

    10. Ives on Thoreau, 1920

      Thoreau played the flute, though Ives was more interested in his ear. In the opening paragraph of “Thoreau” in Essays Before a Sonata (1920), Ives wrote that Thoreau “did not have to go to Boston to hear ‘the Symphony’”.

    11. Return of the cyclic melody

      The sonata’s recurring melody returns above repeating figures from the movement’s opening. Later the A–C–G ostinato takes over beneath it. The same tune was heard at length in The Alcotts; here it returns in a much quieter ending.

    12. Flute sketch, 1911

      In Ives’s evening, “the poet’s flute is heard out over the pond”. A sketch with a flute playing the sonata’s main melody probably dates from 1911, when Ives holidayed at Pell’s in the Adirondacks and conceived the sonata. The sketch does not yet contain the Beethoven motive.

    13. Closing bars, high chords

      The opening figures return briefly, followed by chords high on the piano. An earlier phrase sounds beneath them. The final notes keep a slight dissonance, unlike the plain C major ending of The Alcotts.

    Joins

    1. G major chord opens first walking episode

      The rising piano figures lead to a plain G major chord, which begins the first of three walking episodes. Each episode grows quicker and louder before falling back, and the same G major chord begins the second and third.

    2. A–C–G ostinato in the bass

      The last walk reaches a bell-like climax. The piano then begins repeating A–C–G in the bass, and a melody in another key moves freely above it. The left hand keeps returning to the same three notes while the right hand plays an independent line.

    3. Dotted rhythms lead to repeated chords

      The piano writing becomes more animated, with a dotted-rhythm figure related to the sonata’s recurring melody. Repeated dissonant chords then build to a climax.

    4. Converging chords and the returning bass figure

      The dissonant climax subsides in converging chords in the piano. The A–C–G figure returns in the bass, below the wandering melody heard earlier. Figures from the opening page then interrupt the bass figure and the melody, and they gradually become more agitated.

    5. Recurring melody over the introductory figures

      The sonata’s recurring melody enters above repeating figures from the introduction. As the melody continues, the accompaniment changes to the A–C–G ostinato, so the same tune runs over both piano patterns.

    6. High chords above an earlier phrase

      The recurring melody drops out and the piano’s ostinato continues alone. The opening figures return briefly. The piano then plays high chords, with an earlier phrase in the lower register beneath them. The last notes keep a slight dissonance.
