Charles Ives / VI. Modernism

Piano Sonata No. 2, ‘Concord, Mass., 1840–60’

c. 1916–19; revised 1920s–40s · Piano; optional additional flute and viola passages

Four New England literary portraits, with Beethoven’s Fifth passing through them. A felt-covered board plays black-key clusters in Hawthorne; a flute may join the pianist in Thoreau.

4 recordings · 45 min

In the Hall

  1. I. EmersonEric Xi Xin Liang, piano · 2024
    15:38

    Ives crowds seven themes onto the first page of his portrait of Emerson, then spends the movement letting them speak. Beethoven’s four-note knock keeps breaking into the piano’s argument.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Seven themes crowd the page

      Seven themes crowd the opening page, some only a few notes long. They interrupt and overlap in the piano, including scraps of the melody that will sing so plainly in The Alcotts. Ives begins in the middle of the argument.

    2. Two hundred unexpected parcels

      In January 1921 Ives mails two hundred copies of the Concord Sonata to people in the music world. He has paid for the printing himself. An insurance executive’s enormous piano sonata arrives on their desks, accompanied by a book of essays.

    3. Emerson and the crowd

      Behind this piano movement stands an unfinished concerto from 1910–11. Ives casts the soloist as Emerson and the orchestra as the crowd answering him. The sonata brings both sides of that exchange under one pianist’s hands.

    4. A verse among the speeches

      A quiet lyrical theme makes room in the piano’s argument. Ives calls it verse in the score: Emerson’s poetry beside his prose. The tune leads towards an apparent restart and then a large Beethoven-based climax.

    5. Downward tune, upward bass

      A high melody descends while figures rise beneath it in the bass. The melody comes round repeatedly, gaining notes and thicker chords each time. This middle episode begins and ends softly, with its great swell between.

    6. The song starts to multiply

      The Emerson theme returns in a delicate, song-like passage. Then the piano grows busy with fugue-like entries, largely drawn from the earlier verse theme. One line begins before another has finished.

    7. A score still changing

      Ives starts revising the sonata by 1921, with Emerson receiving particular attention. A substantially revised edition reaches print in 1947. For more than twenty years after mailing the score, he is still changing the notes.

    8. Old phrases, another outburst

      Music from the opening episodes returns. Beethoven’s repeated-note figure again drives towards a C minor climax, answering the great outburst in the movement’s first third.

    9. The knock grows distant

      The Emerson theme breaks into pieces. Beethoven’s four-note figure echoes in A major and E♭ major, keys a tritone apart. At the end, the knock survives as a slow, dark phrase in the bass.

    How it is joined

    1. A quiet verse emerges

      The overlapping piano phrases make room for a quiet lyrical theme, labelled verse. Its clear upper line begins another episode, which eventually grows into the first great C minor outburst on Beethoven’s repeated-note figure.

    2. A high tune above rising figures

      After the Beethoven climax, a descending melody starts quietly high in the piano. Rising bass figures move beneath it. The melody repeats with added notes and thicker chords, building a long arch that will end softly as it began.

    3. The Emerson tune sings again

      The repeated high melody subsides. A delicate song-like version of the Emerson theme follows in the piano, then the earlier verse theme supplies much of the fugue-like writing. The new lines pick up tunes already heard among the louder chords.

    4. C minor breaks into distant echoes

      The later Beethoven climax in C minor begins to dissolve. The Emerson tune breaks apart, while the repeated-note figure sounds in A major and E♭ major. These distant echoes grow quieter until the piano’s bass is left with the slow final statement.

  2. II. HawthorneEric Xi Xin Liang, piano · 2024
    12:42

    Frost on a window, a train to heaven, a hymn interrupted by a circus: Hawthorne’s stories set Ives’s piano loose. This scherzo runs from delicate black-key clusters to ragtime played with fists.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Frost on the window

      Arpeggios whirl across the piano. Ives links the opening to the children in Hawthorne’s The Three Golden Apples, waking to frost on the window. The glittering figures soon set a train-like motion running.

    2. A board on black keys

      The score calls for a felt-covered board, fifteen inches long, to press neighbouring black keys high on the piano. The clusters sound softly while a slower melody moves below. A handful of clashing notes can make a remarkably gentle accompaniment.

    3. The piano starts ragging

      The slow melody breaks into a fast rag. Accents arrive just before the beat, jostling the piano’s forward motion. Ives keeps changing the tunes while those displaced accents do their mischief.

    4. Heaven by railway

      Hawthorne’s The Celestial Rail-Road, published in 1843, makes Bunyan’s arduous pilgrimage into a comfortable train ride. The passengers can stow their sins with the luggage. Ives’s scherzo borrows the train’s bustle and the story’s irreverence.

    5. A hymn stops the dance

      The rag is interrupted by the quiet hymn Martyn, associated with the words “Jesus, lover of my soul”. Its shape resembles the sonata’s recurring melody. The busy piano music breaks back in, and the hymn interrupts again.

    6. A hymn against the circus

      In his 1920 Hawthorne essay, Ives pictures an old hymn haunting a churchyard while a circus parade comes down Main Street. The hymn shelters the people among the graves from the parade’s noise.

    7. A march, then more ragtime

      A march steps out of the ragtime, with piano clusters imitating drums. It slows to a pause before a longer rag begins. That passage works towards the great eruption of fist clusters.

    8. Columbia comes in pieces

      After the cluster climax, the piano starts slowly again. The left hand introduces phrases of Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean. The patriotic tune begins to take shape among the fragments.

    9. The scraps become a song

      Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean grows more recognisable as its phrases accumulate. Ives works backwards from the usual theme and variations: the tune emerges after its pieces have already been tossed around the piano.

    10. Tunes inside the whirlwind

      Rapid triplet figures race across the keyboard, carrying scraps of familiar tunes with them. Near the end, Columbia and the quiet hymn make their last appearances before a final splash across the piano.

    How it is joined

    1. Black keys held above a tune

      The rapid piano figures stop racing. In the slow episode, neighbouring black keys sound softly high on the keyboard, pressed together with a felt-covered board. A languid melody moves beneath the cluster, at a quite different pace from the opening arpeggios.

    2. The piano breaks into a rag

      The soft high clusters and slow lower melody break into fast ragtime. Accented notes fall just before the beat, making the piano lurch forward. The held black-key chords are replaced by quick, sharply articulated phrases.

    3. Martyn interrupts the rag

      The piano’s ragtime breaks off for a quiet statement of Martyn. The hymn’s longer notes resemble the sonata’s recurring melody. The rag returns between two hymn statements, so each passage interrupts the other.

    4. A march steps out of the rag

      The rag’s third continuation leads abruptly into a march. Piano clusters imitate drum strokes beneath its firmer tread. The march slows to a pause, then a longer ragtime passage begins and works towards the fist-cluster climax.

    5. Fists stop, a tune starts

      The long ragtime passage culminates in fist clusters. Afterwards the piano begins slowly again, and the left hand introduces phrases of Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean. The patriotic song arrives a little at a time.

    6. The left hand pieces Columbia together

      The piano develops the left hand’s scraps of Columbia, the Gem of the Ocean into increasingly recognisable phrases. The song grows clearer as the music gathers speed and force around it, with interruptions still breaking up its course.

    7. Triplets sweep across the piano

      After the play with Columbia, complex triplet figures take over the piano. Fragments of tunes pass through the running notes. The long rush continues towards the last recalls of the patriotic song and hymn.

  3. III. The AlcottsEric Xi Xin Liang · 2024
    5:47

    At the Alcotts’ piano, hymns and Scottish songs rub shoulders with Beethoven’s Fifth. Ives turns that household music into the broad C major melody at the heart of the Concord Sonata.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A hymn in two keys

      The piano sings a hymn-like melody in B♭ while the bass chords belong to A♭. The tune is almost complete already: it is the melody that keeps appearing, in pieces, throughout the Concord Sonata.

    2. Beth at the spinet

      In his 1920 essay, Ives points to a “little old spinet-piano” at Orchard House. His picture mingles the Alcott family with Little Women: Beth plays Scottish airs and tries her hand at Beethoven’s Fifth.

    3. Beethoven raises his voice

      The short-short-short-long figure of Beethoven’s Fifth pushes through the piano chords. It meets another borrowed tune, Missionary Hymn, whose repeated notes also drop by a third. Ives sets them against one another in different keys.

    4. Music for the parlour

      A song-like piano passage brings the music indoors again. Its turns suggest Scottish folk songs; twice it brushes against the wedding music from Wagner’s Lohengrin. Ives lets the parlour piano enjoy itself.

    5. Kirkpatrick at Town Hall

      On 20 January 1939 John Kirkpatrick gives the complete sonata its public première at New York’s Town Hall. He has already played it from memory at a private concert in Cos Cob. The score has been circulating for eighteen years.

    6. The song starts to strain

      The Scottish-song turns stretch into a tense chromatic passage. The piano chords crowd around the melody, working towards a much broader statement of the sonata’s recurring tune.

    7. C major, with both hands

      The whole melody opens out in broad C major chords. Its climbing do–re–mi and Beethoven’s repeated-note figure now belong to one continuous tune. The piano seems almost too small for it.

    8. One last B♭

      As the tune fades, Ives slips back into B♭ for a moment. Then the piano closes on C major, simply and quietly.

    How it is joined

    1. The piano returns to the parlour

      After the Beethoven climax, the recurring melody returns quietly over dissonant piano chords. The next episode separates a song-like upper line from its accompaniment. Scottish turns and a glimpse of Wagner’s wedding music replace the pounding repeated notes.

    2. The parlour tune loses its bearings

      The piano keeps the parlour tune’s Scottish turns but alters them with chromatic notes. Increasingly crowded chords unsettle the easy accompaniment. The little song grows into a tense link before the broad C major melody.

    3. The hands agree on C major

      The chromatic piano passage opens into the complete recurring melody in C major. Both hands play broad chords beneath its do–re–mi opening and Beethoven-derived repeated notes. The fragments that crowded the earlier movements now fit into a single singing phrase.

    4. B♭ before the last C

      The broad C major chords grow quieter. Ives briefly brings back B♭, recalling the opening key, then closes with a C major chord. The last change of harmony happens inside the slowing piano phrase.

  4. IV. ThoreauEric Xi Xin Liang, piano · 2024
    10:31

    Ives’s Thoreau walks around Walden Pond, grows restless, then settles beside a repeated A–C–G in the piano’s bass. The Concord Sonata ends with delicate high chords and a question still hanging underneath.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Two keys in the mist

      Quiet chords rise through the piano, sounding in two keys at once. Their little upward steps will return near the end. For now, each seems to appear through the haze and disappear again.

    2. An autumn day at Walden

      In his 1920 essay, Ives sets Thoreau beside Walden Pond on an autumn day: “a shadow of a thought at first, colored by the mist and haze over the pond”. The mist lifts; Thoreau grows restless and starts walking.

    3. Three walks from G major

      A plain G major chord starts the first of three walking episodes. Each quickens and grows louder before subsiding. The last builds towards a ringing, bell-like climax.

    4. Three notes keep returning

      A–C–G rises from the bass, over and over. Above it a melody wanders in another key. The repeated notes let the piano keep moving while the tune takes its time.

    5. The chords become insistent

      The repeated piano chords grow tense and dissonant. Earlier in this stretch, a dotted rhythm has begun to reveal its kinship with the sonata’s recurring melody; now repetition drives the music towards a climax.

    6. A–C–G comes back

      The climax dies away in converging chords. The repeated bass figure and its wandering melody return, until figures from the opening page begin to stir the piano again.

    7. A symphony without Boston

      Thoreau plays the flute, but Ives is more interested in his ear. In the 1920 essay he writes that Thoreau “did not have to go to Boston to hear ‘the Symphony’”. There is music enough beside the pond.

    8. The melody across four portraits

      The sonata’s recurring melody returns above repeating figures from this movement’s opening. Later the A–C–G pattern takes over beneath it. The tune that filled The Alcotts now sings inside a much quieter ending.

    9. High chords, an unanswered bass

      The opening figures return briefly, followed by delicate chords high on the piano. An earlier phrase sounds beneath them. The final notes keep a little dissonance: the sonata closes without the plain C major certainty of The Alcotts.

    How it is joined

    1. A G major chord starts the walk

      The rising piano figures lead to a plain G major chord. It begins the first of three walking episodes, each growing quicker and louder before falling back. The same G major chord will start the next two attempts.

    2. The bass settles on three notes

      The last walk reaches a bell-like climax, then the piano begins repeating A–C–G in the bass. A melody in another key moves freely above it. The left hand keeps returning to the same three notes while the right hand wanders.

    3. The repeated chords grow harder

      The piano grows more animated, with a dotted-rhythm figure related to the sonata’s recurring melody. Repeated dissonant chords build towards a climax. The steady recurrence that first held the bass in place now makes the upper chords increasingly insistent.

    4. Converging chords recover the bass figure

      The dissonant climax subsides in converging piano chords. A–C–G returns below the earlier wandering melody. Figures from the opening page then interrupt them and gradually grow more agitated.

    5. The recurring melody sings above the piano

      The sonata’s recurring melody emerges above repeating figures from the introduction. As the melody continues, the accompaniment returns to A–C–G. The same tune now spans two of the movement’s most persistent piano patterns.

    6. High chords above an earlier phrase

      The recurring melody fades, leaving the piano’s repeated pattern. A brief return of the opening figures leads to delicate high chords, with an earlier phrase beneath them. The last notes retain a slight dissonance.
