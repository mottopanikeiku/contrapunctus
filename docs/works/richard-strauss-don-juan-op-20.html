Richard Strauss / IV. Fin de siècle

Don Juan

1888 · Op. 20 · Orchestra

After Nikolaus Lenau, first conducted by Strauss in Weimar in 1889. The headlong E major opening ends in a spent E minor.

14 min

The recording

  1. Acoustic recording · four disc sidesSymphony Orchestra (Gramophone Company), Albert Coates · 28 April and 5 May 1922
    14:09

    Strauss sends Don Juan leaping up the violins in E major, then stops him cold in E minor. Albert Coates conducts this 1922 acoustic recording, originally spread across four sides of two records.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Up the violin strings

      A flourish launches Don Juan into E major. The violins’ theme keeps leaping higher, with quick notes rushing between its long, accented strides. Strauss gives his hero scarcely a moment to draw breath.

    2. Lenau’s restless Don Juan

      Strauss prints three extracts from Nikolaus Lenau’s verse drama before the score. This Don Juan keeps pursuing a love that no single encounter can satisfy. The last extract finds the world empty and dark around him.

    3. One violin begins to plead

      The first love scene brings a solo violin forward. Its line pleads and lingers before the melody opens out through the orchestra. Don Juan’s upward-rushing theme will soon interrupt the singing.

    4. Blue in the face

      At the Weimar rehearsals, the horn and trumpet players blow themselves “blue in the face”. Strauss tells his father so on 8 November 1889, delighted with the sound. Three days later he conducts the première.

    5. Four sides, two records

      Coates’s performance reaches its first owners on two twelve-inch Victor records, numbered 55176 and 55177. The music takes four sides. A listener at home has to lift the needle, turn a disc and eventually fetch the other record.

    6. The oboe’s long confession

      A low-string serenade and flute replies lead towards the oboe’s long melody. Strauss surrounds it with muted strings. After the opening’s headlong rush, the score gives one wind player an extraordinary amount of room to sing.

    7. Four horns, one call

      Strauss gives four horns a broad call in unison. It joins the opening theme in the restless middle of the piece, where quick figures race around the larger melodies. The horn call will return at the last great crest.

    8. A harp, a hangover

      Strauss calls this the “hangover passage” in his letter to his father on 8 November 1889. The score has trembling harp chords and strings played close to the bridge, giving the quiet recollection a thin, glassy edge.

    9. The violin climb returns

      The main theme returns in E major, with the violins climbing again. The horn call joins the final drive. Strauss keeps pressing beyond the point where this music might have made a triumphant ending.

    10. A trumpet and the dark

      A sudden silence stops the rush. A trumpet note cuts through the wind chord, and the violins tremble beneath it. E major turns to E minor. The last gestures have none of the opening’s appetite.

    How it is joined

    1. The solo violin opens a love scene

      An expressive melody first breaks into the rush at fortissimo. The orchestra then makes room for a solo violin, whose pleading phrases prepare the full statement of that melody. One player’s lingering line opens into the first broad love scene.

    2. Low strings make room for the oboe

      The upward-rushing theme interrupts the first love scene. In the next, violas and cellos begin a serenade, answered by the flute. The oboe then takes the long melody above muted strings, stretching its phrases across the quiet accompaniment.

    3. The horn call takes command

      A broad unison call for four horns carries Don Juan away from the oboe’s love scene. Marked sehr energisch, it becomes a partner to the opening theme in the development. Quick orchestral figures gather around these two large ideas.

    4. The harp trembles under a recollection

      The driven middle gives way to quiet recollections of the love music. Strauss writes trembling chords for the harp and asks the violas and second violins to bow close to the bridge. Their thin sound leaves the remembered melodies exposed.

    5. The violins recover E major

      The quiet recollections release the main theme in E major. The violins restore their upward leaps and the orchestra gathers behind them. The horn call returns later in the drive, bringing the work’s two most forceful themes into its closing stretch.

    6. Silence, then a dissonant trumpet

      The rushing music breaks off. Wind chords enter the silence, pierced by a dissonant trumpet note, and the violins continue in tremolo. E remains the home note, but the chord turns minor. The orchestra spends its remaining phrases quietly.
