Jean-Philippe Rameau / I. The Baroque

Nouvelles Suites de pièces de clavecin · Suite

c. 1728 · Harpsichord

La Poule makes a whole drama from a clucking repeated note. The collection also includes Les Sauvages, L’Enharmonique and the A minor Gavotte with six doubles.

7 min

The recording

  1. Gavotte et six doublesMarcelle Meyer, piano · 29–30 October 1953
    6:47

    Marcelle Meyer plays Rameau’s gavotte and six variations on the piano. A measured dance becomes a workout for the fingers, with quick runs passing from one hand to the other.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A dance in two halves

      The gavotte moves in two broad beats, with trills and small turns decorating its tune. Each half repeats. Rameau keeps these phrases and their cadences through six doubles — variations that give the fingers steadily more to do.

    2. An organist moves to Paris

      In 1722, Rameau moves from Clermont to Paris and publishes his Traité de l’harmonie. He already has years of church organ playing behind him. His first opera at the Paris Opéra, Hippolyte et Aricie, follows in 1733, when he is fifty.

    3. The right hand starts running

      The first double sets a continuous stream of semiquavers above a slower tune in the left hand. The gavotte’s chords still arrive at their familiar places, but the right hand now fills the spaces between them.

    4. A theory of chord roots

      In the preface to his Traité de l’harmonie (1722), Rameau declares that “music is a science which must have definite rules”. His central rule: a chord and all its inversions share one root. The basse fondamentale, the imagined line of those roots, is the true bass beneath the music.

    5. Now the left hand runs

      The second double turns the arrangement upside down. The left hand takes the semiquavers, climbing through broken chords, while the right hand holds the slower notes. The busy part of the piano has moved below the tune.

    6. A voice between the others

      The third double puts quick notes inside the chords. A held upper note and a slower bass surround the moving middle voice. The piano seems to have more than two hands at work.

    7. Two strikes on one note

      The fourth double makes the pianist repeat notes in quick pairs before moving on. The tune bounces from one pair to the next, with a few running figures breaking through. Rameau keeps the gavotte’s two broad beats underneath.

    8. The right hand leaps

      The fifth double sends the right hand jumping between separated notes. Short runs bridge the gaps, while slower bass notes keep the dance grounded. The same A minor phrases now demand a much wider reach.

    9. The bass has the last word

      The sixth double gives the running figures to the left hand beneath held right-hand chords. Low and high notes alternate within the bass line. The whole set ends with this variation’s cadence in A minor.

    How it is joined

    1. Semiquavers fill the dance’s spaces

      The gavotte closes in A minor. The first double begins the same phrases with continuous right-hand semiquavers above slower left-hand notes. On Meyer’s piano, the tune now sits beneath the running part; the two-beat dance continues.

    2. The flowing line breaks into pairs

      The third double completes its A minor cadence. The fourth restarts the dance with pairs of repeated notes, each pair nudging the tune onwards. The moving inner voice has become a succession of quick repeated strikes above the bass.
