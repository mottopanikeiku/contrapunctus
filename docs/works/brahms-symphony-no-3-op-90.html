Johannes Brahms / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 3, Op. 90

1883 · Op. 90 · Orchestra

Every movement ends quietly. In the finale, the first movement’s opening returns after the battle has spent itself.

6 min

The recording

  1. III. Poco allegrettoMusopen Symphony (Commons credit; ensemble attribution disputed)
    6:10

    The Third Symphony’s C-minor interlude begins with a cello melody that seems almost too private for an orchestra. When it returns, a solo horn sings it, and the cellos weave a new line underneath.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The cellos’ song

      The cellos sing high in their range, softly, in C minor. Above them the two violin sections pass little triplet figures back and forth; below, the double basses pluck. The melody keeps leaning across the three-beat bar.

    2. A turn towards major

      Before the C-minor cadence has quite finished, the cellos begin a gentler phrase in major, marked dolce. The first violins join them a sixth above. For a moment the bass stays on C while the melody reaches out across the bar lines.

    3. Rooms in Wiesbaden

      In the summer of 1883 Brahms interrupts a trip along the Rhine and rents rooms in Wiesbaden to finish the Third Symphony. He is fifty. Six years have passed since the Second.

    4. Chords across the bar

      The winds hold their chords across the bar lines, making the upbeat feel like the beginning. A♭ minor alternates with A♭ major. The cellos slip between the chords with a syncopated descent from A♭ to E♭.

    5. Strings alone in B

      The winds fall silent. Strings begin a swaying melody in B major, and the downbeat becomes clear again. As the phrase climbs, the double basses join with their bows: until now they have only plucked.

    6. Brahms’s Eroica

      Hans Richter conducted the Vienna Philharmonic in the première on 2 December 1883. According to the critic Eduard Hanslick, he had christened the new symphony “Brahms’s Eroica” shortly before. All four movements end quietly, this one included.

    7. The horn takes over

      A solo horn brings back the opening song in C minor. The cellos, free of the melody, add wide upward arpeggios beneath it. The tune has acquired a warmer, more distant voice.

    8. The oboe sings it

      The horn hands the song on, and a solo oboe takes it where the first violins sang it the first time. Beneath, the cellos keep up their wide upward arpeggios, and the violins now share the rippling triplets between them.

    9. Violins and cellos together

      The melody spreads across three octaves in first violins and cellos. Clarinets join the rippling accompaniment, while flute, oboe and horns take the smaller answering phrases. This is the song’s fullest statement.

    10. The last wind chords

      The wind chords from the middle section return, still crossing the bar lines. This time they lead back to C minor. After one last swell, the strings pluck beneath the held final chord.

    How it is joined

    1. A♭ minor interrupts the cadence

      The C-minor cadence is followed within the same bar by an A♭-minor chord. Winds hold across the bar line; the cellos answer with a syncopated descent towards E♭. The written metre remains 3/8, but the chords make its upbeats sound like downbeats.

    2. Three notes climb towards home

      Clarinet and bassoon try the song’s first three notes in A♭ minor, then B♭ minor, over chromatic string chords. The sequence reaches C minor; flute and oboe lengthen the notes, and the horns hold C. The solo horn then unfolds the complete melody above new cello arpeggios.

    3. The wind chords return

      After the song’s C-minor cadence, winds recall the chords that led into the middle section. Their A♭-minor colouring soon turns back towards C. Strings join them on a diminished seventh; a rising phrase stretches across the 3/8 bars before three descending chords bring the music to rest.
