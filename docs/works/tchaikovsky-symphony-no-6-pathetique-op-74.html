Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 6 “Pathétique”, Op. 74

1893 · Op. 74 · Orchestra

The march’s blazing finish is followed by an Adagio lamentoso. In the last pages the orchestra shrinks to low strings and the symphony ends quietly in B minor.

10 min

The recording

  1. IV. Finale — Adagio lamentosoMusopen Symphony
    10:19

    The finale begins with a violin melody that neither violin section plays complete: the notes pass between them. After the preceding march’s G-major finish, this slow lament takes the symphony back to B minor and leaves it there.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One tune, two violin sections

      The first and second violins exchange notes of a falling melody. Neither section has the whole tune on its page; together they make one continuous lament. The first chord already aches, a half-diminished chord in the symphony’s B-minor home.

    2. The ending comes first

      On 23 February 1893, months before the orchestration, Tchaikovsky tells Vladimir Davydov that the finale will be “a long drawn-out adagio”. In the same letter he writes of feeling blissfully sure that he can still compose.

    3. D major, still falling

      Horns support a broad new melody in the first violins and violas. It is in D major, and the trombones answer it. The tune still travels downwards, but its long phrases have room to sing.

    4. Bewilderment in Petersburg

      Tchaikovsky conducts the first performance in Saint Petersburg on 28 October 1893. Two days later he writes to his publisher, Jurgenson: “It’s not that it displeased, but it has caused some bewilderment.” He adds that he is more proud of it than of any of his other works.

    5. The scales rush down

      The broad tune gathers speed and fills the orchestra. Fast downward scales carry its climax; the fall that began as a quiet violin lament now runs through the ensemble.

    6. The lament returns

      The strings bring back the opening B-minor melody. Its falling phrases stretch further as the orchestra takes them up, pressing towards another climax.

    7. The most sincere of all

      On 14 August 1893, still scoring, Tchaikovsky tells his nephew Vladimir Davydov that the symphony is “the most sincere of all my creations. I love it as I have never loved any of my other musical offspring.” Davydov is its dedicatee.

    8. Gong and low brass

      A single tam-tam stroke marks the withdrawal from the climax. Trombones and tuba sing a quiet chorale after the long hold on F♯, the dominant of B minor.

    9. The song in B minor

      The broad melody returns, now in B minor. Muted strings descend over the double basses’ repeated low B. The tune that opened a window onto D major has reached the symphony’s darkest room.

    10. Nine days later

      Nine days after the première, at a quarter past three on the morning of 6 November 1893, Tchaikovsky dies of cholera in his brother Modest’s flat in Saint Petersburg. Twenty days after the première, Eduard Nápravník conducts the symphony in a memorial concert.

    11. Cellos and basses remain

      The final descent passes into the cellos and double basses. The symphony ends down here, almost out of reach, with B minor still sounding.

    How it is joined

    1. The bassoon leads towards D major

      A descending bassoon line dies away over horns and strings. At the Andante, the horns support a new D-major melody in the first violins and violas. Trombones answer its broad downward sweep.

    2. The strings reopen the lament

      The Andante’s climax breaks into fast descending scales and subsides. The opening string melody returns in B minor, its long falling phrases replacing the rush of notes.

    3. F♯ fades into the brass chorale

      The climax holds F♯ in the bass and gradually loses force. A single tam-tam stroke sounds in the withdrawal; quiet trombones and tuba take over with a chorale. Their chords round off the dominant harmony before the last B-minor return.

    4. The broad melody settles into B minor

      After the low-brass chorale, muted strings recover the broad second melody in B minor. Double basses pulse on low B underneath it. The falling phrases pass progressively into the lower strings as the other instruments stop.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. IV. Finale — Adagio lamentosoBerlin Philharmonic Orchestra; Wilhelm Furtwängler, conductor · October–November 1938, Beethovensaal, Berlin
    10:09

    Furtwängler and the Berlin Philharmonic play Tchaikovsky’s slow finale, recorded in Berlin in 1938. Its falling violin melody passes from one section to the other before the music sinks to the double basses’ low B.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A tune shared between desks

      The first and second violins pass the falling melody between them, one note at a time. Each section’s part looks broken on the page. Together they sing a continuous line above the opening half-diminished chord.

    2. The composer’s private ending

      In February 1893 Tchaikovsky writes to Vladimir Davydov about his planned finale: “a long drawn-out adagio”. He is delighted with the new symphony, whose programme he intends to keep secret.

    3. Horns beneath the song

      The Andante brings a broad melody in D major. First violins and violas sing above the horns, and trombones answer them. Even this warmer tune moves downwards.

    4. Back to B minor

      The opening lament returns in the strings. Tchaikovsky stretches its falling phrases across the orchestra, building again from the melody that began the movement.

    5. The lament fills the orchestra

      Strings and brass carry the returning lament towards its last climax. F♯, the dominant of B minor, holds beneath the agitated melody before the music begins its long withdrawal.

    6. Pride after the première

      On 30 October 1893, two days after conducting the première in Saint Petersburg, Tchaikovsky writes to his publisher Jurgenson. The symphony has caused bewilderment, he says, but “I’m more proud of it than any of my other works”.

    7. Low B beneath the strings

      The last return of the broad melody is in B minor. Muted strings descend above the double basses’ repeated low B. The closing pages leave more and more of the music to the cellos and basses.
