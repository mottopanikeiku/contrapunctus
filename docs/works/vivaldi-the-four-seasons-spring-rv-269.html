Antonio Vivaldi / I. The Baroque

The Four Seasons · Spring, RV 269

1725 · Op. 8 Nos. 1–4; RV 269, 315, 293, 297 · Solo violin, strings and continuo

Four concertos, printed with sonnets and with letters linking words of the poems to musical events. Their keys are E major, G minor, F major and F minor.

3 recordings · 11 min

In the Hall

  1. I. AllegroJohn Harrison, violin; Wichita State University Chamber Players; Robert Turizziani, conductor · 6 February 2000
    3:35

    John Harrison and the Wichita State University Chamber Players play the first movement, Allegro, of Spring, RV 269, from The Four Seasons. In it three violins imitate birds, running semiquavers represent water, and a storm interrupts the sunshine, and the score names what each of these spring sounds stands for.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening in E major

      The 1725 sonnet begins: “Giunt’é la Primavera e festosetti / La Salutan gl’Augei con lieto canto” — “Spring has arrived, and the birds joyfully greet her with happy song.” The strings establish E major in firm phrases, each followed by a soft echo.

    2. Spring at the Concert Spirituel, Paris

      Spring was the work played on eleven of the sixteen Vivaldi occasions at the Concert Spirituel in Paris between 1736 and 1763. Michel Corrette turned it into a motet in 1765, and Rousseau made it into a piece for solo flute in 1775.

    3. The streams, bars 31–40

      “E i fonti allo Spirar de’ Zeffiretti / Con dolce mormorio Scorrono intanto” — “Meanwhile the streams flow with a sweet murmur beneath the little breezes.” The violins play the streams of the sonnet in semiquavers. Repeated bass notes hold the passage in E major.

    4. Storm episode, bars 44–59

      “Vengon’ coprendo l’aer di nero amanto / E Lampi, e tuoni ad annuntiarla eletti” — “Lightning and thunder come, chosen to announce her, covering the sky with a black cloak.” Vivaldi set the storm as repeated notes beneath racing arpeggios from the solo violin. The music reaches C♯ minor.

    5. Return of the birds, C♯ minor

      “Indi tacendo questi, gl’Augelletti / Tornan’ di nuovo al lor canoro incanto” — “Then, when these fall silent, the little birds return to their enchanting song.” In bars 59–65 the trills return in C♯ minor. The continuo holds a long C♯ underneath them, where the bass was silent earlier.

    6. Final ritornello and piano echo

      The full strings play the closing phrase of the ritornello in E major. Vivaldi repeats it piano, and the movement ends with B moving to E in the bass.

    Joins

    1. Three violin parts after the cadence

      The bass resolves from B to E, and then the bass and violas stop. Three violin parts continue: the soloist and two orchestral violins exchange trills and short calls. Held notes on E and B in the violins keep the birdsong in E major.

    2. Bass returns on E for the refrain

      The bass returns on E, and the strings play the refrain’s second phrase together. A-major chords pass over the repeated E before B resolves to E at the cadence. The next stream stays in the same key, with paired violin semiquavers above repeated bass quavers.

    3. Thunder on the bass B

      The refrain reaches B major. Its bass B becomes a barrage of repeated demisemiquavers, marked thunder, and the solo violin follows with rapid arpeggios. The storm moves through E major towards C♯ minor, where the refrain’s closing phrase returns.

    4. Bass line returning to E major

      After the bird calls the strings play together again. Beneath the refrain the bass moves C♯–F♯–B–E, giving C♯ minor, F♯ minor seventh, B seventh and E major. F♯ minor seventh belongs to both keys and serves as the pivot back to E major, the key of the concerto’s opening.

    5. The tutti returns with the closing phrase

      The last solo ends over E and B in the bass. The tutti resumes on E with the refrain’s closing phrase, then repeats it piano. The bass closes with a B–E cadence in the home key, and the Allegro tempo continues unchanged to the last chord.

  2. II. LargoJohn Harrison, violin; Wichita State University Chamber Players; Robert Turizziani, conductor · 6 February 2000
    2:52

    The Largo is the second movement of Spring, RV 269, from Vivaldi’s The Four Seasons, and depicts a goatherd asleep with his dog beside him. Vivaldi gave the sleeping goatherd to the solo violin, the rustling leaves to the orchestral violins and the barking dog to the viola.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, sonnet and accompaniment

      “E quindi Sul fiorito ameno prato / Al caro mormorio di fronde e piante” — “And then, on the pleasant flowered meadow, to the gentle murmur of leaves and plants.” Vivaldi’s 1725 sonnet places the goatherd under trees. Two violin parts play dotted rhythms beneath the solo melody.

    2. The dog, viola

      “Dorme’l Caprar col fido can’ à lato” — “The goatherd sleeps with his faithful dog beside him.” The viola plays the bark, two notes followed by a rest, marked always very loud and sharply attacked. The continuo is silent throughout.

    3. Return of the opening, bar 20

      After a cadence in G♯ minor, the solo violin rests for a bar and in bar 20 restates its opening three notes, G♯, E, C♯. The rustling of the leaves continues through the rest. This time the melody reaches C♯ minor by a shorter route.

    4. Charles de Brosses in Venice, 1739

      In 1739 the French traveller Charles de Brosses wrote home about the concerts at Venice’s four girls’ hospitals. Some forty young women performed, and one conducted in a white habit with a bunch of pomegranates at her ear. His favourite was the Pietà, where Vivaldi had become violin master in 1703.

    5. Final cadence, solo violin

      The solo violin repeats its last approach to C♯ and trills on D♯ just before the resolution. It stops before the other strings. The murmuring violins and the dog’s two-note bark continue for another bar, and then the accompanying strings hold the final chord.

    Joins

    1. Viola moves through descending fifths

      The solo violin repeats its opening G♯–E–C♯ and then moves into longer notes. Beneath it, the viola’s barking figure follows a descending-fifths progression towards G♯ minor. The orchestral violins continue their soft dotted rhythm, and the cello stays silent.

    2. The solo’s return after a bar’s rest

      The solo violin holds G♯ at the cadence and then rests for a bar. Its opening figure, G♯–E–C♯, returns over C♯ in the viola. The two accompanying violin parts keep playing their rustling figure through the solo’s rest, and the melody re-enters while they are still playing.

    3. The violin climbs to C♯

      The solo violin starts again with G♯–E–C♯ over C♯ in the viola, then rises to A. Beneath the longer phrase the viola moves to F♯ and D♯. The violin climbs as high as C♯ and then descends towards its final cadence.

    4. The cadence returns

      The solo violin repeats C♯–F♯–A from the preceding phrase. Its trill on D♯ resolves to C♯ over the viola’s G♯–C♯ cadence. The solo violin then rests, and the accompanying parts complete their rustling and barking figures on a held C♯-minor chord.

  3. III. Allegro — Danza pastoraleJohn Harrison, violin; Wichita State University Chamber Players; Robert Turizziani, conductor · 6 February 2000
    4:04

    The finale of Spring, RV 269, from Vivaldi’s The Four Seasons is a Danza pastorale for nymphs and shepherds, in which the violins play over the long E and B of a country bagpipe. The dance moves into C♯ minor and E minor before it returns to the home key.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Bagpipe drone in the bass

      “Di pastoral Zampogna al Suon festante” — “To the merry sound of a shepherd’s bagpipe.” The 1725 sonnet names the instrument for this dance. Long E and B notes in the bass imitate the bagpipe’s drone, and the violin tune moves in four groups of three quavers.

    2. The dance in C♯ minor

      The strings play the rocking dance again, now in C♯ minor. The bass holds C♯ where it first held E. It then moves again, and the tune passes through E major towards B major.

    3. Spring sonnet, final lines, 1725

      “Danzan Ninfe e Pastor nel tetto amato / Di primavera all’apparir brillante” — “Nymphs and shepherds dance beneath their beloved shelter at spring’s brilliant arrival.” The sonnet places mythological nymphs beside working shepherds, and both dance to the same country dance.

    4. First opera, Vicenza, 1713

      In May 1713 Vivaldi’s first opera entirely his own, Ottone in villa, was given in Vicenza, away from the censorious eyes of his fellow Venetians. It was a success. He followed it with the oratorio La vittoria navale and then returned to Venice.

    5. Turn to E minor

      The dance returns in E major, then turns to E minor. G♯ becomes G♮, and the bass descends by semitones beneath the dance rhythm.

    6. First edition, Amsterdam, 1725

      The Amsterdam publisher Le Cène issued the Seasons in 1725 as the first four of the twelve concertos in Op. 8, Il cimento dell’armonia e dell’inventione. They were dedicated to Count Wenzel von Morzin, who had known them for some years. No autograph survives.

    7. Return to E major

      The long bass B resolves to E, and the full strings return to the dance in the major key (bars 79–89). The final phrase is repeated piano as an echo.

    Joins

    1. The bass leaves the E pedal

      The solo violin leaves the full-string dance while the bass holds E. The bass then begins to move, and ascending sequences pass through A and B towards C♯ minor. A descent by semitones leads into the next refrain.

    2. Return to the dance in C♯ minor

      The bass resolves G♯ to C♯. The full strings resume the opening dance in C♯ minor over a long C♯ pedal. The dotted rhythm is kept unchanged. A descending-fifths progression then takes the phrase through E major towards B major.

    3. Cadence in B major and solo episode

      The bass moves E–F♯–B at the cadence. The soloist begins a new episode in B major, with short bass notes punctuating the phrase. A chain of fifths moves through E, A and D, and the harmony then returns to B.

    4. The dance refrain turns to E minor

      The solo episode ends in B major. The bass returns to its long E and the strings begin the dance in E major. At bar 61 the key signature changes to one sharp. G♮ replaces G♯, and the refrain continues in E minor.

    5. A long B under the solo

      The bass descends E–D♯–D–C♯–C–B and holds the B. The orchestral violins stop, and the soloist plays above this dominant pedal. The B is held through the episode until the final return to E major.

    6. E major after the long B

      The bass moves from its long B to E, and the full strings resume the dance in E major. The soloist rejoins the orchestral violins. Long E and B notes support the last phrases, including their piano echo.

      The full strings bring back the refrain’s closing phrase in E major. Vivaldi repeats it softly, then ends with B resolving to E in the bass.

    Joins

    1. Three birds after the cadence

      The bass resolves B to E and falls silent with the violas. Three violin lines remain and exchange trills and short calls. Held E and B notes among the violins keep the birdsong in E major.

    2. The stream starts in E major

      A shortened refrain closes with B resolving to E in the bass. At letter C the violins begin flowing semiquavers, marked piano, over repeated E quavers. The new scene is in the same key, E major, and the same Allegro tempo.

    3. Thunder on the bass B

      The refrain reaches B major. Its bass B turns into a barrage of repeated demisemiquavers, marked thunder, and the solo violin plays rapid arpeggios in reply. The storm moves through E major towards C♯ minor. In C♯ minor the closing phrase of the refrain returns.

    4. Bass descends by fifths to E major

      After the bird calls the full strings return in bars 66–70. Beneath the refrain the bass moves C♯–F♯–B–E, with the harmony running C♯ minor, F♯ minor seventh, B seventh, E major. The repeated C♯ of the birdsong is the first step of this descending-fifths progression.

    5. Final solo to closing tutti

      The last solo ends over E and B in the bass. The tutti resumes on E with the refrain’s closing phrase, then repeats it piano. B resolves to E beneath the final cadence.
