Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Das Lied von der Erde (The Song of the Earth)

1908–09 · Tenor, alto (or baritone) and orchestra

Six songs on Hans Bethge’s free German versions of Tang-dynasty poems. The last, Der Abschied (The Farewell), lasts half an hour and ends on the word ewig — forever — repeated into silence. Mahler added the final lines himself.

6 recordings · 1 h 2 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Das Trinklied vom Jammer der Erde — Allegro pesanteCarl Martin Öhmann, tenor; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Kerstin Thorborg sings the alto songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    8:56

    The wine is ready, but the singer insists on a song about death before anyone drinks. Carl Martin Öhmann and Carl Schuricht open Das Lied von der Erde in Amsterdam in 1939 with blaring horns and a refrain that climbs a semitone each time it returns.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Horns before the wine

      The horns throw out a three-note call. Flutes flutter-tongue, trumpets rattle behind their mutes, and Öhmann has to sing high against the orchestra. The heading is Allegro pesante: fast music with heavy feet.

    2. Li Bai’s sorrowful song

      The song descends from Li Bai’s “Tale of Sorrowful Song”, which begins: “Sorrow comes; sorrow comes. Host has wine; pour not yet.” Bethge turned that into “Schon winkt der Wein im goldnen Pokale, doch trinkt noch nicht”, and gave the song its refrain: “Dunkel ist das Leben, ist der Tod”.

    3. The garden laid waste

      “Wenn der Kummer naht” — when sorrow draws near. The boast falls quiet. A solo violin and the harps accompany a garden in which joy and song wither; Mahler marks the voice “düster, zart” — gloomy, tender.

    4. The first dark refrain

      “Dunkel ist das Leben, ist der Tod” — dark is life, dark is death. The tenor’s refrain rests in G minor, with solo horn and harp arpeggios around it. Each later return lifts the same sentence one semitone higher.

    5. Back to the cellar

      The horns’ call and flutter-tongued flutes burst back at Tempo I subito. The next stanza addresses the host and his well-stocked cellar. The singer brings a lute; he considers a full cup worth more than all the kingdoms of the earth.

    6. Three blows of 1907

      In 1907 Mahler resigns from the Vienna Court Opera, “because I can no longer endure the rabble”, and signs a contract with the Metropolitan Opera on 21 June. Within weeks his elder daughter Maria dies of scarlet fever and diphtheria, and a doctor discovers a defect in his own heart.

    7. One semitone higher

      The second refrain climbs to A♭ minor. Öhmann has the same words, now a little higher in the throat. Schuricht lets these refrains move much more slowly than the drinking music around them.

    8. The orchestra takes the floor

      The tenor falls silent for a long interlude. An oboe comes forward, flutes flutter-tongue, and a muted trumpet picks out its notes above plucked strings. The music gathers into A♭ major before the voice returns.

    9. Cylinders of Chinese music

      In August 1908 Paul Hammerschlag visits Mahler at Toblach, finds him curious about China and Chinese music, and buys him phonograph cylinders of Chinese music recorded in China.

    10. The sky stays blue

      “Das Firmament blaut ewig” — the heavens are blue for ever. Öhmann’s line places the earth’s returning spring beside the short human life. The strings have just sung out at full strength; the tenor answers quietly, marked passionate.

    11. “Du aber, Mensch”

      The tenor turns on his listener: “Du aber, Mensch, wie lang lebst denn du?” — and you, man, how long do you live? Not a hundred years, he adds, to enjoy this rotten earth, and a long intensification begins.

    12. Moonlight on the graves

      The opening fanfares return with glockenspiel, triangle and muted brass. The tenor points towards a shape crouching on the graves: an ape, howling into the sweet scent of life. The drinking song has become a nightmare.

    13. Schuricht empties the cup

      At last the singer permits the wine. The toast opens in A major before the final dark refrain. Schuricht stretches the emptying of the cup almost to a standstill: Öhmann takes his time over “zu Grund” — to the bottom.

    14. Death leaves a dissonance

      The final refrain reaches A minor. On the last “Tod” — death — Mahler leaves a diminished-seventh chord above the tonic bass. Then the horns and flutes reclaim the opening tempo, and the orchestra ends the song with a short, hard chord.

    How it is joined

    1. A solo violin after the boast

      The sustained ending of the first boast softens into Sempre l’istesso tempo — still the same pace. Quiet strings and harps surround “Wenn der Kummer naht”; the solo violin soon adds a coaxing phrase. Mahler marks the tenor düster, zart.

    2. The harp slows into G minor

      Zurückhaltend. Ruhig. holds back the end of the garden stanza. Harp arpeggios replace the repeated chords, and a solo horn accompanies the refrain in G minor. The tenor’s line is marked sehr getragen — very sustained.

    3. The horns break into the refrain

      The held “Tod” meets a diminished-seventh chord over G. Tempo I subito then restores the horn call, flutter-tongued flutes and glockenspiel. Open trumpets join the renewed fanfare as the strings break into repeated notes.

    4. The same words rise a semitone

      The wine stanza ends on “Reiche dieser Erde”. A ritardando leads into a tempo, sehr ruhig, with harp arpeggios and plucked bass. The second refrain moves the first refrain’s G minor up to A♭ minor; the strings remain muted through its approach.

    5. A muted trumpet takes over

      The refrain’s held notes run into an orchestral interlude in the A♭ region. An oboe projects above the fading winds. At a tempo, plucked strings and harp chords begin again, followed by a muted trumpet and flutter-tongued flutes.

    6. The opening returns on the graves

      The tenor finishes the line about earth’s rotten trinkets. A rising woodwind rush brings back the opening fanfare, with glockenspiel, triangle and muted trumpets. The low strings strike plucked notes as the tenor turns towards the graves.

    7. A-major wine after the howl

      Brass raise their bells for the ape’s howl, with cymbal and bass drum beneath. The following ritardando brings the toast into A major. Winds and horns sustain as the tenor finally tells the company to drink; harp figures accompany the slowing phrase.

    8. A diminished chord, then the horns

      The last refrain settles into A minor, but its final “Tod” lands on a diminished-seventh chord over A. Tempo I releases the horns’ opening call and the high woodwinds. The orchestra carries the dissonance straight into its short final outburst.

  2. II. Der Einsame im Herbst — Etwas schleichend. ErmüdetKerstin Thorborg, contralto; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Carl Martin Öhmann sings the tenor songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    9:37

    Muted violins move softly beneath a solitary oboe, and Kerstin Thorborg sings of frost, a cold wind and a lamp that has gone out. The second song of Das Lied von der Erde saves its great outburst for a plea to the sun of love.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The oboe above the frost

      Muted first violins set out in quiet, unbroken quavers. A solo oboe begins a long melody above them. Mahler marks the music weary and somewhat creeping; the little violin steps continue even when the oboe pauses for breath.

    2. A woman at her loom

      Behind the song stands a poem by Qian Qi: a woman weaves through a long autumn night beside a failing lamp. Judith Gautier’s French adaptation drops the loom. Through Hans Bethge’s German version, the frost, fading light and loneliness reach Mahler.

    3. Blue mist on the lake

      The flute echoes the oboe. The alto begins quietly in D minor: autumn mist drifts blue over a lake, and frost lies on the grass like jade dust scattered by an artist. The moving violin line continues beneath the scene.

    4. Mahler needs his walks

      In July 1908, while composing these songs, Mahler struggles with his doctors’ restrictions on exercise. He writes to Bruno Walter: “I can’t work at a desk. For my inner activity I need outer activity.”

    5. The strings take the words

      After the golden lotus petals drift across the water, the singer falls silent. Violins and violas press into a tender, passionate melody. Winds and horns swell with them, then the music sinks back through the cellos.

    6. The little lamp goes out

      The opening pace returns with “Mein Herz ist müde” — my heart is weary. The alto sings without expression as the quavers pass through violas and cellos. Then the little lamp goes out with a crackle, and the strings play close to the bridge.

    7. A heart that tires

      In July 1907, after Maria’s death, a doctor at Maiernigg finds a valvular defect in Mahler’s heart, confirmed by a Vienna specialist. It is not fatal in itself, but such defects were known to lead to the bacterial endocarditis that kills him four years later. He sets these words a year later.

    8. A place to rest

      The singer turns towards a familiar resting place and asks for peace. Mahler marks the voice heartfelt. A solo violin answers, while the flowing accompaniment carries on beneath the plea.

    9. The sun of love

      Two harps sweep together beneath the singer’s great plea in E♭ major: will the sun of love never shine again? The voice stretches the word for love across the bar, then presses on towards the bitter tears.

    10. One word makes it gentler

      The full orchestra falls away. Over plucked cellos, the alto asks whether those tears will ever be dried gently. Mahler adds that last kindness — “mild” — to Bethge’s poem, then gives the remaining music to the instruments.

    11. The première, and a baritone

      Bruno Walter conducts the first performance in Munich on 20 November 1911, six months after Mahler’s death, with the Americans Sara Cahier and William Miller. At the alto’s entry in this song Mahler notes that, if necessary, the part may be sung by a baritone.

    12. The violin steps grow faint

      Oboe, bassoon and horn finish their phrases over the first violins’ continuing quavers. The violins slow and hold their last note. A few wind notes remain, marked to die away.

    How it is joined

    1. Clarinet trills bring the cold wind

      The oboe’s phrase subsides and the moving first violins fall silent. Over soft winds, the alto begins the second stanza, about flowers whose scent has gone. The voice is marked very restrained; then clarinet trills shiver beneath the words about the cold wind.

    2. The solo cello joins the section

      A solo cello answers the alto’s words about lotus petals on the water. At Zart drängend, the singer stops and the full cello section joins the violins and violas. Their rising phrases draw in woodwinds and horns, all pressing towards a crescendo.

    3. The cellos dwindle to four p’s

      The wind and string crescendo ends at fortissimo. The violins soften, and repeated cello quavers dwindle to pianississimo and beyond. Tempo I restores the opening pace; violas carry the moving notes beneath the alto’s plain statement that the heart is weary.

    4. Two harps carry the plea

      The singer’s long autumn grows into an orchestral crescendo. Two harps enter with sweeping unison arpeggios. In E♭ major, at Mit großem Aufschwung, the alto reaches for the sun of love; the winds soften beneath the beginning of this long phrase.

    5. A quiet question over plucked cellos

      The harps keep sweeping as the alto presses through the words about bitter tears. Then the first tempo returns abruptly, with nearly every instrument silent. The singer finishes the question over plucked cellos. Bassoon and oboe begin the postlude.

  3. III. Von der Jugend — Behaglich heiterCarl Martin Öhmann, tenor; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Kerstin Thorborg sings the alto songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    3:28

    Friends drink, talk and write verses in a green-and-white porcelain pavilion. Mahler paints this small scene with bright woodwinds, then turns it upside down in the pond.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A pavilion made of woodwinds

      A horn note and a touch of triangle open this tiny scene. Flutes, oboe and clarinets trade trills and quick, neat figures. When the tenor describes the porcelain pavilion, violins and violas join him with a bouncing bow-stroke.

    2. Sleeves rolled up for verses

      The music turns to G major, with chattering clarinets and a muted trumpet. Inside the little pavilion, friends drink, talk and write verses. Their silk sleeves are pushed back; their caps sit low on their necks.

    3. A pavilion that never stood

      The porcelain pavilion began as a mistranslation. Li Bai’s poem is called “Banquet at Tao’s Family Pavilion”, and the Chinese word for the surname Tao can also mean porcelain. Judith Gautier’s French turned the family’s house into china; Bethge and Mahler kept it.

    4. The pond becomes a mirror

      A solo violin sings above rocking strings as the music slows into G minor. The tenor describes the pond’s still surface. Everything the song has shown us is there again, reflected in the water.

    5. Toblach, summer 1908

      Mahler composes the songs largely in the summer of 1908, forbidden the long walks he needs. From Bethge’s 83 paraphrases of 38 poets he chooses seven. In early September he reports to Bruno Walter: “I was very diligent … I believe it is the most personal thing I have yet created.”

    6. The bridge turns upside down

      The first tempo returns suddenly in B♭ major, and the strings take off their mutes. In the tenor’s words, everything stands on its head: pavilion, friends and the bridge, whose reflected arch becomes a half-moon.

    How it is joined

    1. A muted trumpet in G major

      Oboes and clarinets carry the quick figures out of B♭ major. At rehearsal figure 5 the signature changes to G major: a muted trumpet joins the woodwinds, clarinets repeat little chords and the triangle keeps shimmering. The tenor enters a few bars later with the friends in the pavilion.

    2. A solo violin darkens the pond

      The woodwind figures subside while a solo violin carries a longer line. At Ruhiger, G turns from major to minor. The strings rock quietly beneath the violin, and the tenor begins to describe the scene’s reflection.

    3. The mutes come off

      Repeated ritardandos slow the strings almost to a halt. Tempo I subito brings the opening pace back at once, with flutes and oboes in B♭ major. The strings remove their mutes and resume their bouncing accompaniment as the tenor describes the world standing on its head.

  4. IV. Von der Schönheit — Comodo. DolcissimoKerstin Thorborg, contralto; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Carl Martin Öhmann sings the tenor songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    6:45

    Girls pick lotus flowers by a river; horsemen ride past, leaving one girl watching after them. In this 1939 Amsterdam performance, Kerstin Thorborg sings both the gentle river scene and the breathless gallop that tears through it.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Flowers by the river

      Trilling flutes and muted violins open a small, bright scene in G major. Thorborg’s first words place the girls at the water’s edge: “Junge Mädchen pflücken Blumen” — young girls pick flowers. The tune moves easily between bars of two and three beats.

    2. A lotus song, filtered twice

      The poem descends from Li Bai’s “Lotus-plucking Song”, set at Ruoye Brook near Shaoxing. The Marquis d’Hervey de Saint-Denys put it into French, Bethge into German verse, and Mahler retouched that. Li Bai’s original is far shorter than the song it became.

    3. Sunlight and silk sleeves

      G major slips down to E major. The violins remove their mutes, and a solo violin floats above the words about the breeze lifting the girls’ sleeves. The harp’s quiet chords leave room for the long vocal line.

    4. Horses before their riders

      Thorborg finishes on “Luft” — air — and the orchestra takes over. Stopped horns and muted trumpet and trombones begin to stir, with harp glissandi underneath. The horsemen arrive in the music well before the poem names them.

    5. A mandolin among the drums

      The march adds a mandolin to its drums and brass. Tambourine rattles, bass drum and cymbal mark the stride, and both harps strike chords. The small plucked instrument is part of this boisterous parade.

    6. Thorborg names the riders

      The orchestra suddenly drops to piano for the singer’s return. “O sieh, was tummeln sich für schöne Knaben” — see the handsome boys romping there. Timpani played with wooden sticks keep the hooves moving as the vocal part grows faster and more crowded.

    7. Mahler adds a cry

      In the horse’s lines Mahler adds a cry of his own: “Hei! Wie flattern im Taumel seine Mähnen, dampfen heiß die Nüstern!” — how his mane flies in a frenzy, how hot the nostrils steam. He also changes the girl’s pride from a lie, “Lüge”, to a pretence, “Verstellung”.

    8. The river reappears

      The horse’s mane flies and its nostrils steam. Then the opening pace returns in a single step. Muted violins and harp restore the sunlight on the water; Thorborg repeats the words “Gold’ne Sonne” — golden sun.

    9. A glance that lingers

      The girl’s proud bearing is a pretence. Her eyes follow the rider, and her heart still trembles after he has gone. Mahler writes “Ganz ruhig” — quite calm — while clarinet phrases and the violins carry on beneath Thorborg’s last words.

    10. A conductor who stayed with Mahler

      Carl Schuricht, born in Danzig in 1880, was Wiesbaden’s music director from 1912. He gave the city the Eighth Symphony in 1913 and Mahler weeks in 1920 and 1921. He conducts this performance in Amsterdam on 5 October 1939, when Mahler’s music has vanished from German concert programmes.

    How it is joined

    1. The violins uncover E major

      A ritardando ends the image of sunlight reflected in water. The music resumes in E major, a minor third below G, at a tempo (ruhiger). The violins take off their mutes; their high melody and the woodwinds’ little turns prepare the breeze stanza.

    2. The singer leaves the horses approaching

      After the voice’s final “Luft”, high flute and violin notes hang above the harp. Allmählich belebend sets the orchestra moving: stopped horns, muted trumpet and trombones, with the harp sweeping through D major. The march gathers speed towards its C-major harp glissando.

    3. Wooden sticks beneath the voice

      The brass-heavy march drops to piano subito. At Noch etwas flotter, Thorborg names the horsemen over oboes, clarinets, bassoons and strings. Timpani played with wooden sticks pick out the rhythm beneath her line.

    4. Muted violins stop the gallop

      The horse’s steaming nostrils end the racing vocal line. Tempo I subito (Andante) brings back muted violins, followed by the harp and a solo violin. The music returns to G major and to the words about golden sunlight on the water.

    5. The clarinets sustain the last glance

      The words about the girl’s pretended pride give way to Ganz ruhig. Quiet violin figures descend while the clarinets exchange short phrases. The final vocal line ends in G major; the winds and strings continue its small gestures into the closing bars.

  5. V. Der Trunkene im Frühling — Allegro. Keck, aber nicht zu schnellCarl Martin Öhmann, tenor; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Kerstin Thorborg sings the alto songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    4:50

    A bird announces spring; the drinker would prefer another cup. Carl Martin Öhmann sings this unruly little drama with the Concertgebouw Orchestra and Carl Schuricht in their live 1939 Das Lied von der Erde.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A drinker’s first question

      Oboes and clarinets trill above the horns; the strings wait. Öhmann enters with a question: “Wenn nur ein Traum das Leben ist” — if life is only a dream. Why bother with toil and trouble? The horn figures keep tripping over their grace notes.

    2. Li Bai wakes from drink

      The poem began as Li Bai’s “Feelings upon Awakening from Drunkenness on a Spring Day”. The Marquis d’Hervey de Saint-Denys made it a French “Un jour de printemps”, Hans Heilmann turned that into German, and Bethge into rhyme. The first thought, that life is a great dream and toil pointless, is already Li Bai’s.

    3. The bird wakes him

      The drinker wakes to birdsong. A solo violin curls above the softened orchestra; the flute answers, and the harp joins. “Ein Vogel singt im Baum” — a bird sings in the tree. Mahler holds back the pace around this small discovery.

    4. Is spring here already?

      The tenor asks whether spring has come. His repeated “mir ist” hesitates before “als wie im Traum” — as though in a dream. The solo violin and harp give the question space, with only a few low winds and strings beneath.

    5. Face to face with nothing

      In July 1908, composing again with difficulty, Mahler writes to Bruno Walter that he has lost all the clarity and reassurance he ever won and stands “vis-à-vis de rien”, face to face with nothing. A poem that calls life a dream and spring beside the point meets a composer whose doctors have forbidden exertion.

    6. A moment with the bird

      For a moment the drinker stays with the birdsong. D♭ major opens beneath his quiet line, and Mahler writes “Zögernd” and “Ganz langsam” — hesitant, very slow. Muted strings, bassoons and horns sustain the stillness around him.

    7. Another cup wins

      “Ich fülle mir den Becher neu” — I fill my cup again. The first tempo snaps back. Harp arpeggios and plucked cellos set the drinker on his feet, and the violins have taken off their mutes. He sings on until the moon appears.

    8. Spring can wait

      “Laßt mich betrunken sein!” — let me be drunk! Öhmann’s final answer dismisses the bird and its good news. An A-major Allegro sends woodwind runs tumbling over string chords; triangle and trumpet join the last flourish.

    How it is joined

    1. A solo violin wakes the drinker

      The drinker’s sleep gives way to a solo violin and quiet wind figures in A major. Noch ruhiger, then Zurückhaltend, slows his waking question. The harp enters with soft chords as the tenor locates the bird in its tree.

    2. Plucked cellos refill the cup

      The D♭-major pause begins to flow again at Etwas fließender. The strings remove their mutes. The tenor’s “Ich” leads straight into Tempo I subito, where harp arpeggios and plucked cellos accompany his freshly filled cup.

  6. VI. Der Abschied — SchwerKerstin Thorborg, contralto; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Carl Martin Öhmann sings the tenor songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    28:52

    Kerstin Thorborg sings the long farewell that closes Das Lied von der Erde: night falls, a friend is late, and a last drink is offered. Between the waiting and the parting, an orchestral funeral march crosses the evening landscape.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A low C, an oboe

      A low C sounds in contrabassoon and strings, with horns, harps and a soft tam-tam stroke around it. Then a solo oboe begins its restless phrase. The score says Schwer — heavy — and the bass notes have a long way to fall.

    2. Two friends, two Chinese poems

      Mahler joins two poems. Meng Haoran’s speaker waits for a friend among the mountains; Wang Wei’s offers a traveller a farewell drink. Their words reach him through French versions by d’Hervey de Saint-Denys and German poems by Hans Bethge.

    3. Not written in 1907

      Alma Mahler says the songs were sketched on lonely walks in the summer of 1907. Alfred Roller, who visited that summer, reports: “This summer remained without artistic fruit.” Bethge’s book was announced only on 5 October 1907; the dated manuscripts indicate that the songs were composed largely in the summer of 1908.

    4. The sun leaves the mountains

      The alto begins as a storyteller, quietly and without expression. The sun disappears behind the mountains; evening descends into the valleys. A cello holds one long note beneath the voice while a flute wanders freely above it.

    5. A silver boat on the sky-lake

      The orchestra hushes for the moon: “O sieh! Wie eine Silberbarke schwebt der Mond am blauen Himmelssee herauf” — look, the moon floats up like a silver boat on the blue lake of the sky.

    6. A practical word to the flautist

      The brook sings through the dark in a flute solo. Mahler adds a practical footnote: if the flautist lacks a large enough tone, the oboist takes over. The singer must keep her voice delicate enough to leave the instrumental melody clear.

    7. Mahler edits Bethge

      Bethge’s “hardworking people” go home longing for sleep. Mahler’s are “tired people”, who go home to learn forgotten happiness and youth anew in sleep. At the end of the poem he adds a cry of his own: “O Schönheit! O ewigen Liebens — Lebenstrunkne Welt!”

    8. Still waiting beneath the trees

      The evening scene gives way to the bare music of narration again. The flute curls above a held double-bass note. In the cool shade of the spruces, the singer waits for a friend and the last farewell.

    9. “For the first time”

      On 30 December 1917 Willem Mengelberg conducts Das Lied with the Vienna Philharmonic. Its concertmaster Arnold Rosé, who has played it before, tells the tenor Jac. Urlus: “Now we have heard Das Lied von der Erde for the first time.” Alma Mahler gives Mengelberg the manuscripts of this song the same day.

    10. How to conduct that?

      Bruno Walter recalled that Mahler showed him the score of this movement, pointed to one passage and asked: “Can you think of a way of conducting that? Because I can’t.” The voice and solo instruments flow over the barlines, and Mahler writes Ohne Rücksicht auf das Tempo: without regard for the tempo.

    11. The orchestra takes over

      The singer’s cry to a world drunk with life ends, and the orchestra continues alone. Cor anglais and strings recall the opening figures. A heavy funeral march grows out of them, with plucked bass notes, harp and tam-tam.

    12. How slow is the march?

      Conductors disagree about this march. Colin Davis’s 1981 Abschied, almost thirty-five minutes, is the slowest in Christian Utz’s survey of ninety-two recordings and fills 51 per cent of the whole cycle; in Klemperer’s 1951 recording it fills 43 per cent. Schuricht’s takes twenty-eight minutes fifty-two.

    13. A drink before parting

      The alto returns to plain narration. A friend dismounts and offers the farewell drink, then asks where the traveller is going and why. Low cellos and basses hold C beneath her; the tam-tam marks the silence around the words.

    14. A voice from the hall

      Just before this, at about 19:30, a woman in the hall shouts. The person who uploaded the recording hears “Deutschland muss raus … Herr Schuricht”, or perhaps “Deutschland über alles”. The concert falls five weeks into the Second World War and seven months before Germany invades the Netherlands.

    15. The traveller’s voice is veiled

      The traveller answers with a veiled voice: fortune has been unkind, and he is going into the mountains to find rest. The strings play very quietly near the fingerboard. Mahler asks the singer for a soft, expressive tone.

    16. Wang Wei: ask no more

      In Wang Wei’s poem the traveller says he is “at odds with the world” and goes home to rest by the South Hill. “Please go. Ask no more. Endless, the white clouds.” Bethge keeps the clouds, “ewig, ewig”; Mahler’s ending replaces them.

    17. Mahler makes the earth green

      Bethge’s traveller ends among eternal white clouds. Mahler writes a new ending: the heart waits for its hour, the earth blossoms in spring and grows green again, and the far horizons shine blue for ever. The singer stays with that last word.

    18. He never heard it

      Mahler never hears this music. Bruno Walter conducts the première in Munich on 20 November 1911, six months after Mahler’s death. Mahler had avoided numbering the work, for fear of a Ninth; he gave it its title on the sheet where he gave the number 9 to the symphony he had just completed.

    19. Soft, softer, pianississimo

      Willem Mengelberg’s score notes on its title page that Mahler himself thought this his most difficult work to perform. On the last page, where the music stills, Mengelberg wrote a number of times, to remind himself: “soft, softer, pianississimo”.

    20. The last word keeps returning

      “Ewig” — for ever — returns again and again. Harps, mandolin and celesta place small sounds among held wind and string chords. The last sung syllables fall from E to D; the final C-major harmony keeps an A within it.

    How it is joined

    1. A cello note after the silence

      The introduction ends in rests held by fermatas. At Fließend. Im Takt, a cello begins a long C and the alto enters in a plain, narrating tone. The flute’s winding countermelody supplies almost all the movement around the voice.

    2. Harps recover the opening tread

      The flute’s phrase and held cello note die away under fermatas. Tempo I brings back the opening bass tread in contrabassoon and harps. Trilling clarinet chords, the oboe’s curling line and horn replies lead into the moonlit scene.

    3. The flute above a held bass

      The words about the sleeping world lead into a long stillness. A held double-bass note remains. At Sehr gleichmäßig. Nicht eilen, the alto resumes the bare narration and the flute returns with its curling melody.

    4. Mandolin and harps replace the held note

      The flute descends and the bass note fades beneath a fermata. Fließend introduces mandolin and two harps in a new flowing accompaniment. Second violins take up the small repeated figure; first violins enter with a longer melody as the conductor gradually moves towards beating whole bars.

    5. The cor anglais continues alone

      The alto’s final phrase sinks into held winds and strings. At Mäßig, the cor anglais takes up the curling figure over short violin notes. Bassoon, horns and harps answer. This orchestral conversation leads into Schwer and the funeral march’s plucked low-string tread.

    6. The bass C survives the march

      The march dwindles through held chords and short wind replies. The harps strike their last notes sharply. Cellos and basses settle on a low C, with quiet tam-tam strokes, and the alto begins the story of the farewell drink.

    7. Quiet strings for a veiled voice

      Woodwinds and harps interrupt the narrator with echoes of the march. Her phrase about the friend’s veiled voice ends beneath a fermata. The strings enter pianississimo, then bow near the fingerboard; flute and horn phrases surround the friend’s soft answer.

    8. Harps open the greening earth

      The strings slow through Ritenuto molto, then hold their notes under a fermata. At Langsam, two harps begin quiet arpeggios. The alto sings of the earth blossoming again above sustained violins and plucked lower strings; Mahler asks for no increase in force.
