Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Das Lied von der Erde (The Song of the Earth)

1908–09 · Tenor, alto (or baritone) and orchestra

Six songs on Hans Bethge’s free German versions of Tang-dynasty poems. The last, Der Abschied (The Farewell), lasts half an hour and ends on the word ewig (forever), sung several times until the music stops. Mahler wrote the final lines himself.

6 recordings · 1 h 2 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Das Trinklied vom Jammer der Erde — Allegro pesanteCarl Martin Öhmann, tenor; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Kerstin Thorborg sings the alto songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    8:56

    In the first song of Das Lied von der Erde, the wine is ready, and the singer calls for a song about death before anyone drinks. Carl Martin Öhmann sang it with Carl Schuricht in Amsterdam in 1939. The horns open the song, and the refrain rises a semitone each time it returns.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening horn call

      The horns play a three-note call. Flutes flutter-tongue, muted trumpets rattle, and Öhmann sings high against the orchestra. The heading is Allegro pesante.

    2. Li Bai’s source poem

      The song derives from Li Bai’s “Tale of Sorrowful Song”, which begins: “Sorrow comes; sorrow comes. Host has wine; pour not yet.” Bethge rendered this as “Schon winkt der Wein im goldnen Pokale, doch trinkt noch nicht”, and gave the song its refrain, “Dunkel ist das Leben, ist der Tod”.

    3. “Wenn der Kummer naht”

      “Wenn der Kummer naht” — when sorrow draws near. The boast falls quiet. A solo violin and the harps accompany the lines about a garden in which joy and song wither, and Mahler marks the voice “düster, zart” — gloomy, tender.

    4. First refrain, G minor

      “Dunkel ist das Leben, ist der Tod” (dark is life, dark is death). The tenor sings the refrain in G minor, accompanied by solo horn and harp arpeggios. At each later return the same sentence is set a semitone higher.

    5. Second stanza, Tempo I subito

      The horn call and the flutter-tongued flutes return at Tempo I subito. The next stanza is addressed to the host and his well-stocked cellar. The singer has brought a lute, and he holds a full cup to be worth more than all the kingdoms of the earth.

    6. Mahler’s resignation and losses, 1907

      In 1907 Mahler resigned from the Vienna Court Opera, “because I can no longer endure the rabble”, and on 21 June signed a contract with the Metropolitan Opera. Within weeks his elder daughter Maria died of scarlet fever and diphtheria. A doctor then found a defect in Mahler’s own heart.

    7. Second refrain, A♭ minor

      The second refrain is a semitone higher, in A♭ minor, and Öhmann sings the same words. Schuricht takes the refrains much more slowly than the drinking music around them.

    8. Orchestral interlude

      The tenor rests for a long interlude. An oboe plays a solo, the flutes flutter-tongue and a muted trumpet plays above pizzicato strings. The harmony moves to A♭ major before the voice returns.

    9. Cylinders of Chinese music, 1908

      In August 1908 Paul Hammerschlag visited Mahler at Toblach and found him curious about China and Chinese music. Hammerschlag bought him phonograph cylinders of Chinese music recorded in China.

    10. “Das Firmament blaut ewig”

      “Das Firmament blaut ewig” — the heavens are blue for ever. Bethge’s line sets the earth’s returning spring beside the short human life. After the strings play sempre ff, the tenor enters p ma appassionato.

    11. “Du aber, Mensch”, bars 298–299

      At bars 298–299 the tenor sings “Du aber, Mensch, wie lang lebst denn du?”, asking how long a man lives. Not a hundred years, he continues, to enjoy this rotten earth. A long intensification follows.

    12. Return of the opening fanfares

      The opening fanfares return with glockenspiel, triangle and muted brass. The tenor sings of a shape crouching on the graves, an ape howling into the sweet scent of life.

    13. The toast, Schuricht recording

      The singer now permits the wine. The toast opens in A major before the final dark refrain. Schuricht takes an extreme ritardando at bar 381 for the emptying of the cup, and Öhmann draws out “zu Grund” — to the bottom.

    14. Final refrain in A minor

      The final refrain is in A minor. On the last “Tod” (death), Mahler writes a diminished-seventh chord above the tonic bass. The horns and flutes then return to the opening tempo, and the orchestra ends the song on a short, hard chord.

    Joins

    1. Into “Wenn der Kummer naht”

      After the held close of the first section, the music continues at Sempre l’istesso tempo, at the same pace. Strings and harps play softly around “Wenn der Kummer naht”, and a solo violin soon adds a phrase of its own. Mahler marks the tenor düster, zart.

    2. Harp arpeggios and G minor refrain

      The end of the garden stanza is marked Zurückhaltend. Ruhig. Harp arpeggios replace the repeated chords, and a solo horn accompanies the refrain in G minor. The tenor’s line is marked sehr getragen (very sustained).

    3. The horn call returns at Tempo I

      The held “Tod” meets a diminished-seventh chord over G at bar 90. Tempo I subito then brings back the horn call, with flutter-tongued flutes and glockenspiel. Open trumpets join the fanfare as it returns, and the strings play repeated notes.

    4. Second refrain a semitone higher

      The wine stanza ends on “Reiche dieser Erde”. A ritardando leads into a tempo, sehr ruhig, with harp arpeggios and plucked bass. The strings stay muted through the approach to the second refrain, which takes the first refrain’s G minor up to A♭ minor.

    5. Transition to muted trumpet

      The held notes of the refrain run into an orchestral interlude in the region of A♭. An oboe plays above the winds as they die away. At a tempo, plucked strings and harp chords start again, followed by a muted trumpet and flutter-tongued flutes.

    6. Return of the opening fanfare

      The tenor finishes the line about earth’s rotten trinkets. A rising run in the woodwind leads back to the opening fanfare, scored with glockenspiel, triangle and muted trumpets. The low strings play pizzicato notes as the tenor begins the lines about the graves.

    7. Ape’s howl to the A-major toast

      The brass play the ape’s howl with bells raised, and cymbal and bass drum sound beneath them. A ritardando follows, and the toast moves into A major. Winds and horns hold sustained chords as the tenor tells the company to drink, and harp figures accompany the phrase as it slows.

    8. Diminished-seventh chord, then the horns

      The last refrain moves to A minor. Its final “Tod” falls on a diminished-seventh chord over A. At Tempo I the horns play their opening call again, with the high woodwinds. The orchestra goes straight from the dissonance into its short final outburst.

  2. II. Der Einsame im Herbst — Etwas schleichend. ErmüdetKerstin Thorborg, contralto; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Carl Martin Öhmann sings the tenor songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    9:37

    “Der Einsame im Herbst”, marked “Etwas schleichend. Ermüdet”, is the second song of Das Lied von der Erde, sung here by Kerstin Thorborg. Muted violins play softly beneath a solo oboe, and the text tells of frost, a cold wind and a lamp gone out; the loudest passage sets a plea to the sun of love.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Solo oboe over muted violins

      Muted first violins play unbroken quavers. Above them a solo oboe, marked molto espressivo, begins a long melody. Mahler marks the movement Etwas schleichend. Ermüdet (somewhat creeping, weary), and the violin quavers continue while the oboe rests.

    2. Source poem by Qian Qi

      The song is based on a poem by Qian Qi in which a woman weaves through a long autumn night beside a failing lamp. Judith Gautier’s French adaptation omitted the loom. The frost, the fading light and the loneliness passed through Hans Bethge’s German version to Mahler.

    3. First vocal stanza, alto

      The flute echoes the oboe. The alto enters in D minor with the first stanza, in which autumn mist drifts blue over a lake and frost lies on the grass like jade dust scattered by an artist. The moving violin line continues beneath the voice.

    4. Letter to Bruno Walter, July 1908

      In July 1908, while he was composing these songs, Mahler was struggling with his doctors’ restrictions on exercise. He wrote to Bruno Walter: “I can’t work at a desk. For my inner activity I need outer activity.”

    5. Orchestral passage at figure 10

      After the lines about the golden lotus petals on the water, the singer stops. At figure 10 the violins and violas play a melody marked Zart drängend, zart leidenschaftlich, and the winds and horns crescendo with them. The cellos then play a diminuendo.

    6. Tempo I, figure 11

      The opening tempo returns at “Mein Herz ist müde” (my heart is weary). The alto sings p, without expression, over quavers in the violas and cellos. As the text tells of the little lamp going out with a crackle, the violas play am Steg, close to the bridge.

    7. Diagnosis of heart defect, 1907

      In July 1907, after Maria’s death, a doctor at Maiernigg found a valvular defect in Mahler’s heart, and a specialist in Vienna confirmed it. The defect was not fatal in itself, but such defects were known to lead to bacterial endocarditis, which killed him four years later. He set these words in July 1908.

    8. Figure 13, alto and solo violin

      At figure 13 the alto sings “Ich komm zu dir, traute Ruhestätte”, marked innig. A solo violin plays against the voice. Beneath them the strings continue in quavers.

    9. Harps in unison, E♭ major

      At figure 18, marked Mit großem Aufschwung, two harps play in unison under the voice in E♭ major as the singer asks whether the sun of love will never shine again. The voice holds “Liebe” across the bar, then moves on to the bitter tears.

    10. Alto over pizzicato cellos

      The full orchestra drops out. Over pizzicato cellos, the alto asks whether the tears will ever be dried. Mahler added the word “mild” to Bethge’s poem, and the remaining music goes to the instruments.

    11. First performance, Munich, 1911

      Bruno Walter conducted the first performance in Munich on 20 November 1911, six months after Mahler’s death, with the Americans Sara Cahier and William Miller. At the alto’s entry in this song Mahler noted that, if necessary, the part may be sung by a baritone.

    12. Closing bars, violins and wind

      Oboe, bassoon and horn finish their phrases over continuing quavers in the first violins. The violins slow and hold their last note. A few wind notes remain, marked to die away.

    Joins

    1. Clarinet trills under the cold wind

      The oboe’s phrase ends and the first violins, which have been moving, stop. Over soft winds the alto begins the second stanza, about flowers whose scent has gone. The voice is marked pp sehr gehalten. At the words ein kalter Wind the clarinets trill beneath the voice (full score, p. 42, figures 7–8).

    2. The solo cello joins the section

      A solo cello follows the alto’s words about lotus petals on the water. At Zart drängend the singer stops and the full cello section joins the violins and violas. The strings play rising phrases, the woodwinds and horns enter, and all build towards a crescendo.

    3. Cello diminuendo to pppp and Tempo I

      The wind and string crescendo reaches fortissimo. The violins drop in volume, and the cellos repeat quavers in a diminuendo to pianississimo and then to pppp. At Tempo I the opening pace returns. The violas play the moving notes beneath the alto, who states plainly that the heart is weary.

    4. Harps enter at figure 18

      The orchestra builds a crescendo after the alto’s long autumn phrases. At figure 18 (full score, pp. 47–48) two harps enter, playing arpeggios in unison. The music moves to E♭ major, marked Mit großem Aufschwung, and the alto begins a long phrase addressed to the sun of love. The winds drop to pianissimo under the start of this phrase.

    5. Tempo I over plucked cellos

      The harps keep playing as the alto presses on through the words about bitter tears, marked Drängend. Then Tempo I returns subito, and nearly every instrument stops. The singer finishes the question over plucked cellos. Bassoon and oboe begin the postlude (full score, pp. 49–50, figure 19).

    Open in the Concert Hall

  3. III. Von der Jugend — Behaglich heiterCarl Martin Öhmann, tenor; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Kerstin Thorborg sings the alto songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    3:28

    “Von der Jugend”, marked “Behaglich heiter”, is the third song of Mahler’s Das Lied von der Erde. Friends drink, talk and write verses in a green-and-white porcelain pavilion, and Mahler sets the scene with bright woodwinds before it appears upside down, reflected in the pond.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, winds, horn and triangle

      The movement opens with a horn note and the triangle. Flutes, oboe and clarinets exchange trills and short, quick figures. When the tenor describes the porcelain pavilion, violins and violas enter with a bouncing bow-stroke.

    2. G-major section, figures 5–9

      At figure 5 the music moves to G major, with clarinets and a muted trumpet. Inside the little pavilion, friends drink, talk and write verses. Their silk sleeves are pushed back, and their caps sit low on their necks.

    3. Source of the porcelain pavilion

      The porcelain pavilion came from a mistranslation. Li Bai’s poem is called “Banquet at Tao’s Family Pavilion”, and the Chinese word for the surname Tao can also mean porcelain. Judith Gautier’s French made the family’s house into porcelain, and Bethge and Mahler kept her reading.

    4. Ruhiger, G minor, figure 10

      At figure 10, marked Ruhiger, the music slows into G minor and a solo violin plays above rocking strings. The tenor describes the still surface of the pond and the reflection in it.

    5. Toblach, summer 1908

      Mahler composed the songs largely in the summer of 1908, when he was forbidden the long walks he needed. From Bethge’s 83 paraphrases of 38 poets he chose seven. In early September he wrote to Bruno Walter: “I was very diligent … I believe it is the most personal thing I have yet created.”

    6. Return of Tempo I, figure 14

      At figure 14 the first tempo returns subito in B♭ major, and the strings remove their mutes. The tenor sings of the reflected scene standing on its head, with pavilion, friends and the bridge, whose arch becomes a half-moon.

    Joins

    1. Muted trumpet at the move to G major

      Oboes and clarinets carry the quick figures out of B♭ major. At rehearsal figure 5 the key signature changes to G major. A muted trumpet joins the woodwinds, the clarinets repeat short chords and the triangle continues. A few bars later the tenor enters with the friends in the pavilion.

    2. Solo violin and the turn to G minor

      The woodwind figures subside as a solo violin plays a longer line. At Ruhiger (figure 10, p. 57 of the full score), G major changes to G minor. The strings play a rocking accompaniment beneath the violin, and the tenor begins to describe the reflection of the scene.

    3. The mutes come off

      Repeated ritardandos slow the strings to near standstill. At Tempo I subito the opening tempo returns at once, with flutes and oboes in B♭ major. The strings remove their mutes and resume their accompaniment figure as the tenor describes the world standing on its head.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  4. IV. Von der Schönheit — Comodo. DolcissimoKerstin Thorborg, contralto; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Carl Martin Öhmann sings the tenor songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    6:45

    “Von der Schönheit” (Comodo. Dolcissimo) is the fourth movement of Das Lied von der Erde. Girls pick lotus flowers by a river; horsemen ride past, and one girl watches after them. Kerstin Thorborg sang it in this 1939 performance in Amsterdam.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening in G major

      Trilling flutes and muted violins open the movement in G major. Thorborg’s first words are “Junge Mädchen pflücken Blumen” (young girls pick flowers). The melody moves between bars of two and three beats.

    2. Sources of the poem

      The poem descends from Li Bai’s “Lotus-plucking Song”, set at Ruoye Brook near Shaoxing. The Marquis d’Hervey de Saint-Denys put it into French, Bethge into German verse, and Mahler retouched Bethge’s text. Li Bai’s original is far shorter than the song.

    3. Zephyr stanza, solo violin

      The harmony moves from G major down to E major. The violins remove their mutes, and a solo violin plays above the words about the breeze lifting the girls’ sleeves. The harp plays chords beneath the long vocal line.

    4. Orchestral interlude before the horsemen

      Thorborg finishes on “Luft” — air — and the orchestra takes over, Allmählich belebend. Stopped horns and muted trumpet and trombones enter over harp glissandi. The voice is silent until the horsemen stanza (rehearsal figures 7–10).

    5. March section with mandolin

      The march adds a mandolin to the drums and brass. The tambourine rattles, bass drum and cymbal mark the beat, and both harps play chords.

    6. Horsemen stanza, voice

      The orchestra drops to p subito for the singer’s return: “O sieh, was tummeln sich für schöne Knaben” (see the handsome boys romping there). Timpani played with wooden sticks keep the rhythm of the hooves, and the vocal part becomes faster and more crowded.

    7. Mahler’s changes to Bethge’s text

      In the lines about the horse Mahler added words that are not in Bethge: “Hei! Wie flattern im Taumel seine Mähnen, dampfen heiß die Nüstern!” (how his mane flies in a frenzy, how hot the nostrils steam). He also changed the word for the girl’s pride from “Lüge” (lie) to “Verstellung” (pretence).

    8. Return of Tempo I, rehearsal 16

      The text describes the horse’s flying mane and steaming nostrils. At rehearsal 16 the opening tempo returns subito (Tempo I, Andante). Muted violins and harp play, and Thorborg repeats the words “Gold’ne Sonne” — golden sun.

    9. Final stanza, “Ganz ruhig”

      In the text, the girl’s proud bearing is a pretence; her eyes follow the rider, and her heart still trembles after he has gone. Mahler marks the passage “Ganz ruhig” (quite calm). Clarinet phrases and the violins continue beneath Thorborg’s last words.

    10. Carl Schuricht, Wiesbaden and Amsterdam

      Carl Schuricht, born in Danzig in 1880, was Wiesbaden’s music director from 1912. He conducted the Eighth Symphony there in 1913 and gave Mahler weeks in 1920 and 1921. He conducted this performance in Amsterdam on 5 October 1939, by which time Mahler’s music had vanished from German concert programmes.

    Joins

    1. Unmuted violins and E major

      A ritardando closes the image of sunlight reflected in water. At rehearsal 5 the music resumes in E major, a minor third below G, marked a tempo (ruhiger). The violins take off their mutes. Their high melody and the woodwinds’ little turns lead into the breeze stanza.

    2. From the voice’s “Luft” into the march

      After the voice’s final “Luft”, flute and violins hold high notes above the harp. At Allmählich belebend the orchestra begins to move. Stopped horns, muted trumpet and trombones enter, and the harp plays a glissando in D major. The march accelerates to a C-major harp glissando (Universal Edition score, pp. 65–68, figures 7–9).

    3. Voice returns over wooden-stick timpani

      At rehearsal 10 the march, heavy with brass, drops to piano subito and the voice returns. At Noch etwas flotter, Thorborg names the horsemen over oboes, clarinets, bassoons and strings. Timpani played with wooden sticks mark the rhythm beneath her line.

    4. Return to Tempo I with muted violins

      The racing vocal line ends on the words about the horse’s steaming nostrils. At rehearsal 16 (Universal Edition score, p. 77), Tempo I subito (Andante) brings back the muted violins, followed by the harp and a solo violin. The return begins under a signature of two flats. The G-major signature comes back in the following system, with the words about golden sunlight on the water.

    5. Ganz ruhig and the orchestral ending

      The words about the girl’s pretended pride give way to Ganz ruhig. Quiet violin figures descend while the clarinets exchange short phrases. The final vocal line ends in G major. The winds and strings continue its small gestures into the closing bars.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  5. V. Der Trunkene im Frühling — Allegro. Keck, aber nicht zu schnellCarl Martin Öhmann, tenor; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Kerstin Thorborg sings the alto songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    4:50

    “Der Trunkene im Frühling”, marked Allegro. Keck, aber nicht zu schnell, is the fifth song of Das Lied von der Erde. In it a bird announces spring and the drinker asks for another cup. Carl Martin Öhmann sings with the Concertgebouw Orchestra under Carl Schuricht in a live performance of 1939.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening and tenor entry

      Oboes and clarinets trill above the horns, which play figures with grace notes; the strings rest. In bar 5 Öhmann enters with “Wenn nur ein Traum das Leben ist” (if life is only a dream), and asks why toil and trouble are worth the effort.

    2. Li Bai’s poem and its translators

      The poem began as Li Bai’s “Feelings upon Awakening from Drunkenness on a Spring Day”. The Marquis d’Hervey de Saint-Denys translated it into French as “Un jour de printemps”, Hans Heilmann put that into German, and Bethge rhymed it. The opening thought, that life is a great dream and toil pointless, comes from Li Bai.

    3. Birdsong episode

      The drinker wakes to birdsong; “Ein Vogel singt im Baum” — a bird sings in the tree. A solo violin plays above the softer orchestra, followed by the flute and then the harp. Mahler holds back the tempo here, marking it Noch ruhiger and Zurückhaltend.

    4. Tenor’s question, Langsam

      The tenor asks whether spring has come. Under Rit. and Langsam he repeats “mir ist” before “als wie im Traum” (as though in a dream). Solo violin and harp accompany the question, with only a few low winds and strings beneath.

    5. Letter to Bruno Walter, July 1908

      In July 1908, composing again with difficulty, Mahler wrote to Bruno Walter that he had lost all the clarity and reassurance he ever won and stood “vis-à-vis de rien”, face to face with nothing. His doctors had forbidden exertion. The poem calls life a dream and spring beside the point.

    6. Passage in D♭ major

      The music moves to D♭ major beneath the drinker’s line, and Mahler marks the passage “Zögernd” and “Ganz langsam” (hesitant, very slow). Muted strings, bassoons and horns hold sustained chords.

    7. Return of the first tempo

      “Ich fülle mir den Becher neu” (I fill my cup again). The first tempo returns suddenly, with harp arpeggios and pizzicato cellos, and the violins have removed their mutes. The voice continues until the text reaches the appearance of the moon.

    8. Final words and A-major ending

      “Laßt mich betrunken sein!” (let me be drunk!) With these final words Öhmann dismisses the bird and its good news. In an A-major Allegro, woodwind runs pass over string chords, and triangle and trumpet join the last flourish.

    Joins

    1. Solo violin and winds at the waking

      The drinker’s sleep gives way to a solo violin and quiet wind figures in A major. The markings Noch ruhiger and then Zurückhaltend slow the tempo of his waking question. The harp enters with soft chords as the tenor locates the bird in its tree.

    2. Return to Tempo I with pizzicato cellos

      The D♭-major pause moves on at Etwas fließender, and the strings take off their mutes. The tenor’s “Ich” leads directly into Tempo I subito, where harp arpeggios and pizzicato cellos accompany the line about his freshly filled cup.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  6. VI. Der Abschied — SchwerKerstin Thorborg, contralto; Concertgebouw Orchestra, Amsterdam; Carl Schuricht (live; Carl Martin Öhmann sings the tenor songs of the same concert) · 5 October 1939
    28:52

    Kerstin Thorborg sings “Der Abschied”, the sixth and last movement of Das Lied von der Erde, marked “Schwer”. In the text night falls, a friend is late and a last drink is offered, and between the waiting and the parting the orchestra plays a funeral march.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening low C, oboe entry

      A low C sounds in contrabassoon and low strings, with horns, harps and a soft tam-tam stroke. A solo oboe then enters. The marking is Schwer (heavy).

    2. Source poems by Meng Haoran and Wang Wei

      Mahler joined two poems. Meng Haoran’s speaker waits for a friend among the mountains, and Wang Wei’s offers a traveller a farewell drink. Mahler knew them through French versions by d’Hervey de Saint-Denys and German poems by Hans Bethge.

    3. Date of composition

      Alma Mahler wrote that the songs were sketched on lonely walks in the summer of 1907. Alfred Roller, who visited that summer, reported: “This summer remained without artistic fruit.” Bethge’s book was announced only on 5 October 1907, and the dated manuscripts indicate that the songs were composed largely in the summer of 1908.

    4. Alto recitative, figure 3

      The alto begins in a narrating tone, without expression (In erzählendem Ton, ohne Ausdruck). The text tells of the sun going down behind the mountains and evening falling over the valleys. A cello holds one long note beneath the voice, and a flute plays a countermelody above it.

    5. The moon as a silver boat

      The orchestra drops in volume for the line “O sieh! Wie eine Silberbarke schwebt der Mond am blauen Himmelssee herauf”. Translated, it reads: look, the moon floats up like a silver boat on the blue lake of the sky.

    6. Footnotes at figure 9

      At figure 9 a flute solo accompanies the line about the brook singing through the dark. Mahler added two footnotes on p. 102 of the full score. If the flautist lacks a large enough tone, the oboe takes over, and the alto must not cover the flute solo.

    7. Mahler’s changes to Bethge’s text

      Bethge’s “hardworking people” go home longing for sleep. Mahler changed them to “tired people”, who go home to learn forgotten happiness and youth anew in sleep. At the end of the poem he added a cry of his own, “O Schönheit! O ewigen Liebens — Lebenstrunkne Welt!”

    8. Figure 22, flute over double bass

      After the evening scene, the bare music of narration returns at figure 22, marked “Sehr gleichmäßig. Nicht eilen”. A solo flute plays above a held double-bass note. At “Es wehet kühl”, the singer is in the cool shade of the spruces, waiting for a friend and the last farewell.

    9. Vienna performance, 30 December 1917

      On 30 December 1917 Willem Mengelberg conducted Das Lied with the Vienna Philharmonic. Its concertmaster Arnold Rosé, who had played it before, told the tenor Jac. Urlus: “Now we have heard Das Lied von der Erde for the first time.” The same day Alma Mahler gave Mengelberg the manuscripts of this song.

    10. Bruno Walter’s recollection of Mahler

      Bruno Walter recalled that Mahler showed him the score of this movement, pointed to one passage and asked: “Can you think of a way of conducting that? Because I can’t.” The voice and solo instruments run across the barlines, and the passage is marked Ohne Rücksicht auf das Tempo (without regard for the tempo).

    11. Orchestral interlude, funeral march

      After the singer’s cry to a world drunk with life, the orchestra plays alone. Cor anglais and strings return to the opening figures. From them a heavy funeral march develops, with pizzicato basses, harp and tam-tam.

    12. Recorded durations of the Abschied

      Conductors take this march at different speeds. Colin Davis’s 1981 Abschied lasts nearly thirty-five minutes; it is the slowest in Christian Utz’s survey of ninety-two recordings and fills 51 per cent of the whole cycle, against 43 per cent in Klemperer’s 1951 recording. Schuricht’s lasts twenty-eight minutes fifty-two.

    13. Recitative, bars 374–380

      The alto returns to plain narration. A friend dismounts and offers the farewell drink, then asks where the traveller is going and why. Low cellos and basses hold C beneath her, and the tam-tam sounds.

    14. Interruption from the audience

      Just before this passage, at about 19:30, a woman in the hall shouted. The person who uploaded the recording heard “Deutschland muss raus … Herr Schuricht”, or perhaps “Deutschland über alles”. The concert took place five weeks into the Second World War and seven months before Germany invaded the Netherlands.

    15. The traveller’s reply

      The traveller replies with a veiled voice (“seine Stimme war umflort”). Fortune has been unkind to him, and he is going into the mountains for rest. The strings play pianississimo near the fingerboard, and the voice is marked sehr weich und ausdrucksvoll.

    16. Wang Wei’s “Farewell”

      In Wang Wei’s poem the traveller says he is “at odds with the world” and goes home to rest by the South Hill. “Please go. Ask no more. Endless, the white clouds.” Bethge kept the clouds (“ewig, ewig”), and Mahler’s ending replaces them.

    17. Mahler’s added ending to Bethge

      Bethge’s traveller ends among eternal white clouds. Mahler wrote a new ending, which Stephen E. Hefling identifies as beginning “Still ist mein Herz”. In it the heart waits for its hour, the earth flowers in spring and grows green again, and the far horizons shine blue for ever.

    18. First performance, Munich, 1911

      Bruno Walter conducted the première in Munich on 20 November 1911, six months after Mahler’s death. Mahler had avoided numbering the work for fear of a Ninth. He gave it its title on the same sheet on which he gave the number 9 to the symphony he had just completed.

    19. Mengelberg’s conducting score

      A note on the title page of Willem Mengelberg’s score records that Mahler thought this his most difficult work to perform. On the last page Mengelberg wrote “soft, softer, pianississimo” several times as a reminder to himself.

    20. Repetitions of “ewig” and close

      “Ewig” — for ever — is sung again and again. Harps, mandolin and celesta play short notes against held wind and string chords. The last sung syllables fall from E to D, and the final C-major harmony keeps an A within it.

    Joins

    1. Cello C after the fermata rests

      The introduction ends in rests held by fermatas. At Fließend. Im Takt, a cello holds a long C and the alto enters. Around the voice, the flute plays a countermelody.

    2. Return of the opening bass tread

      The flute’s phrase and the held cello note fade under fermatas. At figure 4, marked Tempo I, the contrabassoon and harps play the opening bass tread again. No tam-tam is written here. Trilled clarinet chords, the oboe’s curling line and horn entries follow and lead into the moonlit scene.

    3. The flute above a held bass

      The words about the sleeping world lead into a long stillness, with a held double-bass note underneath. At Sehr gleichmäßig. Nicht eilen, the alto resumes the bare narration and the flute returns with its curling melody. In the full score this join falls on pp. 108–109, at figures 20–22.

    4. Mandolin and harps follow the held note

      The flute descends and the bass note fades under a fermata. At Fließend the mandolin and two harps enter with a new flowing accompaniment. The second violins take up the short repeated figure and the first violins enter with a longer melody. The conductor moves step by step towards beating whole bars, marked “Allmählich zu ganzen Takten übergehend” at figures 23–24 (full score, pp. 109–110).

    5. The cor anglais continues alone

      The alto’s final phrase ends over held winds and strings. At Mäßig, the cor anglais takes up the curling figure over short violin notes. Bassoon, horns and harps follow. The orchestral passage leads into Schwer and the funeral march, with pizzicato low strings.

    6. From the march to the low-C recitative

      The march thins out into held chords and short phrases in the wind. The harps play their last notes sharply. At figure 48 the cellos and basses hold a low C with quiet tam-tam strokes, and the alto begins the recitative about the farewell drink (bars 374–380).

    7. Pianississimo strings after the fermata

      Woodwinds and harps interrupt the narrator with echoes of the march. Her phrase about the friend’s veiled voice ends beneath a fermata. The strings enter pianississimo, then bow near the fingerboard (Griffbrett), and flute and horn phrases surround the friend’s soft reply.

    8. Harps enter at Langsam

      The strings slow through Ritenuto molto and then hold their notes under a fermata. At Langsam, two harps begin ppp arpeggios. Over sustained violins and pizzicato lower strings, the alto sings of the earth blossoming again. Mahler marked the passage Ohne Steigerung, without any increase in force.
