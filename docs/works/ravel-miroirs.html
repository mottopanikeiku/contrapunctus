Maurice Ravel / V. The French Line

Miroirs (Mirrors)

1904–⁠05 · Piano

Five pieces, each dedicated to a member of Les Apaches. Ravel orchestrated the boat on the ocean and the Spanish morning song; the moths, birds and bells remained piano pieces.

5 recordings · 25 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Noctuelles — Très légerOlena Havyuk-⁠Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    4:19

    “Noctuelles” (night moths), marked Très léger, is the first piece in Ravel’s Miroirs. Quick piano figures stop, change direction and break off between phrases, and a slower, darker melody interrupts them.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, Très léger

      The title means night moths. Ravel marks the opening Très léger, very light. Short chromatic figures pass between the hands, often in double notes, and rising runs take them upwards before a pause divides the phrase.

    2. Dedication and the Apaches

      The dedication names Léon-⁠Paul Fargue, a poet in Ravel’s circle of friends, the Apaches. Each of the five Miroirs belongs to someone in that circle. The dedicatees include poets, musicians and the painter Paul Sordes.

    3. Melody in B♭ minor, 5/4 and 3/4

      The flutter stops. In B♭ minor, a slow melody moves above detached bass notes, in bars of five beats alternating with bars of three. Ravel marks it sombre et expressif (dark and expressive), and the right hand holds a chord.

    4. Autobiographical sketch, 1928

      In the autobiographical sketch he dictated in 1928, Ravel said that Miroirs marked a change in his harmony large enough to disconcert the musicians most accustomed to his manner. Noctuelles opens the set.

    5. Return of the first tempo

      The first tempo returns softly. The quick figure starts low in the keyboard over a repeated bass note, rises, and breaks into fragments of the slower song.

    6. Return of the opening figure

      After another pause, the opening figure returns at pp. Ravel shortens it this time. The passage moves quickly to the slower closing chords.

    7. Presque lent and return of tempo

      Presque lent, almost slow. Chords sound over a low bass, marked lointain, distant. The first tempo then returns for a few bars of high repeated chords and a short falling phrase.

    Joins

    1. A cascade into B♭ minor

      A held chord and a quick descending cascade end the first section. The piano moves into B♭ minor, Pas trop lent, and F and B♭ recur in the bass. Over bars of five beats and of three beats, a long upper melody is marked sombre et expressif.

    2. Return of the quick figure over the bass

      The slow chords fade over a repeated low note. Ravel writes revenez au premier mouvement, and the quick figure returns at ppp above that same bass. It climbs through the keyboard, interrupted by longer chords from the song.

    3. Return of the opening figure

      Rising runs slow and stop, and a bar at ppp follows. The first figure then returns at pp, in its original position on the keyboard. The two hands resume the light, uneven phrases of the opening.

    4. Fermata, descent and Presque lent

      A fortissimo chord is held under a fermata. Quick figures descend through both hands to ppp. At Presque lent, quiet chords sound above a low sustained bass, and Ravel marks them lointain. The first tempo returns once more before the end.

  2. II. Oiseaux tristes — Très lentOlena Havyuk-⁠Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    3:34

    Oiseaux tristes (Très lent) is the second piece of Ravel’s Miroirs. It opens on a high B♭ alone, and the bird calls sound above slow piano figures, with a brief flurry in the middle.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, unaccompanied B♭

      A high B♭ repeats very softly, then turns into a short flourish. The left hand rests for three bars. When it enters, its quavers move in threes against the upper part’s twos.

    2. First performance, Paris, 1906

      Oiseaux tristes is dedicated to Ricardo Viñes. On 6 January 1906, at the Salle Érard in Paris, he gave the first performance of all five Miroirs.

    3. Enharmonic change to E major

      E♭ is rewritten as D♯, the same piano key, and the music moves to E major. A rolled chord opens a fuller passage. High calls sound forte and are then repeated softly, marked lointain (far away).

    4. Ravel on Oiseaux tristes

      In dictated notes, Ravel called Oiseaux tristes the first of the five pieces in date and, in his view, the most typical. He described it as “birds lost in the torpor of a very dark forest in the hottest hours of summer”.

    5. Unbarred cadenza

      Ravel drops the bar lines for a cadenza. Slurred runs climb and turn over a slower line in the bass. The marking is Lent, presque ad lib., and even the fastest notes stay at ppp.

    6. Closing section in E♭ minor

      The music returns to E♭ minor over a low held E♭. Ravel marks the passage Encore plus lent, sombre et lointain (dark and distant). Short calls sound above the bass and fade under perdendo.

    Joins

    1. E♭ respelled as D♯ for E major

      A short 2/4 bar closes the opening section. E♭ is respelled as D♯, the leading note of E major, and four sharps replace six flats. A rolled right-hand chord, marked expressif, sounds over a low held bass. Rising left-hand notes follow.

    2. The call returns above held chords

      The fast chords subside and Ravel writes revenez au mouvement. A one-flat key signature brings D minor. The opening’s short–long call returns above tied chords, with the pedal held.

    3. Into the unbarred cadenza

      A short 2/4 bar leads into Lent, presque ad lib. The piano stays at ppp with the pedal held down. From here the runs are written without bar lines. Below the repeating right-hand figures, the left hand plays a slower melody.

    4. Return to the opening tempo

      The cadenza pushes slightly ahead, then au mouvement brings back the original tempo and the bar lines. A rising left-hand figure leads to a held chord beneath a short phrase in the upper part. Over three bars the dynamic grows from p to mf and falls back, and the passage ends under a fermata.

    5. A low E♭ beneath the birds

      After the fermata the six-flat signature returns. A low E♭ holds the piano in E♭ minor while the calls in the upper register are played slowly and softly. Ravel marks Encore plus lent, then perdendo over the last phrases.

  3. III. Une barque sur l’océan — D’un rythme soupleOlena Havyuk-⁠Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    6:43

    Une barque sur l’océan, marked D’un rythme souple, is the third piece of Ravel’s Miroirs. A slow melody sits above arpeggios that grow from a ripple into large waves covering the whole keyboard.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, 6/8 and 2/4

      The marking is D’un rythme souple — très enveloppé de pédales (a supple rhythm, wrapped in pedal). The left hand plays rising and falling figures beneath slow, tied notes in the right. Ravel writes two metres together, 6/8 and 2/4.

    2. Dedication to Paul Sordes

      Ravel dedicated Une barque sur l’océan to Paul Sordes, a painter in his circle of friends, Les Apaches. Each of the five pieces in Miroirs carries the name of one of the Apaches.

    3. First crescendo to fortissimo

      Low bass notes begin a long crescendo. The right hand plays chromatic runs while the left strikes deep notes and rolled chords. Both hands then cover the full width of the keyboard, with quieter bars between the loud passages.

    4. Phrase marked très expressif

      After the runs, a glissando leads into a phrase marked très expressif. The bass arpeggios move more slowly. The piano plays a long melodic line, and small surges still break through it.

    5. Shakespeare and the title Miroirs

      Ravel explained the title Miroirs with a line from Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar: “The eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things.” In 1928 he accepted the label “impressionist” only by analogy.

    6. Section in three flats

      Three flats replace the opening’s three sharps. High tremolos marked pianissimo alternate with runs through both hands, marked fortissimo.

    7. Return to three sharps

      The three-sharp signature returns. High in the right hand a quick figure circles, pianissimo and sans nuances, “without shading”. Below it the left hand plays an expressive melody that rises and falls on its own.

    8. Crescendo to full-keyboard sweep

      The high figure repeats while the notes beneath it thicken into chords. Ravel marks augmentez peu à peu. The hands spread apart again, and the figure runs into another sweep across the full keyboard.

    9. Cruise on the yacht Aimée, 1905

      From 2 June to the end of July 1905 Ravel cruised the canals of France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Germany on the yacht Aimée, owned by Alfred and Misia Edwards. The painter Pierre Bonnard was among the guests. Miroirs was first played in January 1906.

    10. Tremolo in the bass register

      The writing moves into the lower register, with both staves in the bass clef. A right-hand tremolo sounds above rolling bass notes, and Ravel marks it très fondu — thoroughly blended.

    11. Return of the opening figure

      The opening ripple returns beneath tied notes in the right hand, now for only a few phrases. The arpeggios rise and fall, and the piece ends on a rolled chord marked pppp.

    Joins

    1. Right hand rests, left hand continues

      The metre changes to 3/4, then to 2/4. The right-hand phrases grow shorter and end in rests. The left hand continues alone, and its arpeggios descend further into the bass. The three-sharp key signature remains, and naturals alter the harmony.

    2. One-beat bar before the 3/4 runs

      A one-beat bar marked sforzando breaks off the low arpeggios. The metre changes to 3/4, and the right hand starts chromatic runs, pp en dehors. The left hand plays rolled chords and deep bass notes. A long crescendo follows, and the writing spreads across both hands.

    3. Glissando into the 2/4 section

      The runs shared between the hands diminuendo. A right-hand glissando crosses above low octaves in the bass, and the metre changes to 2/4. The melody, marked très expressif, is in long notes over slower arpeggios.

    4. Key change from three sharps to three flats

      At a double bar the key signature of three sharps changes to three flats. In 3/4, high pianissimo tremolos sound above a rising left-hand arpeggio. The next sweep moves into common time at fortissimo, with both hands running down the keyboard and back up.

    5. High repeating figure in the right hand

      A short très expressif phrase follows the sweep. The key signature of three sharps returns, and the right hand climbs towards the top of the keyboard. Its quick notes form a repeating figure, marked pp sans nuances, above an expressive line in the left hand.

    6. Right-hand tremolo moves into the bass

      The wide descending sweeps contract into repeated low figures. The right hand’s tremolo, now written in the bass clef, merges with the left hand’s rolling accompaniment. Ravel marks the passage le trémolo très fondu, and the dynamic level falls gradually.

    7. Low chords lead back to the opening

      Low repeated chords diminish beneath short upper phrases. A rising run takes the hands back to the opening’s F♯ minor arpeggios, now pianissimo. The right hand plays its tied notes above them again. A brief reprise follows, and then the final pppp chord.

  4. IV. Alborada del gracioso — Assez vifOlena Havyuk-⁠Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    5:57

    Alborada del gracioso, marked Assez vif, is the fourth piece of Ravel’s Miroirs: a dawn song for a Spanish jester, with guitar-like strumming on the piano between the verses. An unaccompanied melodic line interrupts the dance.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, guitar-style chords

      The title means a dawn song of a jester. The piano opens with tightly rolled chords, marked sec, imitating a strummed guitar, with sharp bass notes beneath. The dance is in 6/8, and Ravel repeatedly interrupts its rhythm.

    2. Repeated-note passage

      The right hand plays a rapidly repeated note, with short scales running off from it. The left hand keeps to a spare accompaniment below.

    3. Recitative, Plus lent

      The dance stops under a fermata. A single melodic line follows, Plus lent and expressif en récit, with the left hand silent. Strummed chords interrupt it repeatedly at the original tempo.

    4. Ravel on the gracioso

      Ravel explained that his clown, the gracioso, was humorous but had an edge to him, and was less well meaning than Beaumarchais’s Figaro. Roger Nichols noted that the drier tone and lighter action of the Érard pianos Ravel favoured would sharpen the “guitar” chords.

    5. Dedication to Calvocoressi

      Ravel dedicated this movement to Calvocoressi, a critic and musicologist in his circle of friends. In Miroirs, Calvocoressi received the Spanish jester and Ricardo Viñes the sad birds.

    6. Repeated A in the bass

      The song descends from its high chords and the bass holds A. Ravel writes sans ralentir, without slowing. The repeated note restores the pulse of the dance before the dry guitar chords return.

    7. Return of the strummed chords

      The strummed chords return at ppp over E♭ in the bass, marked très sec et bien rythmé. Repeated notes and glissandos follow.

    8. Final section and close in D major

      Another dry chord stops under a fermata. Quiet staccato chords follow over low repeated notes, and the song does not return. The piano moves to a final rush to D major, played without slowing at the end.

    9. Ballets russes, London, 1919

      The Ballets russes danced Alborada, in Ravel’s orchestration, at London’s Alhambra Theatre in July 1919 as part of Les jardins d’Aranjuez, with Chabrier’s Menuet pompeux and Fauré’s Pavane. Paris had heard the orchestral version first, on 17 May 1919, under Rhené-⁠Baton with the Pasdeloup Orchestra.

    Joins

    1. Fermata, then an unaccompanied line

      The dance reaches a fortissimo chord and stops under a fermata. Ravel changes to 3/4 and Plus lent, and a single unaccompanied line enters, marked expressif en récit. Between its phrases the strummed chords return at 1er Mouvement.

    2. The song descends to A

      The high chords marked ff très expressif descend into a softer phrase. The piano holds a repeated A, marked sans ralentir. The dance pulse resumes as the last notes of the song end.

    3. Bass moves from A to E♭

      The repeated A gets softer, and the bass then moves to E♭ beneath the returning guitar chords. Ravel marks the chords ppp and très sec et bien rythmé. The harmony moves on towards F♯ as the chords grow louder.

    4. Quiet chords after the fermata

      The repeated-note passage ends on a fortissimo chord marked sec., held under a fermata. Quiet staccato chords follow, with low repeated notes in the left hand, and lead into the coda, which closes in D major.

  5. V. La vallée des cloches — Très lentOlena Havyuk-⁠Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    4:43

    “La vallée des cloches” (Très lent) is the fifth and last piece of Ravel’s Miroirs, a setting of bells for piano with high chimes, repeated notes in the middle register and a deep toll that sounds after the others stop. Ravel wrote it on three staves to keep their separate rhythms clear.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening bars, three bell layers

      High G♯ octaves sound in alternation. Soft sextuplets begin beneath them, and then repeated fourths enter. Each layer moves at its own speed and covers its own range of the keyboard, and the music is written on three staves that keep the layers apart.

    2. Letter to Maurice Delage, June 1905

      In June 1905 Ravel wrote to his pupil Maurice Delage from the Netherlands: “Yesterday, an excursion to Alkmaar: a cheese market with carillons ringing ceaselessly.” He also described windmills turning as far as he could see. The piece is dedicated to Delage.

    3. Fermata and très calme, bars 11–⁠13

      A fermata holds the first bells. The piano continues très calme, with slow chords above low octaves. The tolling fourths from the opening carry across the join and continue while the other bells change.

    4. Central melody, Largement chanté

      Largement chanté — broadly sung. A long melody begins among the repeated bell figures. Its phrases move from note to note over the sustained low bass, and it is the broadest melodic writing in the piece.

    5. Noon bells in Paris

      Ravel told the pianist Robert Casadesus that the piece came from the bells ringing at noon in Paris. On the piano, the chimes overlap high above the slow bass.

    6. Piano roll, 1922

      The piano roll of this piece, recorded in 1922, is sold as Ravel’s own playing. Ravel admitted that he hired a better pianist for some of the rolls, but Ronald Woodley judged that for this one the probability favours the composer. Roy Howat heard “moments of mild anarchy between the hands”.

    7. End of the melody, final system

      The melody line ends, and a chord is held under a fermata, pp. The key signature of four sharps returns with the earlier très calme chords, and the low octaves continue.

    8. Return of the opening carillon

      The opening carillon returns above a deep rolled chord. Ravel identified this great bell to the pianist Henriette Faure as La Savoyarde. It sounds four times, each softer than the last, and the final bar is a rest under a fermata.

    Joins

    1. Fermata into the très calme chords

      The sextuplets stop, and a fermata sustains the piano’s remaining resonance. The next chords, marked pp, très calme, sit over low octaves. The key signature of four sharps stays in place. The tolling fourths continue across the join into the new passage, while the metre changes to 6/4 and then to 3/4.

    2. Five flats and largement chanté

      The accented chords die away above held bass notes. Ravel changes the key signature to five flats and marks the new line largement chanté. It starts in the middle register of the keyboard, mezzo-forte, and the low notes continue beneath it. In the Demets first edition (1906) this change comes on the final system of PDF p. 45, at the start of the central section.

    3. A fermata before four sharps

      The melody descends over long bass notes, and the piano reaches pianissimo. A fermata separates this passage from the return of the très calme chords. The key signature changes from five flats to four sharps, and low octaves bring back the earlier accompaniment.

    4. High chimes return over a low chord

      The slow chords thin to held notes and rests. The piano resumes its high sextuplets at pianissimo, with the G♯ octave bells beside them. At bar 50 a low rolled chord sounds beneath the carillon. It is the deep bell first heard in the middle section, at bar 28.
