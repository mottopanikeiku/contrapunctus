Maurice Ravel / V. The French Line

Miroirs (Mirrors)

1904–05 · Piano

Five pieces, each dedicated to a member of Les Apaches. Ravel orchestrated the boat on the ocean and the Spanish morning song, leaving the moths, birds and bells at the piano.

5 recordings · 25 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Noctuelles — Très légerOlena Havyuk-Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    4:19

    Night moths dart through the first of Ravel’s Miroirs: quick piano figures stop, turn and vanish between phrases. A dark, slower song interrupts their flight.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Moths at the keyboard

      The title means night moths. Ravel writes Très léger, very light: little chromatic figures dart between the hands, often with two notes pressed together. Rising runs carry them upwards, then a quiet gap breaks the flight.

    2. A poet among the moths

      The dedication names Léon-Paul Fargue, a poet in Ravel’s circle of friends, the Apaches. Each of the five Miroirs belongs to someone in that circle: poets and musicians share the set with the painter Paul Sordes.

    3. A song in five beats

      The flutter stops. In B♭ minor, a slow melody moves above detached bass notes, with five-beat bars alternating with three. Ravel asks for sombre et expressif: dark and expressive. The right hand suddenly has time to hold a chord.

    4. Ravel on his new harmony

      In the autobiographical sketch he dictated in 1928, Ravel says that Miroirs marked a change in his harmony large enough to disconcert the musicians most accustomed to his manner. Noctuelles, with its chromatic moths, opens the set.

    5. A flutter in the bass

      The first tempo returns softly. The quick figure starts low in the keyboard over a repeated bass note, climbs, and breaks into fragments of the slower song. The moths are back among the chords.

    6. The first flight returns

      The opening figure returns at pp, after another quiet gap. Its crooked little shapes are familiar, but Ravel shortens their route. The passage moves quickly towards the slower closing chords.

    7. Distant chords, one last flutter

      Presque lent: almost slow. Quiet chords sound over a low bass, marked lointain, distant. Then the first tempo returns for a few bars of high repeated chords and a tiny falling phrase.

    How it is joined

    1. A cascade into B♭ minor

      A held chord and a quick descending cascade end the moths’ first flight. The piano settles into B♭ minor, Pas trop lent, with F and B♭ recurring in the bass. Five-beat and three-beat bars carry a long upper melody marked sombre et expressif.

    2. Quick notes over the lingering bass

      The slow chords fade over a repeated low note. Ravel writes revenez au premier mouvement, and the quick figure returns at ppp above that same bass. It climbs through the keyboard while longer chords from the song interrupt it.

    3. A quiet gap before the moths

      Rising runs lose their momentum and leave a quiet bar at ppp. The first figure then reappears at pp, in its original position on the keyboard. The two hands recover the opening’s light, uneven phrases.

    4. The flight ends in distant chords

      A fortissimo chord pauses under a fermata. Quick figures run down through both hands to ppp; then Presque lent brings quiet chords above a low sustained bass. Ravel marks them lointain. The first tempo returns once more before the end.

  2. II. Oiseaux tristes — Très lentOlena Havyuk-Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    3:34

    A high B♭ calls into silence at the start of Oiseaux tristes. Ravel’s birds sing above slow, uneasy piano figures, with a brief flurry of flight in the middle.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One bird, no accompaniment

      A high B♭ repeats, very softly, then opens into a small flourish. The left hand waits for three bars. When it begins, its quavers move in threes beneath the upper part’s twos, keeping the bird’s call slightly apart from its surroundings.

    2. Viñes at the Salle Érard

      The dedication belongs to Ricardo Viñes. On 6 January 1906, at the Salle Érard in Paris, he gives the first performance of all five Miroirs. The pianist who receives these solitary birds also has to launch the ocean and the Spanish dance.

    3. E♭ becomes D♯

      E♭ is rewritten as D♯, the same piano key with a new destination: E major. A rolled chord opens a fuller passage. High calls sound forte, then answer themselves softly, marked lointain — far away.

    4. Ravel on his birds

      Ravel dictates that Oiseaux tristes, the first of the five pieces in date and in his view the most typical, strikes him as “birds lost in the torpor of a very dark forest in the hottest hours of summer”.

    5. Flight without bar lines

      Ravel removes the bar lines for a quiet cadenza. Slurred runs climb and turn over a slower line below. Lent, presque ad lib. gives the pianist room to linger; even the fastest notes stay at ppp.

    6. The forest grows still

      The music returns to E♭ minor over a low held E♭. Ravel asks for an even slower pace, sombre et lointain, dark and distant. Short calls linger above the bass, then dwindle under perdendo.

    How it is joined

    1. One pitch turns towards E major

      A short 2/4 bar brings the opening to a close. E♭ becomes D♯, now the leading note of E major, and four sharps replace six flats. A rolled right-hand chord, marked expressif, sounds over a low held bass; rising left-hand notes follow.

    2. The call returns above held chords

      The fast chords subside and Ravel writes revenez au mouvement. A one-flat key signature brings D minor. The opening’s short–long call returns quietly above tied chords, with the pedal held.

    3. The bar lines disappear

      A short 2/4 bar leads into Lent, presque ad lib.. The piano stays at ppp with the pedal down, but its runs now cross the page without bar lines. A slower left-hand melody moves below the circling right hand.

    4. Three bars recover the pulse

      The cadenza presses forward slightly, then au mouvement restores the original pace and the bar lines. A rising left-hand figure reaches a held chord beneath a short upper phrase. Three bars swell from p to mf and back, ending under a fermata.

    5. A low E♭ beneath the birds

      After the fermata, the six-flat signature returns. A low E♭ anchors the piano in E♭ minor while the upper calls sound slowly and softly. Ravel marks Encore plus lent, then perdendo as the last phrases fade.

  3. III. Une barque sur l’océan — D’un rythme soupleOlena Havyuk-Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    6:43

    A small boat, and an ocean that takes up the whole keyboard. In the third of Ravel’s Miroirs, a slow melody rides above arpeggios that grow from a ripple into great sweeping waves.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Wrapped in pedal

      D’un rythme souple — très enveloppé de pédales: a supple rhythm, wrapped in pedal. The left hand keeps rising and falling beneath slow, tied notes in the right. Ravel writes two metres together, 6/8 and 2/4, so the boat rocks without a square, heavy beat.

    2. For the painter Paul Sordes

      Ravel dedicates the boat to Paul Sordes, a painter in his circle of friends, Les Apaches. Each of the five pieces in Miroirs carries an Apache’s name. Sordes gets the widest stretch of water.

    3. The first great wave

      Low bass notes start a long crescendo. The right hand runs through chromatic curves while the left strikes deep notes and rolled chords. Soon both hands sweep across the keyboard, their surges separated by quieter bars.

    4. A line begins to sing

      After the sweeping runs, a glissando leads into a phrase marked très expressif. The bass arpeggios move more slowly. The piano has room for a long singing line, with little surges still breaking through it.

    5. A line from Julius Caesar

      Ravel explains the title Miroirs with a line from Shakespeare’s Julius Caesar: “The eye sees not itself / But by reflection, by some other things.” In 1928 he accepts the label “impressionist” only by analogy.

    6. Three flats, another wave

      Three flats replace the opening’s three sharps. High, quiet tremolos alternate with runs that plunge through both hands, marked fortissimo.

    7. The left hand sings

      Three sharps return. High in the right hand, a quick figure begins to circle, pianissimo and sans nuances — without shading. Below it the left hand has an expressive melody, with its own rises and falls.

    8. The circling figure grows

      The high repeated figure keeps turning while the notes beneath it thicken into chords. Ravel asks for a gradual increase. The hands spread out again, and the circling notes spill into another full-keyboard sweep.

    9. Aboard the yacht Aimée

      From 2 June to the end of July 1905 Ravel cruises the canals of France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Germany on the yacht Aimée, owned by Alfred and Misia Edwards. The painter Pierre Bonnard is among the guests. Miroirs is first played in January 1906.

    10. A tremolo that melts

      The writing drops into the lower keys. A right-hand tremolo sits above rolling bass notes, and Ravel asks for it to be très fondu — thoroughly blended. The separate attacks dissolve into a low shimmer.

    11. The boat comes back

      The opening ripple returns beneath the right hand’s tied notes. This time only a few phrases remain. Ravel lets the arpeggios climb and fall away, ending with a rolled chord marked pppp.

    How it is joined

    1. The right hand leaves space

      Ravel moves into 3/4, then 2/4, and lets the right-hand phrases dwindle to rests. The left hand continues alone, carrying its arpeggios deeper into the bass. The three-sharp signature stays in place while naturals loosen the harmony.

    2. One short bar launches the wave

      A one-beat bar marked sforzando interrupts the low arpeggios. In 3/4, the right hand starts chromatic runs, pp en dehors, while the left supplies rolled chords and deep bass notes. A long crescendo spreads the writing across both hands.

    3. A glissando clears the way

      The two-handed runs diminish. A right-hand glissando crosses above low bass octaves, then the piano settles into 2/4. The melody, marked très expressif, rests on slower arpeggios, with space between its long notes.

    4. Three flats change the water

      A double bar replaces three sharps with three flats. In 3/4, high pianissimo tremolos hover above a rising left-hand arpeggio. The next sweep breaks into common time at fortissimo, driving both hands down and back up the keyboard.

    5. The high figure starts circling

      A short très expressif phrase follows the sweep. Three sharps return, and the right hand climbs towards the top of the keyboard. Its quick notes settle into a repeating figure, pp sans nuances, above an expressive left-hand line.

    6. The hands sink into the bass

      The wide sweeps contract into repeated low figures. The right hand’s tremolo, now written in the bass clef, blends with the left hand’s rolling accompaniment. Ravel writes le trémolo très fondu and gradually reduces the piano’s force.

    7. Low chords release the first ripple

      Low repeated chords diminish beneath short upper phrases. A rising run carries the hands back to the opening’s F♯-minor arpeggios, now pianissimo. The right hand restores its tied notes above them; only a brief reprise separates this return from the final pppp chord.

  4. IV. Alborada del gracioso — Assez vifOlena Havyuk-Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    5:57

    A Spanish jester sings at dawn, strumming between the verses. Ravel turns the piano into a guitar in Alborada del gracioso, then lets a lonely, unaccompanied voice interrupt the dance.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A guitar with hammers

      The piano strums: tightly rolled chords, marked sec, dry, with sharp bass notes underneath. Ravel sets the dance in 6/8 but keeps interrupting its step. The title promises a dawn song from a Spanish jester.

    2. One key, many fingers

      The right hand hammers a repeated note at extraordinary speed, with little scales darting away from it. The left hand keeps a spare accompaniment underneath. A guitar’s repeated pluck becomes a test of the piano’s quick-returning keys.

    3. The jester’s solitary song

      The dance stops under a fermata. A single melody follows, Plus lent and expressif en récit, like someone speaking in song. The left hand is silent. Strummed chords keep interrupting at the old tempo.

    4. Ravel on his clown

      Ravel explains that his clown, the gracioso, is humorous but has an edge to him: less well meaning than Beaumarchais’s Figaro. Roger Nichols adds that the drier tone and lighter action of the Érard pianos Ravel favoured would sharpen the “guitar” chords.

    5. A jester for a critic

      Ravel dedicates this movement to Michel-Dimitri Calvocoressi, the critic and musicologist in his circle of friends. In Miroirs, Calvocoressi receives the Spanish jester; Ricardo Viñes receives the sad birds.

    6. A underneath the song

      The song comes down from its high chords and the bass settles on A. Ravel writes sans ralentir, without slowing. The repeated note brings back the dance’s pulse before the dry guitar chords return.

    7. The guitar returns on tiptoe

      The strummed chords return at ppp, over E♭ in the bass. Ravel wants them very dry and firmly in rhythm. Their quiet start leaves room for the repeated notes and glissandos that follow.

    8. The jester keeps dancing

      Another dry chord stops under a fermata. This time quiet staccato chords follow over low repeated notes. The song stays away: the piano gathers itself for a final rush to D major, with no slowing at the end.

    9. A ballet at the Alhambra

      The Ballets russes dance Alborada, in Ravel’s orchestration, at London’s Alhambra Theatre in July 1919 as part of Les jardins d’Aranjuez, with Chabrier’s Menuet pompeux and Fauré’s Pavane. Paris hears the orchestral version first, on 17 May 1919, under Rhené-Baton with the Pasdeloup Orchestra.

    How it is joined

    1. A fermata leaves one voice

      The dance reaches a fortissimo chord and stops under a fermata. Ravel changes to 3/4 and Plus lent: one unaccompanied line sings expressif en récit. The strummed chords return between its phrases at 1er Mouvement.

    2. The song descends to A

      The high chords marked ff très expressif descend into a quieter phrase. The piano settles on a repeated A, with sans ralentir above it. The dance pulse resumes as the last notes of the song die away.

    3. A becomes E♭ beneath the strums

      The repeated A quietens, then the bass moves to E♭ beneath the returning guitar chords. Ravel marks them ppp and très sec et bien rythmé. The harmony moves on towards F♯ as the chords grow louder.

    4. Quiet chords after the fermata

      The repeated-note passage ends on a fortissimo chord marked sec., held under a fermata. Quiet staccato chords follow, with low repeated notes in the left hand. Ravel keeps the dance moving into the coda, which eventually closes in D major.

  5. V. La vallée des cloches — Très lentOlena Havyuk-Sheremet, piano · 1 April 2014
    4:43

    Miroirs ends with bells: high chimes, repeated middle notes and a deep toll that outlasts them all. Ravel needs three staves to keep their separate rhythms clear on one piano.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Bells at different heights

      High G♯ octaves ring and answer one another. A soft stream of sextuplets begins beneath them, then tolling fourths join in. Each bell has its own speed and reach across the keyboard; the three staves keep them apart on the page.

    2. A cheese market with carillons

      In June 1905 Ravel writes to his pupil Maurice Delage from the Netherlands: “Yesterday, an excursion to Alkmaar: a cheese market with carillons ringing ceaselessly.” Windmills turn as far as he can see. Delage’s name becomes the dedication of this piece.

    3. One bell crosses the pause

      A fermata leaves the first bells hanging in the air. The piano continues très calme, with slow chords above low octaves. The tolling fourths carry across from the opening, so one bell remains while the others change.

    4. The bells find a melody

      Largement chanté — broadly sung. A long melody begins among the repeated bell figures. Its phrases move from note to note while the low bass continues to sound beneath them; this is the broadest singing in the piece.

    5. Paris at noon

      Ravel tells the pianist Robert Casadesus that the piece comes from the bells ringing at noon in Paris. A city’s churches can strike together without agreeing on a rhythm. On the piano, the chimes overlap high above the slow bass.

    6. A roll said to be Ravel

      The Duo-Art piano roll of this piece, recorded in 1922, is sold as Ravel playing. Ravel admits he hired a better pianist for some of the rolls; for this one, Ronald Woodley judges the probability favours the composer. Roy Howat hears “moments of mild anarchy between the hands”.

    7. The melody falls silent

      The singing line dwindles, and a chord waits under a fermata. Four sharps return with the earlier très calme chords. The low octaves remain, but the long melody has finished.

    8. The great bell of Sacré-Cœur

      The opening carillon returns above a deep rolled chord. Ravel identifies this great bell to the pianist Henriette Faure as La Savoyarde, at Sacré-Cœur. It tolls four times, each softer than the last. The final bar is silence, held under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The fourths survive the pause

      The sextuplets stop and a fermata holds the remaining piano sound. Low octaves underpin the next chords, marked pp, très calme. The four-sharp signature remains, and the tolling fourths link the two passages as the bar lengths expand to 6/4 and contract to 3/4.

    2. A tune rises from the chords

      The accented chords subside above held bass notes. Ravel introduces five flats and marks the new line largement chanté. Its first notes begin in the middle of the keyboard, mezzo-forte, while the low piano notes continue beneath it.

    3. A fermata before four sharps

      The melody falls away over long bass notes, and the piano reaches pianissimo. A fermata separates it from the returning très calme chords. Four sharps replace five flats; low octaves restore the earlier accompaniment.

    4. Small chimes over a deep toll

      The slow chords thin to held notes and rests. The piano restarts its high sextuplets at pianissimo, with the G♯ octave bells beside them. A low rolled chord then rings beneath the carillon: the deep bell first heard in the middle section.
