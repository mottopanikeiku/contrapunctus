Johann Sebastian Bach / I. The Baroque

Partita No. 2, BWV 1004

1720, manuscript · Unaccompanied violin

13 min

The recording

  1. V. CiacconaJascha Heifetz, violin · 1952
    13:02

    Jascha Heifetz plays the Chaconne, the vast final movement of Bach’s D-minor Partita. One violin moves from broad chords to racing runs, opens into D major, and finally recovers the dark chord with which it began.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One bow, several voices

      The violin begins with a D-minor chord and a dotted rhythm, entering on the second beat of the bar. Bass and melody speak together. Over the next four bars Bach sets out the harmonies that will feed an extraordinary succession of variations.

    2. Six works, dated 1720

      Bach gathers three sonatas and three partitas for solo violin in a manuscript dated 1720. The D-minor Partita ends here, after an Allemande, Courante, Sarabande and Gigue. Four dances prepare the way for this immense fifth movement.

    3. Home to a grave

      Back from Carlsbad with Prince Leopold in 1720, Bach learns that his wife, Maria Barbara, has died after a short illness and already been buried. Since 1994 the scholar Helga Thoene has argued that the Chaconne is her epitaph. The idea is disputed, and Bach left no word on it.

    4. Brahms at the piano

      In June 1877 Brahms writes to Clara Schumann: “On a single stave, for a small instrument, the man writes a whole world of the deepest thoughts and most powerful feelings.” He sends her his version of the Chaconne for piano, left hand alone.

    5. The bow crosses the strings

      Bach writes arpeggio above the chords. The bow breaks them into a running pattern across the strings, with a bass note below and a tune at the top. The middle notes keep moving between them.

    6. A Chaconne in the Metro

      At 7:51 on the morning of 12 January 2007 the violinist Joshua Bell opens with this Chaconne at L’Enfant Plaza Metro station in Washington, in a baseball cap. In 43 minutes 1,097 people pass; seven stop to listen, and 27 leave about $32 between them.

    7. D major, at last

      After the long D-minor span, F♯ enters and the violin opens into D major. The dotted rhythm remains, with the melody low on the instrument. The change is as simple as a raised third and as startling as a new voice.

    8. Heifetz on a 45

      This Chaconne comes from a Jascha Heifetz record on an RCA 45-rpm disc, ERA 9544, dated 1952. The two sides of the disc are joined into one track here, so the music runs on without a pause for turning it over.

    9. Chords after the running notes

      Broad dotted phrases return in the D-major section. The violin sounds several notes together again after pages of quick single notes. The upper line descends through a chain of leaning notes, each finding its place in the next chord.

    10. Bach the violinist

      In 1703 the eighteen-year-old Bach spends about half a year as a violinist in Duke Johann Ernst III’s private orchestra at Weimar. Seventeen years later he dates a manuscript of six works for solo violin, and ends the D-minor Partita with this Chaconne.

    11. The minor third returns

      F♮ returns and D major darkens to D minor. The violin begins a fresh group of variations, soon breaking its chords into quick lines again. The major section is over; the opening theme still has one return ahead of it.

    12. The first chords come back

      A last rush of notes lands on D. Then the opening dotted rhythm returns, with its broad chords and upper A. Eight bars bring the violin to the final D, held under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. D minor spread across the strings

      The quick descending line reaches a D-minor chord. Bach marks the following chords arpeggio, and the bow distributes their notes across the strings. Bass and upper melody remain at the edges of the pattern while the inner notes circulate between them.

    2. F♯ changes the chord

      The minor-key variations come to rest on D. The next phrase keeps D as its centre but introduces F♯, the major third. The violin begins a lower, dotted melody in D major, supported by the notes sounded beneath it on the other strings.

    3. F♮ brings back D minor

      The major section closes on D. F♮ returns in the next chord, and the violin begins another set of variations in D minor. The bass remains anchored to D at the join while the upper line starts a new dotted phrase.

    4. The first dotted rhythm returns

      The violin’s last rapid run lands on D and pauses. The opening dotted rhythm returns on the second beat, restoring the D-minor chords and upper A. A final expansion of that phrase leads to the held D under the closing fermata.
