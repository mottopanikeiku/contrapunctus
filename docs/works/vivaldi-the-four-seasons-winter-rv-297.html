Antonio Vivaldi / I. The Baroque

The Four Seasons · Winter, RV 297

1725 · Op. 8 Nos. 1–4; RV 269, 315, 293, 297 · Solo violin, strings and continuo

Four concertos with printed sonnets and letters linking words to musical events. Their keys are E major, G minor, F major and F minor.

3 recordings · 9 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro non moltoJohn Harrison, violin; Wichita State University Chamber Players; Robert Turizziani, conductor · 6 February 2000
    3:29

    Cold gets into the strings: first a shiver, then stamping feet and chattering teeth. Between them the solo violin sends gusts of demisemiquavers across the orchestra.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Cold from the ground up

      “Aggiacciato tremar trà nevi algenti” — “To shiver, frozen, among icy snows.” The 1725 sonnet starts with a body in the cold. Repeated string notes build upwards from the bass; the solo violin joins with a trill on each short note.

    2. The bitter wind

      “Al Severo Spirar d’orrido Vento” — “In the harsh breath of a dreadful wind.” The solo violin breaks into demisemiquavers, leaping through broken chords and racing down scales. Short orchestral interruptions bring back the shivering.

    3. Feet against the cold

      “Correr battendo i piedi ogni momento” — “To run, stamping one’s feet at every moment.” Vivaldi squeezes the repeated notes closer together. Semiquavers sprout little bursts of demisemiquavers: the shivering body is trying to warm itself.

    4. Teeth in the bow

      “E pel Soverchio gel batter i denti” — “And to chatter one’s teeth from the excessive frost.” The solo violin repeats short notes under the words Batter li denti. The teeth keep chattering while the harmony passes through A♭ major and C minor towards F minor.

    5. One last stamp

      The full strings take over in F minor. Vivaldi brings back the stamping part of the refrain, with its rapid repeated notes, and drives it to the final cadence. The movement ends on its feet.

    How it is joined

    1. A C-minor gust

      The shivering passage moves from F minor to C minor. The solo violin begins the wind on C, leaping an octave before racing through the notes of a C-minor chord. The orchestra interrupts with short returns of the shivering rhythm; the Allegro non molto marking remains.

    2. The shiver becomes a stamp

      The wind returns to F minor and the full strings resume the repeated-note refrain. At bar 20 the violin turns its short quavers into semiquavers on F. Demisemiquaver bursts then break into that pulse under the inscription about running and stamping feet.

    3. The violin runs out of F minor

      The stamping strings release the soloist in F minor. Its demisemiquavers wind around F, E♮ and G before climbing higher. The orchestra keeps the shivering figure beneath it, then interrupts with bursts marked winds as the episode travels towards E♭ major.

    4. The strings shiver in E♭

      The solo episode reaches E♭ major. The full strings bring back the repeated-note refrain in that key, including its semiquaver version. Vivaldi saves the denser stamping figures for the final return in F minor.

    5. The solo begins to chatter

      The soloist leaves the E♭-major refrain and begins another rapid passage. At bar 47 repeated semiquavers carry the inscription Batter li denti. The episode passes through A♭ major and C minor before reaching F minor for the last tutti.

    6. F minor under stamping feet

      The solo episode reaches F minor and the full strings take up the stamping music. Rapid repeated notes alternate with broken chords. The final refrain keeps this denser part of the earlier tutti through to the F-minor cadence.

  2. II. LargoJohn Harrison, violin; Wichita State University Chamber Players; Robert Turizziani, conductor · 6 February 2000
    2:12

    The solo violin sits by the fire while the orchestral violins make rain against the window. Vivaldi writes the raindrops pizzicato and forte, with the bass kept quiet beneath them.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Indoors by the fire

      “Passar al foco i dì quieti e contenti” — “To spend the days by the fire, peaceful and content.” The 1725 sonnet brings us indoors. The solo violin sings in E♭ major over plucked violin notes; the bass is marked always quiet.

    2. Rain outside the window

      “Mentre la pioggia fuor bagna ben cento” — “While outside the rain soaks a hundred others.” The orchestral violins pluck their raindrops throughout. After its first cadence in B♭ major, the solo melody starts again from B♭ and bends its way back towards E♭.

    3. The melody settles on E♭

      The solo violin reaches E♭ and holds it through the last bars. Below, the rain keeps falling in plucked semiquavers. Only at the final fermata do the orchestral violins stop their patter and hold the chord.

    How it is joined

    1. The tune starts again on B♭

      The solo’s C trill resolves to B♭ at the end of the first eight bars. The melody begins again on B♭, a fourth below its original E♭. Plucked violins and the quiet bass continue underneath; the altered second half leads back to E♭ major.

    2. The solo holds above the raindrops

      The solo’s D rises to E♭ and stays there. The orchestral violins continue plucking E♭-major arpeggios beneath the held note before the final fermata. The bowed melody comes to rest while the raindrops still move.

  3. III. AllegroJohn Harrison, violin; Wichita State University Chamber Players; Robert Turizziani, conductor · 6 February 2000
    3:13

    A solo violin ventures onto the ice above a long bass F; before long, everyone is slipping. Vivaldi ends the concerto with the Sirocco and north wind fighting through the strings.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A foot on the ice

      “Caminar Sopra’l giaccio, e à passo lento / Per timor di cader gersene intenti” — “To walk on ice with slow steps, taking care for fear of falling.” The 1725 sonnet begins cautiously. The solo violin changes direction above a long F; the other upper strings wait.

    2. Down, then up again

      “Gir forte Sdruzziolar, cader à terra / Di nuovo ir Sopra ’l giaccio e correr forte” — “Turn sharply, slip, fall to the ground; go back onto the ice and run hard.” After the orchestra’s falling scales, the solo violin scrambles upwards in detached semiquavers.

    3. Cracks between the notes

      “Sin ch’il giaccio Si rompe, e Si disserra” — “Until the ice breaks and splits open.” The strings interrupt their running with short notes and gaps. Little bursts of demisemiquavers fill the cracks before the soloist takes over again.

    4. Beyond the iron gates

      “Sentir uscir dalle ferrate porte” — “To hear emerging from the iron gates.” The sonnet unleashes the winds. For the Sirocco, Vivaldi writes Lento in E♭ major and removes the bass entirely. Only the upper strings remain.

    5. The winds at war

      “Sirocco, Borea, e tutti i Venti in guerra” — “Sirocco, Boreas, and all the winds at war.” Solo runs collide with orchestral bursts. The sonnet ends: “Quest’è ’l verno, mà tal, che gioja apporte” — “This is winter, yet it brings joy.” Vivaldi drives the strings towards F minor.

    How it is joined

    1. F moves to C

      The bass changes its long F to C, the dominant of F minor. The orchestral strings join the soloist, first in running semiquavers, then in cautious quavers. The C stays underneath as the steps grow more insistent and the falling scales begin.

    2. F minor after the fall

      The strings tumble down in scales, then the soloist starts running from F. Its detached semiquavers climb above held upper-string notes. The basso briefly rests; the solo travels from F minor towards C minor, where long C and G pedals support the later passage.

    3. The C-minor run breaks apart

      The solo’s descending triplets reach a tutti in C minor. Running scales break into short notes, rests and little demisemiquaver flurries. G, the dominant, supports the exchanges before the solo finishes the passage on a low G.

    4. The bass vanishes in E♭

      The C-minor solo ends on G. At the Lento the upper strings begin in E♭ major, and the basso falls silent for nineteen bars. The violin’s opening G–F–E♭ now moves in quavers under the words Il Vento Sirocco.

    5. The north wind starts on E♭

      The solo violin bursts into demisemiquavers on E♭, under the words naming Boreas and all the winds. Repeated-note tutti gusts interrupt its runs and bring the bass back. The episode leaves E♭ major and reaches F minor.

    6. The whole orchestra becomes the wind

      The soloist rejoins the full strings in F minor. Their repeated demisemiquavers and downward runs extend the wind music through the final tutti. E♮ sharpens the approach to F before the last held chord.
