Maurice Ravel / V. The French Line

Le Tombeau de Couperin

1914–17; orch. 1919 · Piano; four-movement orchestral version

Six piano movements bearing the names of seven men killed in the war. The orchestral version keeps the Prélude, Forlane, Menuet and Rigaudon; the Fugue and Toccata stay at the keyboard.

5 recordings · 23 min

In the Hall

  1. I. PréludeIsaac David, piano · 2017
    4:00

    Ravel opens his memorial suite with a handful of quick notes that scarcely stop moving. Isaac David plays the piano original, where a singing line has to survive inside the bustle of semiquavers.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Twelve notes to a bar

      The piano sets off in E minor, Vif, with twelve semiquavers to the bar. Longer notes stretch across the running figures. Even the little ornaments have a firm place: Ravel specifies that grace notes fall on the beat.

    2. Jacques Charlot’s name

      Above the first notes stands the name of Lieutenant Jacques Charlot, Ravel’s friend and a cousin of his publisher Jacques Durand. Charlot is one of the friends killed in the First World War whose names Ravel gives to the suite’s movements.

    3. A French suite, in wartime

      On 1 October 1914, Ravel writes to his former pupil Roland-Manuel about a French suite: “The Marseillaise doesn’t come into it at all, but there’ll be a forlane and a jig.” The war has begun; the dances are already taking shape.

    4. The left hand runs

      The left hand takes over the flowing notes while the right hand holds its line above them. Ravel sends the semiquavers down into the bass, then back across the keyboard. The hands keep exchanging the quick work and the singing work.

    5. Down, then up again

      The last runs descend from the treble into the low bass, where a note hangs under the pedal. Then the hands climb once more. A high tremolo and a short final chord bring the ceaseless movement to a stop.

    How it is joined

    1. The left hand takes over

      The second ending sends the E-minor opening onwards. The semiquavers continue, but the left hand increasingly carries them below sustained right-hand notes. Both hands have to keep the same light articulation as the running line moves into the bass.

    2. The runs reach the bass

      The descending semiquavers cross between the hands and reach a low note held under the pedal. Ravel leaves that bass sounding, then sends a new arpeggio upwards towards the final high tremolo. The running figure survives right to the last chord.

  2. II. FugueAlan Chan, piano
    3:25

    One small, off-beat phrase starts a conversation between three voices. Alan Chan plays the quiet Fugue from Le Tombeau de Couperin, a memorial to Jean Cruppi.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A phrase after silence

      A quaver rest, then the subject: short notes, little gaps and accents that fall between the beats. The piano begins pianissimo. Two more voices enter, each taking up the same phrase while the earlier voices continue around it.

    2. Jean Cruppi’s family

      The dedication remembers Second Lieutenant Jean Cruppi, killed in the First World War. His mother Louise, a pianist and salon hostess, encouraged Ravel’s early work; his father Jean was a politician. Ravel’s opera L’Heure espagnole carries Louise’s name, and this Fugue carries her son’s.

    3. Three voices, two hands

      The subject’s clipped quavers keep moving between the three parts. A held note in one voice can outlast several notes in another, and the middle voice crosses the pianist’s hands. Ravel writes separate stems and slurs for each line.

    4. Three voices stay at home

      In 1919 Ravel sends four movements of the suite into the orchestra. The Fugue stays at the piano, together with the Toccata. One player remains responsible for all three voices.

    5. The subject slows down

      Meno allegro, then Lent: the subject’s little quavers slow without losing their shape. The voices draw closer together and end on an E-minor chord, held under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. E minor, with time to breathe

      At Meno allegro, the piano returns to pianissimo and keeps the subject’s off-beat quavers. Further slowing brings the three lines to Lent. They meet on E minor, with the last bass E and the upper notes held beneath fermatas.

  3. III. Forlaneazuma, piano · January 2007
    6:54

    The Forlane has the lilting step of an old dance and chords full of sharp little surprises. Its dedication remembers Gabriel Deluc, a Basque painter killed in the First World War.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A dance with sharp elbows

      Long–short figures give the 6/8 dance its lilt. The melody steps through chords crowded with sharps and naturals; even the quiet opening has a sting. Ravel marks it Allegretto, and lets the first little strain come round again.

    2. A painter’s memorial

      Lieutenant Gabriel Deluc’s name stands above this dance. He is a Basque painter and a friend of Ravel, one of the men killed in the First World War whom Le Tombeau de Couperin remembers.

    3. Couperin on Ravel’s desk

      Ravel prepares for his own Forlane by transcribing a forlane by François Couperin. The dance comes from northern Italy and finds a home at the French court around 1700. Ravel keeps its dotted step and repeated phrases, then fills them with his own wayward chords.

    4. Chords against a held bass

      The piano alternates clipped chords with notes held across the beat. A bass note can keep sounding while the upper parts take another step. The two broad beats of 6/8 continue beneath these small disagreements.

    5. Trills among the steps

      The later phrases keep the dance’s long–short rhythm and add trills and tiny grace notes. A trill briefly turns one note into a flutter; the phrase carries on underneath. The ornaments share the beat with the main notes.

    6. The dance keeps its pace

      The final gestures become small and widely spaced. Ravel writes sans ralentir — without slowing — over the last pianissimo chord. The dance stops with its pulse still intact.

    How it is joined

    1. A small ending, without slowing

      The piano breaks the dance into short phrases, rests and sustained notes. A last grace-note figure leads to a soft chord over a low E. Ravel marks the ending pianissimo and sans ralentir, keeping the 6/8 step right to the close.

  4. V. Menuetazuma, piano · January 2007
    5:00

    A G-major minuet steps aside for a musette, a dance over a bagpipe-like drone. When the minuet comes back, Ravel lets the two dances share the keyboard.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The minuet’s little ornament

      A tiny turn decorates the melody’s first note. The tune moves lightly through three beats while the bass follows its own path underneath. Ravel marks the G-major opening pianissimo and Allegro moderato.

    2. Jean Dreyfus’s family home

      The Menuet remembers Jean Dreyfus, killed in the First World War. His stepmother, Madame Fernand Dreyfus, receives Ravel’s letters from the front and later takes him into the family home in Normandy. There he recovers from military service and works on this suite.

    3. A bagpipe beneath the chords

      The central dance is a Musette. Its G-minor chords move over a drone, the keyboard’s imitation of a bagpipe. The B♭ darkens the tune, while E♮ gives the minor scale an unexpectedly bright sixth step.

    4. The dances meet

      The minuet’s first melody returns in G major. Beneath it, the left hand keeps the musette’s chords. The piano now plays both dances at once: the ornamented tune above, the quieter chordal steps below.

    5. Arpeggios open the hands

      The left hand turns from chords to flowing arpeggios. The melody stretches above them as the hands spread farther apart. Ravel builds one last broad phrase before drawing the piano back to pianissimo.

    6. Keys pressed without sound

      The closing page asks for much slowing, then Très lent. At the end, Ravel tells the pianist to press keys silently. The pedal clears, leaving only the notes whose dampers remain raised to ring into the final G-major chord.

    How it is joined

    1. G major turns towards the bagpipe

      The minuet’s repeated strain closes, and the piano begins the Musette softly. G remains the home note, but B♭ replaces B♮. Held bass notes support the moving chords; E♮ keeps the new G-minor dance in the Dorian mode. The three-beat bar continues.

    2. The minuet returns above the musette

      The musette subsides to pianissimo. The piano restores G major and raises the opening melody above the musette’s chordal accompaniment. Its grace notes and long slurs belong to the minuet; the left hand continues the middle dance’s steps.

    3. The bass starts to flow

      Ravel keeps the upper melody and replaces the left-hand chords with arpeggios. A gradual crescendo carries the hands farther across the keyboard, then the line falls back to pianissimo. The final G-major close follows after increasingly slow phrases.

  5. VI. ToccataMarcelle Meyer, piano · 5–8 March 1954
    3:56

    Marcelle Meyer plays the finale of Le Tombeau de Couperin: repeated notes start almost under the breath, then fill the keyboard. The Toccata carries its E-minor opening into an emphatic E-major finish.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Two hands on repeated notes

      Ravel begins pianissimo, with staccato semiquavers shared between the hands. The repeated notes have a dry, quick touch. Out of those tiny attacks he builds a finale that eventually needs almost the whole keyboard.

    2. Beethoven and Joseph de Marliave

      The Toccata remembers Captain Joseph de Marliave, a musicologist who writes about Beethoven’s string quartets and is killed in the First World War. His widow is the pianist Marguerite Long, who brings Ravel’s completed suite to the concert platform.

    3. The widow at the piano

      Paris, 11 April 1919: Marguerite Long gives the first performance of the complete suite at the Salle Gaveau. Its last movement bears her husband’s name. The preceding movements carry the names of Ravel’s other friends lost in the war.

    4. A tune inside the hurry

      Long melodic notes stretch over the semiquavers. Ravel marks passages soutenu, sustained, so the singing notes hold their ground while the shorter ones keep moving. The pianist has to give both kinds of touch at once.

    5. Four sharps at the finish

      The final page opens out into E major, with four sharps and a fortississimo flourish. The repeated notes have become full chords, reinforced by low octaves. Ravel ends the suite with a sharp, ringing stop.

    How it is joined

    1. Long notes over quick ones

      The piano keeps its semiquavers while a longer melodic line emerges above them. Sustained notes and slurs ask for a singing right hand; the accompanying notes still need the opening’s light touch. Ravel holds the quick two-beat bar through the change.

    2. The bass joins the charge

      The piano’s repeated attacks expand into chords, with a stronger bass beneath them. The crescendo carries this last stretch towards the four-sharp E-major ending. Even at the broadest chordal statements, semiquavers continue to propel the hands.
