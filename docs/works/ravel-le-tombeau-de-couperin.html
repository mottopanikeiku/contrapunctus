Maurice Ravel / V. The French Line

Le Tombeau de Couperin

1914–⁠17; orchestrated 1919 · Piano; four-movement orchestral version

Six piano movements bearing the names of seven men killed in the war. The orchestral version keeps the Prélude, Forlane, Menuet and Rigaudon; the Fugue and Toccata remain piano-only.

5 recordings · 23 min

In the Hall

  1. I. PréludeIsaac David, piano · 2017
    4:00

    The Prélude is the first movement of Ravel’s memorial suite Le Tombeau de Couperin, and its line is carried in continuous rapid semiquavers. Isaac David plays the original version for piano.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening in E minor

      The piano begins in E minor, Vif, with twelve semiquavers to the bar. Longer notes are held across the running figures. Ravel specifies that the grace notes fall on the beat.

    2. Dedication to Jacques Charlot

      Above the first notes stands the name of Lieutenant Jacques Charlot, Ravel’s friend and a cousin of his publisher Jacques Durand. Charlot was one of the friends killed in the First World War whose names Ravel gave to the suite’s movements.

    3. Letter on a French suite, 1914

      On 1 October 1914, after the war had begun, Ravel wrote to his former pupil about a French suite: “The Marseillaise doesn’t come into it at all, but there’ll be a forlane and a jig.”

    4. Semiquavers in the left hand

      The left hand takes over the semiquavers while the right hand holds its line above them. Ravel moves the semiquavers down into the bass, then back across the keyboard. The hands continue to exchange the running semiquavers and the sustained line.

    5. Final bars, descent and ascent

      The last runs descend from the treble into the low bass, where a note is held under the pedal. The hands then climb again. A high tremolo and a short final chord end the continuous movement.

    Joins

    1. The left hand takes over

      The second ending carries the E minor opening on into the next section. The semiquavers continue, and the left hand plays them more and more often below sustained notes in the right hand. As the running line moves into the bass, both hands keep the same light articulation.

    2. Descending runs to the bass

      The descending semiquavers cross between the hands and reach a low note, which is held under the pedal. Ravel keeps that bass sounding while a new arpeggio rises towards the final high tremolo. The running figure continues to the last chord.

  2. II. FugueAlan Chan, piano
    3:25

    Alan Chan plays the Fugue, the second movement of Le Tombeau de Couperin. The movement is a three-voice fugue on a short off-beat subject and is dedicated to the memory of Jean Cruppi.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening subject and entries

      The subject begins after a quaver rest, with short notes, small gaps and accents off the beat. The piano starts pianissimo. Two more voices enter in turn with the same phrase, and the earlier voices continue against each new entry.

    2. Jean Cruppi and his family

      The Fugue is dedicated to Second Lieutenant Jean Cruppi, who was killed in the First World War. His mother Louise, a pianist and salon hostess, encouraged Ravel’s early work, and his father Jean was a politician. Ravel had dedicated his opera L’Heure espagnole to Louise.

    3. Three-voice texture

      The subject’s clipped quavers move between the three parts. A held note in one voice can outlast several notes in another, and the middle voice passes between the pianist’s hands. Ravel writes separate stems and slurs for each line.

    4. Orchestration of 1919

      In 1919 Ravel orchestrated four movements of the suite. He left out the Fugue and the Toccata, which exist only for piano. The Fugue is in three voices, all played by one pianist.

    5. Final bars of the Fugue

      At Meno allegro, then Lent, the quavers of the subject slow down and keep their shape. The voices move closer together and end on an E minor chord held under a fermata.

    Joins

    1. Meno allegro to Lent, closing on E minor

      At Meno allegro the piano returns to pianissimo and keeps the subject’s off-beat quavers. The tempo slows further to Lent, and the three lines close on E minor. The last bass E and the upper notes are held under fermatas.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  3. III. Forlaneazuma, piano · January 2007
    6:54

    The Forlane, the third movement of Le Tombeau de Couperin, takes the rhythm of an old dance and sets it to chords that move in unexpected directions. It is dedicated to the memory of Gabriel Deluc, a Basque painter killed in the First World War.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Forlane, opening

      Ravel marks the 6/8 dance Allegretto, and long–short figures set its rhythm. From the quiet opening, the melody moves step by step through chords full of sharps and naturals. The first short strain is repeated.

    2. Dedication to Gabriel Deluc

      This dance is dedicated to Lieutenant Gabriel Deluc. He was a Basque painter and a friend of Ravel, and one of the men killed in the First World War who are commemorated in Le Tombeau de Couperin.

    3. Ravel’s Couperin transcription

      Before writing his own Forlane, Ravel transcribed a forlane by François Couperin. The dance came from northern Italy and was taken up at the French court around 1700. Ravel’s movement keeps its dotted step and repeated phrases and sets them to his own chords.

    4. Chords over a held bass

      The piano alternates detached chords with notes held across the beat. A bass note can sound on while the upper parts move to the next chord. The two dotted-crotchet beats of 6/8 continue throughout.

    5. Trills and grace notes

      The later phrases keep the dance’s long–short rhythm and add trills and short grace notes. During a trill on one note, the phrase continues in the other parts. A footnote at the opening of the Durand score (1918) specifies that grace notes fall on the beat.

    6. Final bars, sans ralentir

      The final gestures are short and widely spaced. Over the last pianissimo chord Ravel writes sans ralentir (without slowing).

    Joins

    1. The close, without slowing

      The piano breaks the dance into short phrases, rests and sustained notes. A last grace-note figure leads to a soft chord over a low E. Ravel marks the ending pianissimo and sans ralentir, and the 6/8 metre continues to the final bar.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  4. V. Menuetazuma, piano · January 2007
    5:00

    The fifth movement of Ravel’s Le Tombeau de Couperin is a minuet in G major with a musette, a dance over a bagpipe-like drone, as its middle section. When the minuet returns, Ravel combines the two dances at the keyboard.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening of the Menuet

      A turn ornaments the first note of the melody. The tune moves in three beats over an independent bass line. Ravel marked the G major opening pianissimo and Allegro moderato.

    2. Dedication to Jean Dreyfus

      The Menuet is dedicated to Jean Dreyfus, who was killed in the First World War. Ravel wrote to his stepmother, Madame Fernand Dreyfus, from the front. She later took him into the family home in Normandy, where he recovered from military service and worked on this suite.

    3. Composition, 1914–⁠1917

      Ravel began in 1914 with a single piano Forlane. Military service interrupted the work, and he returned to it in the spring of 1917, after his discharge. He dedicated each piece to the memory of a friend or comrade killed in the war.

    4. Musette, bars 33–⁠48

      The central dance is a Musette. Its chords move over a drone, the keyboard’s imitation of a bagpipe. The tune uses B♭ and E♮, which places it in G Dorian, a minor mode with a raised sixth step.

    5. Minuet and musette combined

      The minuet’s first melody returns in G major. Beneath it, the left hand plays the musette’s chords, so the two dances sound together.

    6. Piano and orchestral premières, 1919–⁠1920

      Marguerite Long gave the first performance of the piano suite at the Salle Gaveau on 11 April 1919. Ravel later scored four of its six movements for small orchestra, the Menuet among them. Rhené-⁠Baton conducted them at the Concerts Pasdeloup on 28 February 1920.

    7. Arpeggiated bass, bars 105–⁠120

      The left hand changes from chords to arpeggios, and the melody rises above them as the hands move farther apart. Ravel writes one last broad phrase with a crescendo, then returns the piano to pianissimo.

    8. Silently depressed keys, final bars

      The closing page asks for much slowing, then Très lent. At the end Ravel told the pianist to press keys silently. The pedal clears, and only the notes whose dampers remain raised ring into the final G major chord.

    Joins

    1. From the Menuet to the Musette

      The repeated strain of the minuet closes, and the piano begins the Musette softly. G remains the home note, and B♭ replaces B♮. Held bass notes support the moving chords. E♮ places the G minor dance in the Dorian mode. The bar stays in three beats.

    2. The minuet returns above the musette

      The musette drops to pianissimo. The piano returns to G major and plays the opening melody of the minuet above the musette’s chordal accompaniment. The right hand keeps the minuet’s grace notes and long slurs. The left hand continues the steps of the middle dance.

    3. Left-hand chords become arpeggios

      The upper melody stays the same, and the left-hand chords are replaced by arpeggios. A gradual crescendo takes the hands farther apart across the keyboard. The line then falls back to pianissimo. Each of the following phrases is slower than the one before, and the movement closes in G major.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  5. VI. ToccataMarcelle Meyer, piano · 5–⁠8 March 1954
    3:56

    Marcelle Meyer plays the Toccata, the finale of Le Tombeau de Couperin. Repeated notes begin softly and spread across the keyboard, and the movement opens in E minor and ends in E major.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, repeated semiquavers

      Ravel begins pianissimo, with staccato semiquavers shared between the hands. The repeated notes are short and quick. By the final pages the texture has grown to chords and wide arpeggios across most of the keyboard.

    2. Dedication to Joseph de Marliave

      The Toccata is dedicated to the memory of Captain Joseph de Marliave, a musicologist who wrote about Beethoven’s string quartets and was killed in the First World War. His widow, the pianist Marguerite Long, gave the first performance of Ravel’s completed suite.

    3. First performance, Paris, 1919

      Marguerite Long gave the first performance of the complete suite at the Salle Gaveau, Paris, on 11 April 1919. The last movement bears her husband’s name. The preceding movements carry the names of other friends of Ravel’s who were lost in the war.

    4. Sustained melody over semiquavers

      Long melodic notes are held over continuing semiquavers. Ravel marks passages soutenu, sustained, and the pianist plays the held notes and the semiquavers with different touches at the same time.

    5. Final page in E major

      The final page moves to E major, with a signature of four sharps and a fortississimo flourish. The repeated notes are now full chords, doubled by low octaves. Ravel ends the suite on a single chord.

    Joins

    1. Sustained melody over semiquavers

      The piano continues its semiquavers, and a longer melodic line enters above them. Sustained notes and slurs mark the right-hand melody for a legato, singing touch, and the accompanying semiquavers keep the light touch of the opening. Ravel keeps the 2/4 metre through the change (Durand piano score, 1918, pp. 27–⁠29).

    2. Chords and bass in the final crescendo

      The piano’s repeated notes widen into chords, and the bass line becomes heavier beneath them. A crescendo runs through this final section to the close in E major, with four sharps, at bar 240. The semiquaver motion continues in the hands through the broadest chordal passages.

    Open in the Concert Hall
