Frédéric Chopin / III. Romanticism

Ballade No. 1, Op. 23

1831–35 · Op. 23 · Piano

Its two principal themes return in reverse order before a headlong coda. The opening Largo sounds like a speaker gathering the room’s attention.

10 min

The recording

  1. Largo — ModeratoFrank Lévy
    10:14

    Frank Lévy plays Chopin’s First Ballade, where a quiet G-minor melody and an E♭-major song return with alarming force. The last word belongs to a coda marked Presto con fuoco — very fast, with fire.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. An octave apart

      Both hands start on C and climb an octave apart, outlining A♭ major. The marking is Largo. The line reaches the treble, hesitates, and settles into chords with an E♭ rubbing against D below it.

    2. G minor, quietly

      The piano settles into Moderato and a broad six-beat bar. A circling G-minor melody sings above detached bass notes and chords. The hands have separated: one speaks, the other keeps the phrase moving.

    3. The E♭-major song

      The second theme opens in E♭ major, marked meno mosso — less movement. Long notes in the right hand float above the left hand’s rolling chords. The phrase has room to spread across several bars.

    4. A minor over E

      The first melody returns in A minor. Low repeated Es take the place of the opening accompaniment, and the tune begins quietly again. Its next answer will come from the second theme, in A major.

    5. The song finds its voice

      The second theme arrives in A major with full right-hand chords and a low A in the bass. What began as a gently accompanied song now fills both ends of the keyboard.

    6. Chopin’s own favourite

      In Leipzig in 1836, Schumann tells Chopin that this Ballade is his favourite of Chopin’s works. Chopin thinks for a long time. Then, in Schumann’s account, he replies: “I’m glad of that because it’s the one I prefer too.”

    7. E♭ major, fortissimo

      The second theme returns first, in its original E♭ major. Chopin marks it fortissimo and sends the left hand sweeping through broken chords. The tune has kept its long notes; the piano beneath them is in full flight.

    8. The first tune whispers

      G minor returns with the first theme, marked meno mosso. The left hand repeats a low D very quietly. This recollection is short: D-major chords soon gather underneath it, preparing the coda.

    9. Presto con fuoco

      The coda starts with a low G and leaping chords. Presto con fuoco: the six-beat sway is gone. Accents land inside the bar, and the right hand repeatedly drops from a high chord to a single lower note.

    10. Scales and silences

      A low G holds the floor while scales sweep up the keyboard. Heavy G-minor chords interrupt the runs; pauses interrupt the chords. The final descent comes in octaves, step after chromatic step, before the last G.

    How it is joined

    1. Octaves become a quiet melody

      The Largo ends with E♭ above a bass D. At Moderato, the metre changes to 6/4 and the hands separate into melody and accompaniment. A low D introduces the quiet G-minor theme, followed by detached bass notes and chords.

    2. The runs leave room for E♭

      The running figures dwindle and stop. Under meno mosso, a long right-hand phrase opens in E♭ major while the left hand rolls gently through its chords. The six-beat bar remains, but the melody now stretches across it.

    3. The first melody moves to A minor

      The E♭-major song slows through its last decorated phrases. Repeated low Es then underpin the first theme in A minor. The accompaniment begins quietly and thickens into E dominant chords, ready for the second theme’s A-major answer.

    4. A falling run reaches the song

      A long descending run reaches B♭ in the bass. The lyrical theme returns above it in full chords, fortissimo, and the bass moves to E♭. The left hand keeps sweeping through broken chords beneath the melody.

    5. The bass settles on D

      The right-hand runs slow and fall towards the middle of the piano. At meno mosso, the first theme returns in G minor above repeated low Ds, marked pianissimo. The broad E♭-major song has shrunk to a quiet recollection.

    6. D major releases the coda

      D-major dominant chords gather beneath the descending right hand. The coda answers with a low G, Presto con fuoco, and a new four-beat bar. High chords leap down to single notes while the left hand alternates bass notes and chords.
