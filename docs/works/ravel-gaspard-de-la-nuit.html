Maurice Ravel / V. The French Line

Gaspard de la nuit

1908 · Piano; after Aloysius Bertrand

The three movements depict a water spirit, a gallows and a tormenting dwarf, and Bertrand’s poems are printed before them. In Le Gibet, placed between Ondine and Scarbo, a B♭ is repeated throughout.

3 recordings · 24 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Ondine — LentAndriy Makarevych, piano · 1 April 2014
    6:48

    Ondine (Lent) is the first of Ravel’s three poems for piano in Gaspard de la nuit, in which a water spirit’s song is set beneath a tremble of piano chords. The pianist is Andriy Makarevych.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Left-hand melody below chords

      The right hand plays closely packed chords, ppp. Below them the left hand has a slower melody, marked très doux et très expressif, several notes below the top of the piano texture.

    2. Bertrand’s poem <i>Ondine</i>

      In Bertrand’s poem, Ondine touches a moonlit window and offers the man inside her ring. “Mon palais est bâti fluide, au fond du lac” — “My palace is built of water, at the bottom of the lake.” She asks him to marry her and become king of the lakes.

    3. Wide arpeggios, chordal melody

      The arpeggios run from the low bass to the high treble. Ravel sets the melody in chords among them, with hairpins from pp to forte within a single arpeggio. The pianist must sustain the longer melody notes through the figuration.

    4. Ending of Bertrand’s poem

      In the final paragraph of Bertrand’s poem, printed before the movement in the Durand score (1909), the man tells Ondine that he loves a mortal woman. The water spirit sheds a few tears, bursts out laughing and vanishes in a shower down the blue windowpanes.

    5. Unaccompanied phrase, Très lent

      Near the end the accompaniment stops. A single line is played in the middle of the keyboard, Très lent, with rests below it.

    6. Closing passage in C♯ major

      Both hands play a rapid passage, Rapide et brillant, which slows and gives way to ppp arpeggios in C♯ major. Bertrand’s last image is “mes vitraux bleus” — “my blue stained-glass windows” — with water streaming down them.

    Joins

    1. Fermata, then unaccompanied Très lent line

      The piano’s running figures climb to a held high note and stop under a fermata. The marking then changes to Très lent, and an unaccompanied phrase follows in the middle of the keyboard. The bass staff is empty.

    2. Rapid passage to the closing arpeggios

      The bare phrase leads into a rush of notes in both hands, marked Rapide et brillant. Ravel then marks retenez peu à peu, and the pianist holds back gradually. C♯-major arpeggios return at ppp, au mouvement du début, and continue to the final chord.

  2. II. Le Gibet — Très lentOE1FEU, piano · 3 April 2020
    7:05

    Le Gibet, marked Très lent, is the second piece of Ravel’s Gaspard de la nuit. It is a setting of Aloysius Bertrand’s poem of a gallows at sunset, and a repeated B♭ sounds like a bell throughout while chords move around it.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Repeated B♭ octaves

      A B♭ in octaves repeats throughout while slow chords move beneath it, in a rhythm independent of theirs. Ravel marked the piece Très lent and asked for the soft pedal throughout.

    2. Bertrand’s poem <i>Le Gibet</i>

      Bertrand’s poem lists small, horrible sounds near a hanged man, among them a fly at the dead man’s ear and a spider making his necktie. The source is named in the line “C’est la cloche qui tinte” — “It is the bell that tolls.” Beyond the town walls, the setting sun reddens the hanging body.

    3. Ravel and Aloysius Bertrand, 1908

      Ravel and Ricardo Viñes read Aloysius Bertrand as students, along with Baudelaire, Poe and Mallarmé. A new edition in 1908 brought Ravel back to him. He wrote Gaspard, “three romantic poems of transcendental virtuosity”, between May and 5 September, and Viñes gave the first performance on 9 January 1909.

    4. Repeated B♭ on three staves

      Ravel writes the piano part on three staves. High and low chords move around the repeated B♭, which passes between the hands. Where the chords fill the keyboard, the B♭ continues to sound inside them.

    5. Upper melody over the B♭

      A melody enters in the upper register above the repeated B♭. Ravel marks it un peu en dehors, mais sans expression, slightly brought out but without expression. The B♭ continues below it unchanged.

    6. Return of the tied chords

      The piano texture fills out again in long, tied chords. The bell note, still B♭, sounds between them. Ravel’s instruction from the first page still applies: “without hurrying or slowing to the end”.

    7. Recording, Kaisersaal, 2020

      OE1FEU made this recording of the piece on a Bechstein D 282 concert grand in the Kaisersaal, and dated it 3 April 2020. It was released under a Creative Commons Attribution–ShareAlike licence.

    8. Final page, repeated B♭

      On the last page the melodic fragments stop. Low bass notes are held under the repeated B♭, and the final bars drop to ppp. The B♭ continues until the last chord.

    Joins

    1. Three staves around the B♭

      The piano writing expands to three staves, with low held notes, the recurring B♭ and chords high above it. The two hands share the bell, and the upper chords are marked très lié, very smoothly joined. The B♭ is repeated through each change of harmony.

    2. Upper melody above the low bell

      The fuller chords thin out. A single upper line, marked pp and sans expression, crosses above the B♭ in the bass. Each of its long notes is held across several strokes of the bell.

    3. Tied chords and the six-flat signature

      The single upper line broadens into tied chords as the six-flat key signature returns. The B♭ bell continues between the hands, above a sustained low bass. Small crescendo hairpins mark the chords, and the slow tempo does not change.

    4. Return to the opening bell texture

      The high chords diminish, and the piano goes back to the slow bell-and-chord writing of the opening. Deep sustained notes lie under B♭ octaves. The repeated figure gets softer, moving through p, pp and ppp. Ravel’s instruction to keep the same pace applies through to the end.

  3. III. Scarbo — ModéréAndriy Makarevych, piano · 1 April 2014
    9:39

    Scarbo, marked Modéré, is the third piece of Ravel’s Gaspard de la nuit. Scarbo is the creature who scratches at the bed curtains, vanishes and comes back larger than the room, and Ravel writes soft repeated notes, very wide leaps and long silences for him.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening of Scarbo

      Three low notes rise in the piano. A soft repeated note sounds beneath a held chord, then stops. Ravel puts a fermata over the silence and marks it très long (very long).

    2. Acceleration to Vif

      The piano accelerates through rising figures to Vif. The hands move far apart and play bass notes and high chords at the same quick tempo. Sudden rests interrupt the passage.

    3. Scarbo in Bertrand’s poem

      Bertrand’s narrator hears Scarbo laughing in his alcove, then “grincer son ongle sur la soie des courtines de mon lit” — “his nail scraping the silk curtains of my bed”. The dwarf spins on one foot and rolls around the room like a witch’s dropped spindle.

    4. Rising three-note figure

      The rising three-note figure of the opening returns in new octaves. Repeated notes and leaps carry much of the writing, and the small figures expand into large groups of chords.

    5. Return of the opening

      The opening returns with its low three-note figure, held chord and repeated notes. This time the repeated notes lie lower in the bass. Ravel marks the soft pedal and writes almost nothing in the upper register of the piano.

    6. Bertrand’s “Scarbo”, fourth paragraph

      In Bertrand’s poem the dwarf grows between the narrator and the moon “comme le clocher d’une cathédrale gothique” — “like the tower of a Gothic cathedral”. A little golden bell shakes on his pointed cap.

    7. Final bars, Sans ralentir

      The piano plays ppp repeated chords, then a final run up the keyboard. Ravel marks the passage Sans ralentir, without slowing. Bertrand’s poem ends “et soudain il s’éteignait”, “and suddenly he went out”, like a candle.

    Joins

    1. From held silences to <i>Vif</i>

      After the held silences, rising figures in the piano accelerate. At Vif the hands move apart, with low notes alternating with high chords, and a crescendo runs from pp to ff. Two bars of rests break off the passage, and then the moving accompaniment resumes.

    2. The low three notes return

      The running figures stop, and the three low notes of the opening return under the soft pedal. The held chord and the repeated-note figure follow them, with another fermata. Ravel then moves the repeated notes down into the bass at ppp.

    3. The chords become a flicker

      The wide arpeggios contract into quiet repeated chords above a low held bass. The pianist moves through short rests and little answering figures, then darts upwards to the last note. Sans ralentir keeps the ending quick.
