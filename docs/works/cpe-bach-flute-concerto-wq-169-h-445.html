Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach / II. Classical

Flute Concerto, Wq 169 / H 445

1755, original version; flute adaptation later · Solo flute, strings and continuo

6 min

The recording

  1. III. PrestoConstance Schoepflin, flute; Advent Chamber Orchestra · February 2008; programme dates 10 and 17 February
    6:07

    Constance Schoepflin and the Advent Chamber Orchestra play the quick finale of Bach’s G major Flute Concerto. The flute starts among the strings, then breaks away with trills and long runs of its own.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A tune for everyone

      The Presto begins with a little rising scale, clipped notes and a trill. Flute and strings share the tune; the bass has running notes of its own. Quick switches between piano and forte give the opening a mischievous edge.

    2. The flute adds a trill

      For its first solo, the flute takes the opening tune and gives it a new flourish: a trill on the pickup G and a slur across the bar line. The strings step back into accompaniment. The same rising notes acquire a more supple voice.

    3. An organ concerto finds breath

      Bach first writes this concerto for organ in 1755. To make a flute version, he raises low passages, changes others and leaves room for breathing. A keyboard player’s hands can keep going when a flautist’s lungs cannot.

    4. At Frederick’s keyboard

      In 1740 Bach accompanies Frederick II’s first flute solo after his accession, at Charlottenburg. The king’s new harpsichordist is twenty-six. Flute playing is part of his daily professional world at the Prussian court.

    5. A blank staff for Bach

      A copyist writes out the keyboard concerto and leaves the top staff empty. Bach fills it with a flute part in his own hand, then changes the accompaniment. The surviving manuscript catches the composer at work, turning one solo instrument’s music into another’s.

    6. G hangs over D

      The running flute line reaches a fermata: G hangs above D in the bass. The violins and viola rest. Bach writes a little turn and the descent towards the returning tune after this held point; the breathless movement has made room for a breath.

    7. Three bars for the lungs

      Late in the finale, Bach inserts three bars absent from the organ version. The flute rests while the strings play. Then the soloist starts another long run. The new bars give a human breath to a phrase first imagined for a keyboard.

    8. The flute rejoins the strings

      The final tutti brings the flute back into the ensemble’s tune. Trills, slurred pairs and repeated high notes lead towards the close. Flute, strings and bass arrive together on G major.

    How it is joined

    1. A trill makes the tune solo

      The opening ensemble passage closes in G major. The flute takes the rising theme alone, adding a trill to the pickup G and slurring across the bar line. Strings and bass accompany more quietly beneath it. The tempo stays Presto; the extra ornament and changed balance distinguish the solo.

    2. The run stops over D

      Descending flute semiquavers run above repeated D notes in the bass. They stop at a fermata, with G in the flute suspended above the bass’s held D. The violins and viola are silent. The figured bass marks the suspended fourth resolving to a third before the G major theme returns.

    3. The ensemble answers the fermata

      After the held D in the bass, a low G announces the return. The flute’s pickup leads into the opening scale, now shared with the violins. Viola and bass resume their moving parts under the forte ensemble. The music keeps its two-four metre and Presto pace.

    4. All together for the last cadence

      The solo flute’s running line gives way to a shared tutti, marked forte in the strings and bass. Slurred pairs and repeated high notes recall the opening. The bass works through the closing phrases to a final D–G cadence, with the flute joining the strings on the G major ending.
