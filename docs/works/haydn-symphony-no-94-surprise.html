Joseph Haydn / II. Classical

Symphony No. 94 “Surprise”

1791–92 · Hob. I:94 · Orchestra

The loud chord comes in the quiet C major theme of the slow movement. The variations that follow have surprises of their own.

4 recordings · 21 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Adagio cantabile — Vivace assaiBoston Symphony Orchestra, Serge Koussevitzky · 1929
    6:10

    The first movement of Haydn’s Symphony No. 94, the “Surprise”, opens with a quiet exchange between winds and strings; at the Vivace assai two violins begin the quick movement before the full orchestra enters. Serge Koussevitzky conducts the Boston Symphony Orchestra in this 1929 recording.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening phrases, winds and strings

      A two-bar phrase in the winds is followed by a two-bar phrase in the low strings. The strings move towards D major, and when the exchange is repeated they return to G. Haydn marked the passage Adagio cantabile.

    2. Slow introduction

      Every London symphony except No. 95 begins with a slow introduction. Orrin Howard described this one as “a brief, patrician prelude” with a passing grey cloud that darkens the horizon. No. 95, the only one in a minor key, has no introduction.

    3. Opening of the Vivace assai

      The Vivace begins in the two violin sections, with a G♯ in the second violins’ first bar pointing towards A minor. Four bars into the new tempo the full orchestra arrives forte on G. The bass holds that G while the notes above it grow quicker.

    4. First performance, London, 1792

      The symphony was first performed on 23 March 1792, in a concert organised by Johann Peter Salomon during Haydn’s first London visit. Haydn’s pupil Ignaz Pleyel was providing music for a rival concert series in the city.

    5. Three themes in the exposition

      Orrin Howard identified three themes in the exposition. He called the first whimsical, noting that it begins “mischievously outside the home key”. He described the second as a restrained, pure waltz tune with a lilt, and the third as a warm melody with distinctive downward leaps.

    6. Morning Herald review of the première

      After the première the Morning Herald’s critic called the subject “remarkably simple, but extended to vast complication”. Richard Wigmore applied this to the movement. The repeated notes and rising chromatic lines of the slow introduction return as material in the quick Vivace.

    7. Development, Vivace assai

      The development begins with a variant of the first tune. At bar 123 loud broken chords take over, moving from A major through D minor and B♭ major to G minor.

    8. Retransition and recapitulation

      The development ends with a cadence in B minor. Haydn repeats the note B, then puts G beneath it, so that the same note now belongs to G major. The recapitulation of the first theme begins at bar 154.

    9. Haydn’s answer to Griesinger

      Griesinger asked Haydn, in jest, whether the Andante’s drum stroke was meant to wake dozing Englishmen. Haydn said he had wanted to surprise the public and debut brilliantly, so that his pupil Pleyel should not outdo him. The first Allegro, he added, had already won countless bravos.

    10. Recapitulation of the first movement

      The return of the main theme brings what Richard Wigmore called, in effect, “an intensive second development”. The theme reaches a rounded conclusion only towards the end.

    Joins

    1. From the Adagio introduction to the Vivace

      The introduction ends on a dominant seventh on D. The first violins lead into the Vivace with falling quavers, and the metre changes from 3/4 to 6/8. Both violin sections begin the Vivace on their own. Four bars later the full orchestra enters forte on G, and the bass holds the G for nine bars.

    2. From exposition into development

      In the Vivace assai (bars 99–153), the exposition closes in D major and thins to repeated B notes in the first violins. The development opens with a variant of the first tune. The broken chords that follow start abruptly on A major and move through D minor, B♭ major and G minor before a chromatic descent.

    3. Recapitulation of the first theme

      A descending chromatic passage reaches F♯ major and then resolves to B minor. The repeated B notes continue. G is added to them, which makes B the third of the home chord. The first theme returns in G major with its repetitions shortened.

  2. II. AndanteBoston Symphony Orchestra, Serge Koussevitzky · 1929
    7:18

    The Andante of Haydn’s Symphony No. 94, the “Surprise”, opens with a short C major tune played softly by the strings, followed by a single chord from the full orchestra. The rest of the movement is built on the same tune.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening theme and fortissimo chord

      The violins play a short theme in C major, and the bass adds a note at the start of each bar. The first phrase is repeated pianissimo, with pizzicato strings. Then the full orchestra plays a fortissimo G major chord.

    2. First variation

      In the first variation (bars 33–48) the second violins and violas play the theme while the first violins add a semiquaver counterpoint above it. A flute joins some of the answering phrases.

    3. Griesinger’s biography of Haydn, 1810

      In Griesinger’s 1810 biography, Haydn described his aim in London: “I wanted to surprise the public with something new.” His pupil Pleyel was then writing for a rival concert series. Haydn intended to make the stronger impression.

    4. Second variation, C minor

      The second variation is in C minor. Loud unison phrases alternate with quiet string passages, and the first and second violins then exchange phrases through E♭ major and F minor.

    5. Third variation, oboe and flute

      C major returns with the oboe. Later the flute joins it in a duet, and the violins play the melody beneath the two wind instruments.

    6. Fourth variation, full orchestra

      The full orchestra plays the fourth variation fortissimo, with off-beat accompaniment. A passage for strings and bassoon follows, marked piano, before the full orchestra returns fortissimo.

    7. Coda over a C pedal

      In the coda, from bar 139, the winds play the theme above held string chords. The harmony moves through seventh and ninth chords over a C pedal in the bass.

    Joins

    1. First variation adds violin semiquavers

      The theme closes in C major. An orchestral chord opens the first variation (bars 32–48), in which the second violins and violas take the melody and the first violins play running semiquavers above it. The bass follows the same harmonic progression as in the theme, and the Andante tempo is unchanged.

    2. C major to C minor

      C major changes to C minor in the second variation, which begins in bar 49. E♭ replaces E♮ above the same home note. The orchestra plays the opening phrase loudly in unison, and quiet strings then play a shortened answer. Haydn joins the beginning of the original tune directly to its ending, which compresses the theme’s two halves.

    3. From the minor variation to the third

      A falling figure in the first violins leads out of the minor variation. The next statement returns to C major, with the tune in the first oboe above the strings. Later the melody passes to the violins, and flute and oboe add their own parts above it.

    4. Into the fourth variation

      The variation led by the winds closes with a cadence in C major. The fourth variation follows in the same key and tempo, fortissimo for the full orchestra, over a syncopated accompaniment. Trumpets and timpani join the strings and winds for the march-like opening of the tune (bars 106–114).

    5. Coda over a held C

      After the final variation, the coda begins at bar 139. The winds play the tune again, and its outline is unchanged. The strings hold seventh and ninth chords beneath it while C stays in the bass.

  3. III. Menuetto: Allegro moltoBoston Symphony Orchestra, Serge Koussevitzky · 1929
    3:50

    This is the third movement of Haydn’s Symphony No. 94, the “Surprise”, a minuet marked Allegro molto, the same tempo marking Haydn gave the finale. In the Trio between the louder outer sections, the first violins and bassoon play together.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening theme of the minuet

      Flute, bassoon and first violins play the minuet’s opening tune, which leaps through G major in crotchets. The marking is Allegro molto.

    2. Minuet, Allegro molto

      Richard Wigmore called this the fastest and lustiest minuet in all Haydn’s symphonies, a rustic dance with an “oompah” accompaniment. The second half is more involved. Its phrases are irregular, it has touches of counterpoint, and a three-note figure recurs.

    3. Inverted figure in the Trio

      The rising quaver figure that closes the first half of the minuet returns inverted in the Trio.

    4. Trio, violins and bassoon

      The Trio stays in G major and leaves out most of the winds. The first violins and a bassoon play the tune above the other strings, all marked piano. The first half falls into two phrases of four bars each.

    5. Minuet and Trio, Orrin Howard

      Orrin Howard called the minuet “a true Austrian peasant dance”. He described the Trio at its centre as “delightfully subtle and dignified”.

    6. Return of the Menuetto

      At the end of the Trio the score marks Da capo, and the minuet is played again from the beginning. The flute plays with the bassoon and violins once more, and the full orchestra plays forte.

    Joins

    1. Minuet to Trio in G major

      The minuet closes over a held G in the bass. The Trio stays in G major and reduces the orchestra to strings and bassoon, marked piano. Its falling quaver figure reverses a rising figure from the minuet.

    2. Trio to Menuetto da capo

      The Trio closes in G major and is followed by the instruction Menuetto da capo. The opening returns in the same key, with the full orchestra at forte. Flute, bassoon and first violins again play the tune together.

  4. IV. Finale: Allegro moltoBoston Symphony Orchestra, Serge Koussevitzky · 1929
    3:31

    The finale of Haydn’s Symphony No. 94, the “Surprise”, marked Allegro molto, opens with a quiet tune in the strings. Later the tune makes a false return, there is an outburst in the minor key, and a timpani roll comes in ahead of the orchestra.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening theme and repeats

      The strings play the opening theme alone, and a flute joins them for the repeat. When the theme returns, flute and bassoon join the strings. The full orchestra enters after these statements, which take up bars 1–38.

    2. Second theme over a pedal D

      The second theme, from bar 75, is also quiet, and the strings again lead. Beneath it the bass repeats D for most of the theme while the harmony alternates between D major and A major.

    3. Opening of the development

      The development opens with the first theme in G major, now with bassoon (bars 99–124). Its closing figure then moves into the bass and passes through a rapid succession of keys.

    4. Recapitulation and coda

      The recapitulation in G major begins at bar 182. It leaves out the repeated opening of the first theme and starts with its contrasting phrase, and Haydn cuts the transition between the two themes. The second theme returns in the home key, and the coda begins at bar 222.

    5. Coda, timpani roll and E♭ major

      The coda begins softly at bar 222 with the upbeat of the theme. A timpani roll reaches forte one bar before the rest of the orchestra, and the first theme returns in E♭ major.

    Joins

    1. Return of the opening tune in G

      The exposition ends with three loud D chords. Repeated C notes then turn the harmony towards G major, and the opening tune returns with bassoon added to the strings. The tune’s closing figure then moves into the bass.

    2. Through D major to a shortened return

      The development moves through E♭, F and B♭ major and then reaches D major. The first violins’ semiquavers come to a stop before G major returns. Haydn begins the recapitulation with the contrasting phrase of the first tune and cuts its repeated opening.

    3. Into the coda: timpani roll, E♭ major

      The second tune, in G major, leads straight into a quiet coda (bars 222–268) built on the opening upbeat. A timpani roll grows loud before the orchestra enters, and the harmony turns to E♭ major. The first tune returns forte in that key, and the movement closes on chords in G major.
