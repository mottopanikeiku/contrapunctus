Joseph Haydn / II. Classical

Symphony No. 94 “Surprise”

1791–92 · Hob. I:94 · Orchestra

The famous loud chord lands in the quiet C major theme of the slow movement. The variations that follow have surprises of their own.

4 recordings · 21 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Adagio cantabile — Vivace assaiBoston Symphony Orchestra, Serge Koussevitzky · 1929
    6:10

    Haydn opens with a quiet exchange between winds and strings, then lets two violins slip into the quick movement before the orchestra catches up. Serge Koussevitzky conducts the Boston Symphony Orchestra in this 1929 recording.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Winds speak, strings answer

      A two-bar phrase in the winds meets a two-bar answer from low strings. The strings turn towards D major; when the exchange comes round again, they return to G. Haydn marks this little conversation Adagio cantabile.

    2. Two violins steal away

      The Vivace starts quietly in the two violin sections, still leaning towards D major. Four bars into the new tempo, the whole orchestra lands on G. The bass holds that G while the notes above it grow quicker.

    3. London, March 1792

      The new symphony reaches its first audience on 23 March 1792, during Haydn’s first London visit. Johann Peter Salomon is organising the concerts; across town, Haydn’s pupil Ignaz Pleyel is providing music for a rival series.

    4. The tune takes a detour

      The development begins with a variant of the first tune. Then loud broken chords take over, travelling from A major through D minor, B♭ major and G minor. The violins have left the exposition’s easy dance behind.

    5. B becomes part of G

      The approach to the return closes in B minor. Haydn keeps repeating B, then places G beneath it: the same note now belongs to G major. The first tune comes home almost under cover.

    How it is joined

    1. A hush, then the Vivace

      The introduction reaches a dominant seventh on D. The first violins lead out with falling quavers, and the metre changes from 3/4 to 6/8. Both violin sections begin the Vivace quietly; only four bars later does the full orchestra arrive forte on G, which the bass holds for nine bars.

    2. The first tune starts travelling

      The exposition’s D major close dwindles to repeated B notes in the first violins. A variant of the first tune starts the development. The later broken chords begin abruptly on A major, then turn through D minor, B♭ major and G minor before a chromatic descent.

    3. The first theme comes home

      A descending chromatic passage reaches F♯ major, then resolves to B minor. The repeated B notes continue, but G joins them and turns them into the third of the home chord. The first tune returns in G major, with its repetitions shortened.

  2. II. AndanteBoston Symphony Orchestra, Serge Koussevitzky · 1929
    7:18

    A small C major tune, soft strings, then one outrageous chord from the whole orchestra. Haydn spends the rest of the Andante showing how much more there is to this tune than the joke.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A tune on tiptoe

      The violins play a little C major tune, with the bass adding a note at the start of each bar. Its first phrase returns even more softly, with plucked strings. Then the whole orchestra hits a fortissimo G major chord.

    2. The tune gains a companion

      The second violins and violas keep the tune while the first violins weave semiquavers around it. A flute joins some of the answering phrases. The plain melody is still there, tucked beneath the decoration.

    3. Haydn wants to surprise London

      In Griesinger’s 1810 biography, Haydn explains his London ambition: “I wanted to surprise the public with something new.” His pupil Pleyel is writing for a rival concert series. Haydn means to make the stronger impression.

    4. C major turns minor

      The second variation strikes out in C minor. Loud unison phrases meet quiet strings; then the two violin sections answer each other through E♭ major and F minor. Haydn stretches the neat tune into a much less tidy conversation.

    5. An oboe takes the tune

      C major returns with the oboe. Later the flute joins it in a duet, while the violins carry the familiar melody beneath the wind parts. The orchestra has become a small chamber ensemble.

    6. The little tune marches

      The full orchestra plays the fourth variation fortissimo, with off-beat accompaniment. Then Haydn slips in a quiet passage for strings and bassoon, marked piano, before the grand manner returns.

    7. Strange chords under familiar notes

      In the coda, the winds bring back the simple tune above held string chords. Sevenths and ninths cloud the harmony while C stays in the bass. Haydn has saved his gentlest surprise for the end.

    How it is joined

    1. Semiquavers grow around the tune

      The theme closes in C major. A fresh orchestral chord launches the first variation, then the second violins and violas take the melody while the first violins add running semiquavers. The bass keeps the same harmonic route and the Andante pulse continues.

    2. C stays while E falls

      C major changes to C minor: E♭ replaces E♮ above the same home note. The orchestra plays the opening phrase loudly in unison, then quiet strings supply a shortened answer. Haydn cuts together the beginning and ending of the original tune.

    3. The violins leave room for an oboe

      A falling first-violin figure leads out of the minor variation. The next statement restores C major and places the tune in the first oboe above strings. Flute and oboe later add their own parts over the melody in the violins.

    4. The orchestra takes over

      The wind-led variation closes in C major. The fourth begins in the same key and tempo, now fortissimo for the full orchestra, with syncopated accompaniment. Trumpets and timpani join the strings and winds for the tune’s march-like opening.

    5. A held C, changing chords

      After the final variation, the coda recalls the tune in the winds. The strings hold seventh and ninth chords beneath it while C remains in the bass. The melody keeps its simple outline; the sustained chords change the sound of its familiar notes.

  3. III. Menuetto: Allegro moltoBoston Symphony Orchestra, Serge Koussevitzky · 1929
    3:50

    Haydn marks this minuet Allegro molto, the same speed-word he gives the finale. Between its boisterous dances, the first violins and bassoon share a quiet Trio.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A dance in big steps

      Flute, bassoon and first violins lead the minuet’s opening tune, leaping through G major in sturdy crotchets. The marking is Allegro molto. This dance moves fast.

    2. A scale turned upside down

      The minuet and Trio share a family resemblance: the rising quaver figure that closes the minuet’s first half returns upside down in the Trio. The quieter dance grows from a scrap of the louder one.

    3. Bassoon beside the violins

      The Trio stays in G major but sends most of the winds away. The first violins and a bassoon share the tune above the other strings, all marked piano. Its opening phrases fall into two balanced groups of four bars.

    4. The big dance returns

      Da capo: back to the minuet’s beginning. The flute rejoins the bassoon and violins, the full orchestra plays forte, and those large, quick steps return.

    How it is joined

    1. G major, now for fewer players

      The minuet closes over a held G in the bass. The Trio keeps G major but reduces the orchestra to strings and bassoon, marked piano. Its falling quaver figure reverses a rising figure from the minuet, carrying a small piece of the dance across the change of players.

    2. The orchestra takes back the dance

      The Trio’s G major close is followed by the instruction Menuetto da capo. The opening returns in the same key, with the full orchestra at forte. Flute, bassoon and first violins lead the tune together again.

  4. IV. Finale: Allegro moltoBoston Symphony Orchestra, Serge Koussevitzky · 1929
    3:31

    Haydn starts his finale with a quiet string tune and keeps finding trouble for it: a false return, a minor-key outburst and a timpani roll that gets ahead of the orchestra. The tune survives them all.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A tune gathering friends

      The strings begin the dancing tune quietly on their own. A flute joins its repeat; later, flute and bassoon join the strings together. By the time the full orchestra breaks in, the tune has made several rounds.

    2. A tune over D

      The second tune is quiet too, with the strings again in front. The bass repeats D while the melody rocks between D major and A major. Haydn keeps that one bass note busy for almost the whole tune.

    3. Back at the beginning already

      The opening tune comes back in G major, now with bassoon. It sounds ready to start the exposition again. Instead, Haydn takes its closing turn down into the bass and sends it through a rapid succession of keys.

    4. Home, with some shortcuts

      The return in G major skips the first tune’s repeated opening and starts with its contrasting phrase. Haydn also cuts the passage between the main tunes. The second tune comes back in the home key, and the coda follows close behind.

    5. The drummer gets there first

      The coda starts quietly with the tune’s upbeat. A timpani roll turns forte a bar before the other instruments, and the orchestra swerves into E♭ major. Haydn still has one more unexpected key in reserve.

    How it is joined

    1. The tune seems to start again

      The exposition ends with three loud D chords. Repeated C notes then turn the harmony towards G major, and the opening tune returns with bassoon added to the strings. The apparent restart soon wanders off: the tune’s closing figure moves into the bass.

    2. Back through D to a shortened return

      The development travels through E♭, F and B♭ major, then reaches D major. The first violins’ semiquavers trail off before G major returns. Haydn begins the recapitulation with the first tune’s contrasting phrase, cutting its repeated opening.

    3. A hush, a roll, and E♭

      The second tune in G major leads straight into a quiet coda built on the opening upbeat. A timpani roll grows loud ahead of the orchestra, announcing a turn to E♭ major. The first tune returns forte in that new key before the final G major chords.
