Franz Schubert / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 8 “Unfinished”, D. 759

1822 · Orchestra

2 recordings · 22 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro moderatoPhiladelphia Orchestra, Leopold Stokowski · 28 and 30 April 1927
    10:28

    Schubert’s Unfinished begins with cellos and basses alone, then oboe and clarinet play the melody over the violins. Leopold Stokowski conducts the Philadelphia Orchestra in this 1927 recording of the first movement.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Cellos and basses alone

      The symphony opens with eight bars for cellos and basses, pianissimo, playing a phrase in B minor. The violins then enter, and oboe and clarinet play together above them.

    2. The score in Graz, 1865

      Schubert’s fair copy of these two movements passed to the brothers Josef and Anselm Hüttenbrenner. In 1860 Josef told the conductor Johann Herbeck that Anselm owned a “treasure”. Herbeck called on Anselm in Graz in May 1865, and an overture by Anselm shared the première programme.

    3. Second subject, cellos, G major

      After a short bridge led by the horns, the cellos begin a long melody in G major. The violins take it up over a syncopated accompaniment.

    4. Tutti chords in the closing group

      Heavy chords from the full orchestra interrupt the melody in G major. Schubert leaves silences between the chords. Fragments of the tune then pass through the closing passage.

    5. Symphony set aside, 1822

      Schubert set the symphony aside in 1822 for a commissioned piece and never returned to it. The reason is unknown. One theory holds that he found no finale to match the opening; others cite illness, lack of time and no prospect of a performance.

    6. Development in E minor

      The development begins in E minor with the phrase that opened the symphony. The low strings play it first, and the higher strings take it up.

    7. Development climax, trombones

      At the climax of the development the trombones play a variant of the opening phrase, first heard in the lowest strings, above the orchestra. The music stays in E minor through the long climax.

    8. First performance, Vienna, 1865

      On 17 December 1865 Johann von Herbeck conducted the first performance in Vienna, forty-three years after Schubert began the score. He followed its two completed movements with the finale of Schubert’s Third Symphony.

    9. Recapitulation, oboe and clarinet

      Oboe and clarinet return over the violins after the trombones. Schubert brings back their melody in B minor and leaves out the eight-bar introduction.

    10. Second subject, recapitulation

      In the recapitulation the cello melody returns in D major, a fourth below its first appearance in G major.

    11. Stokowski’s second recording, 1927

      Stokowski and the Philadelphians recorded this symphony acoustically in 1924. They recorded it again in April 1927 using the electrical process, which, in Pristine’s words, conveys “the burnished sheen of the strings” in a swiftly flowing reading.

    12. Coda in B minor

      The recapitulation closes in B major. The coda returns to B minor and is built on the introductory theme, first heard in the low strings, and the movement ends with loud chords for the full orchestra.

    13. Opening bass phrase, third appearance

      Wolfgang Stähr wrote that on its third appearance the basses’ opening phrase sinks into the abyss and sets off howling and groaning, “as if graves were opening”.

    Joins

    1. A B pedal, then E minor

      The exposition ends with B held in the second bassoon and first horn beneath an F♯-major chord. The development then begins softly in E minor. Cellos and basses play the opening phrase again, and the higher strings take it up.

    2. The violin murmur returns

      The orchestral outburst dies away into soft phrases for flute and oboe. Oboe and clarinet then play the B-minor melody again over murmuring violins and plucked lower strings. The eight-bar introduction is omitted.

    3. B major to B minor at the coda

      The recapitulation closes in B major. At the coda the key changes to B minor, and the opening phrase returns in the low strings. From there the coda builds to the orchestra’s closing chords.

  2. II. Andante con motoPhiladelphia Orchestra, Leopold Stokowski · 28 and 30 April 1927
    11:55

    The Andante con moto, the second movement of the Unfinished Symphony, D. 759, opens in E major with horns, bassoons and pizzicato basses. The opening material is interrupted several times by a minor-key woodwind melody, each time followed by the full orchestra.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening phrase, horns and bassoons

      Horns and bassoons play a two-bar phrase over pizzicato double basses. The strings follow with a long melody in E major. The opening two bars return at the end of the movement.

    2. Wolfgang Stähr on the Andante

      Wolfgang Stähr heard this Andante as a leave-taking without return. He pointed to the horn fifths, the bass lines, the string melodies with their cadences, and clarinet and oboe solos that “sound like farewells from songs of travel”.

    3. Hanslick’s review, Vienna, 1865

      Reviewing the Vienna première in 1865, Eduard Hanslick heard “musical thunder clouds” crossing the Andante. He wrote of how much Schubert achieved with a few horn figures and a clarinet or oboe solo.

    4. Second subject, clarinet and oboe

      The first violins play four bare notes outlining C♯ minor. The clarinet begins the second melody in that key, and the oboe continues it in C♯ major.

    5. Piano short score

      Schubert drafted the symphony first as a piano short score. The draft of this movement survives complete, with the end of the first movement and a scherzo that stops partway through its trio. After his death it passed to his brother Ferdinand; no draft of a finale has been found.

    6. Full orchestra in C♯ minor

      The full orchestra takes over in C♯ minor. Its closing passage reaches D♭ major, the same sounding tonic as C♯ major but written with flats. A short bridge leads back to E major.

    7. Return of the opening theme

      The opening theme returns in E major after the full orchestra has played the closing theme. The strings play long phrases, and the winds reply.

    8. Victor release on three discs

      Victor issued Stokowski’s recording of the symphony on three discs, 6663 to 6665, gathered in an album. Six matrices, recorded on 28 and 30 April 1927, supplied the six record sides for the two movements.

    9. Second subject in A minor, oboe

      The second subject returns in A minor, a major third lower than its earlier C♯ minor. This time the oboe begins and the clarinet continues, which reverses their earlier roles.

    10. Coda on the opening figure

      The two-bar phrase that opened the movement becomes the first melody of the coda. Schubert extends it at length before the final return to E major.

    11. Coda, woodwinds in A♭ major

      In the coda the woodwinds play the first theme in A♭ major, a key far removed from E. The short violin transition figure follows, and the movement ends in E major.

    12. Grove’s proposed finale

      A finale in B minor would normally follow. Soon after Herbeck found the score, George Grove suspected that the long entr’acte in B minor that Schubert wrote for Rosamunde in 1823 was meant for it. Brian Newbould and others have since argued the case.

    13. Scherzo fragment in the manuscript

      The full score ends with this close in E major. On the back of its last page the manuscript continues into a scherzo in B minor, orchestrated for only a few bars before it breaks off. The title page is dated 30 October 1822, and Schubert lived another six years.

    Joins

    1. Four unaccompanied violin notes

      After the E-major first theme, the first violins play four unaccompanied notes that outline C♯ minor. The clarinet then takes up the new melody in that key, and the oboe continues it in C♯ major.

    2. Return to E major after the closing theme

      The full orchestra plays the closing theme, which ends in D♭ major. A short transition brings the music back to E major. The strings then take up the opening melody again, and the winds reply to them.

    3. The oboe takes the clarinet’s melody

      The second melody returns in A minor, a major third below its first appearance. The oboe plays the opening phrase and the clarinet continues, the reverse of their earlier order.

    4. Opening cadence extended into the coda

      The full-orchestra closing passage dies down. The two-bar cadential figure in E major from the opening is extended into a new melody for the coda. The violin motto, heard later, takes this melody on towards the woodwinds’ A♭-major restatement.
