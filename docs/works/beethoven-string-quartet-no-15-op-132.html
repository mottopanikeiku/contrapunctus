Ludwig van Beethoven / II. Classical

String Quartet No. 15, Op. 132

1825 · Two violins, viola and cello

18 min

The recording

  1. III. Molto adagio — Heiliger DankgesangThe Griller String Quartet: Sidney Griller and Jack O’Brien, violins; Philip Burton, viola; Colin Hampton, cello
    18:13

    After serious illness in 1825, Beethoven writes a “holy song of thanksgiving” for four string players. The Griller Quartet plays its long hymn and the two D-major passages where he writes that he feels new strength.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Four players begin a hymn

      A short phrase passes from the first violin down through the quartet. Then the players settle into long, hymn-like chords. Beethoven calls this music a “holy song of thanksgiving of a convalescent to the Deity, in the Lydian mode”.

    2. The hymn’s B natural

      The hymn rests on F, but its scale has B♮ where F major has B♭. That extra half-step opens a wide space above the home note. Beethoven’s Lydian mode uses the seven white notes of a keyboard, with F at their centre.

    3. A spring spent ill

      In the spring of 1825, serious illness interrupts Beethoven’s work on this quartet. He returns to it during his recovery and completes it in July. His own words above the slow movement make the thanksgiving personal.

    4. Feeling new strength

      The strings step into D major and a quicker three-beat gait. A first-violin trill gives way to little runs; the cello lifts the tune with short upward figures. Beethoven writes Neue Kraft fühlend — feeling new strength.

    5. The hymn has companions

      The slow hymn returns, with longer notes in the first violin and moving lines below. The other players curl around the melody, sometimes answering it, sometimes carrying on while it holds a note. F and its natural-note scale are home again.

    6. A prince asks for quartets

      In November 1822 Prince Nikolai Galitzin, an amateur cellist in St Petersburg, asks Beethoven for “one, two or three” quartets, and Beethoven names a fee of 50 ducats each. Galitzin goes bankrupt in 1826; his son settles the debt with Beethoven’s nephew Karl in 1835 and 1852.

    7. A copyist gets a headache

      Before the quartet can be played, its parts need copying. Cellist Joseph Linke tackles the manuscript, but the effort gives him a headache. Violinist Karl Holz takes over the last movement. Beethoven checks and corrects the parts himself.

    8. The prince reads about it

      On 14 January 1826 Galitzin writes that he has just read in a Leipzig journal that the new A minor quartet has been played in Vienna. He is so impatient, he says, that he begs Beethoven to send it by post “without further delay”.

    9. The violin adds flourishes

      D major and the Andante return. The first violin dresses the tune in runs and trills, while the lower strings keep its springing accompaniment. The change from long hymn notes to these small, nimble figures feels almost physical.

    10. Published in Paris and Berlin

      Schlesinger buys this quartet and the F major, Op. 135, for his Paris “Collection complète”. They are the only Beethoven quartets first published outside the German-speaking lands; Schlesinger’s father issues a parallel Berlin edition.

    11. With the deepest feeling

      Mit innigster Empfindung — with the deepest feeling — appears beside each player’s returning phrase. The final hymn begins with one voice answering another. Its long notes and curling figures pass among all four instruments; there is no further dance.

    12. A rehearsal, then the première

      On 8 September 1825 Karl Holz writes to Beethoven: “Yesterday was the rehearsal of the new quartet; Carl was there too: we were delighted.” At the first performance next day, George Smart notes, Beethoven himself conducts the players.

    13. Linke played better than ever

      After the concert of 6 November 1825 Beethoven’s nephew Karl reports that the hall was “infinitely full”, and that “it went very well together, and Linke played better than ever”.

    14. “Our musical Jean Paul”

      Reviewing that concert, Leipzig’s Allgemeine musikalische Zeitung says that what “our musical Jean Paul” gives here is “great, splendid, unusual, surprising and original”, and must be studied as well as often heard. It finds a monotony in the very long Adagio.

    15. Panting behind Beethoven

      A Vienna paper of 24 November 1825 writes that Beethoven “hurries far ahead of his time” while the understanding of his listeners and critics “still pants laboriously behind”. Its reviewer admits that he too is one of the panting.

    16. Fourteen guests at an inn

      On 9 September 1825, fourteen invited guests hear the quartet at Vienna’s Zum Wilden Mann inn. Two days later it is played again, with the visiting British musician George Smart among the listeners.

    17. The last F chord

      The hymn approaches its final F-major chord. Moving phrases give way to held notes, and Beethoven asks for pianissimo. The first violin rests high above the cello’s F; all four players hold the ending under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. F gives way to D major

      The hymn closes on F, with the first violin holding its upper note under a fermata. Neue Kraft fühlend introduces a D-major Andante in 3/8. F♯ and C♯ enter the signature; the first violin trills while the cello starts a short rising figure. Long chords give way to a buoyant dance.

    2. The sharps disappear from the page

      The Andante’s violin figures grow quieter above a descending cello line. Beethoven cancels the two-sharp signature and restores common time at Molto adagio. The F-Lydian hymn returns in long first-violin notes, with the lower strings moving more freely beneath it.

    3. The dance comes back decorated

      The second hymn closes over F in the cello. The bass moves to D, and the two-sharp signature and 3/8 return with Andante. The first violin leaps into the upper register; its tune now carries quicker flourishes above the lower strings’ rising accompaniment.

    4. One voice answers another

      The second Andante subsides, and Molto adagio restores common time and F Lydian. Second violin, viola and cello enter in turn before the first violin joins their moving phrases. Beethoven writes Mit innigster Empfindung beside each entry. The hymn’s long notes gradually emerge among those lines.
