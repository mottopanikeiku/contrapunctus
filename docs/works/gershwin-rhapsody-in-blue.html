George Gershwin / VI. Modernism

Rhapsody in Blue

1924 · Piano and jazz band; orchestration by Ferde Grofé

A clarinet’s sliding ascent opens a succession of piano solos, band exchanges and broad tunes. Later Grofé scorings enlarge the ensemble.

2 recordings · 27 min

In the Hall

  1. 1924 abridged recording — both original sidesGeorge Gershwin, piano; Paul Whiteman Concert Orchestra; Paul Whiteman, conductor · 10 June 1924, New York
    9:06

    Gershwin plays the piano in the first recording of his Rhapsody, with Paul Whiteman’s band and Ross Gorman’s sliding clarinet. Both sides of the 1924 record are heard here: its nine-minute version cuts about a third of the score.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Gorman’s sliding clarinet

      Ross Gorman’s clarinet rises from a low trill to a high B♭ in one long smear. Gershwin originally writes a scale; Gorman stretches it into a glissando during rehearsal, and Gershwin keeps it. The clarinet’s bends and slides belong to this band’s way of playing.

    2. An afternoon at Aeolian Hall

      On 12 February 1924, Gershwin sits at the piano in New York’s Aeolian Hall while Whiteman’s band plays his new Rhapsody. The concert is called “An Experiment in Modern Music”. Four months later they make this record.

    3. Trains and dancing feet

      The faster stretch brings together repeated train-like rhythms and stride piano, with its jumps between bass notes and chords. To fit the two sides, the record drops the ‘Shuffle’ tune and the third cadenza: after the piano’s stride solo it moves almost straight to the slow melody.

    4. Grofé knows the band

      Ferde Grofé turns Gershwin’s two-piano draft into parts for Whiteman’s players, taking pages as Gershwin finishes them. He knows their instruments and their individual strengths. The clarinet and trumpet solos are written for a band whose players also make dance records.

    5. A long E-major tune

      The slow section opens out into a long melody in E major. The band can now sustain a tune across whole phrases; the piano later takes it into its own hands before the closing rush of returning themes.

    6. Two sides, one dollar fifty

      Victor advertises the two-sided Rhapsody for $1.50 in the Chicago Tribune on 5 September 1924. The record holds about nine minutes: roughly a third of the score is cut to fit the two sides.

  2. 1924 jazz-band orchestrationBramwell Tovey, piano; United States Marine Band · 10 July 2018, The Music Center at Strathmore, North Bethesda, Maryland
    18:10

    Bramwell Tovey plays the Rhapsody with the United States Marine Band at its 220th-anniversary gala. Grofé’s original jazz-band scoring puts reeds, brass and banjo around the piano, with room for the long solo passages cut from Gershwin’s first record.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A clarinet bends the scale

      A low trill swells into the clarinet’s long slide up to B♭. The tune that follows keeps bending between major and minor. Piano cadenzas will interrupt the band, stretch the phrases, and send them off in another direction.

    2. Gorman’s rehearsal slide

      In Gershwin’s own writing the opening was a plain rising run. At a rehearsal Whiteman’s clarinettist, Ross Gorman, stretched it into the slide that now opens the piece, to Gershwin’s delight.

    3. A concerto he had forgotten

      On 4 January 1924 a small item in the New York Tribune announces that George Gershwin is writing a “jazz concerto” for Paul Whiteman’s concert on 12 February. By legend his brother Ira shows it to him over a game of pool: Whiteman had asked months before, and Gershwin had forgotten.

    4. A birthday at Strathmore

      At the Music Center at Strathmore on 10 July 2018, the Marine Band celebrates its 220th anniversary. Bramwell Tovey is the evening’s guest conductor, composer and piano soloist; for the Rhapsody he takes the keyboard.

    5. The train theme

      The faster music turns on a short, repeated figure: the train theme. Stride piano joins the dance, with the left hand springing between bass notes and chords. Band passages and piano solos keep exchanging these tunes.

    6. The Tribune’s verdict

      The morning after the première, Lawrence Gilman of the Tribune finds “how trite, feeble and conventional the tunes are”. Yet he praises “the rich inventiveness of the rhythms, the saliency and vividness of the orchestral color”.

    7. Grofé’s first scoring

      This performance uses Grofé’s 1924 jazz-band version. Its reeds, brass, banjo and violins were chosen for Whiteman’s particular players. The familiar symphonic orchestration, with a larger orchestra, follows in 1942.

    8. Two pianos and a page at a time

      Gershwin writes the Rhapsody as a two-piano score: one piano for the soloist, the other standing for the band. Ferde Grofé takes the pages away as they are finished and fills in parts for each of Whiteman’s players.

    9. Room for the piano

      Gershwin writes several cadenzas into the Rhapsody: stretches where the solo piano takes charge of the speed and the space between phrases. The tunes survive these interruptions, returning in new keys or in the band’s instruments.

    10. Bernstein on the paragraphs

      Thirty-one years after the première, Leonard Bernstein writes that “the themes are terrific, inspired, God-given”. But the Rhapsody is “a string of separate paragraphs stuck together”, and you can cut parts of it without affecting the whole.

    11. The long E-major melody

      After the piano’s wandering comes the broad E-major melody. The band holds its long phrases together; later, the piano takes the tune into a more decorated solo. The last stretch gathers earlier themes back around it.

    12. From Salvation Army bands

      Bramwell Tovey began in Salvation Army bands and studied at the Royal Academy of Music. A jazz and classical pianist and a tuba player, he led the Vancouver Symphony for eighteen years from 2000 and became principal conductor of the BBC Concert Orchestra in January 2018. He died in 2022.

    13. Beyond the first disc

      Gershwin’s own 1924 record lasts about nine minutes. Roughly a third of the score is cut to fit one two-sided disc, including the ‘Shuffle’ tune, the third cadenza and most of the grand cadenza. A concert performance has room to let those passages unfold.

    14. The record made again in 1927

      Three years after the first record, Victor brings Gershwin and Whiteman together again, this time electrically and with Grofé’s slightly larger scoring. Gershwin is unhappy with some of Whiteman’s conducting, and Nathaniel Shilkret conducts. Side one takes nine takes, side two six.

    15. The opening tune comes home

      The closing pages bring back the opening tune and settle in B♭ major. Piano and band compress their exchanges into the final flourish.
