Frédéric Chopin / III. Romanticism

Nocturne, Op. 27 No. 2

1835 · Piano

6 min

The recording

  1. Lento sostenutoFrank Lévy · c. 2014
    6:18

    Frank Lévy plays a nocturne that keeps turning a solo song into a duet. A stream of left-hand semiquavers runs beneath three statements of the D♭-major melody, each with something new to say.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The left hand alone

      The left hand opens the nocturne alone, spreading D♭ major across the keyboard in groups of six semiquavers. In the next bar the right hand begins on F, marked dolce. Its first phrase floats over a bass that keeps returning to D♭.

    2. A second singer

      The single melody becomes a duet in thirds. B♭ enters the bass, turning the harmony towards the relative minor. The right hand sings espressivo, while the left keeps up its long sweeps of semiquavers.

    3. From Geneva to New York

      Lévy enters the Geneva Conservatory at fifteen, later studies with Maria Tipo and Leon Fleisher, and spends thirteen years teaching in Juilliard’s Pre-College Division. In 2014 he joins Musopen’s project to record Chopin’s complete works.

    4. The opening F returns

      After the wandering harmonies, F returns above a deep D♭. The first melody starts again, a tempo and dolce. Its long notes soon gather more ornaments, including a feather-light run marked leggierissimo.

    5. The duet changes spelling

      D♭ and F become C♯ and E in the right hand. The bass keeps the same sounding C♯, but an A♮ in the accompaniment changes the chord to A major. The duet has stepped into a new room without moving its floor.

    6. The third verse

      The opening melody comes home again over D♭. This time its continuation swells into a long written-out flourish: a stream of small notes climbs high, then tumbles back down through the right hand.

    7. D♭ holds the floor

      The bass settles on D♭ and keeps renewing it. Above, the right hand makes a descending chain of little sighs, each beginning high and falling. Chopin marks the passage dolcissimo, very sweetly.

    8. Mendelssohn learns it by ear

      In September 1835 Chopin plays this nocturne at Friedrich Wieck’s house in Leipzig. Mendelssohn is there, and writes to his sister Fanny about the “lovely new notturno”, a considerable part of which he has learnt by ear.

    9. Sixths climb into the treble

      Two notes at a time climb in sixths through two groups of seven. The marking is dolcissimo e diminuendo: very sweetly, growing softer. The hands finally come to rest on D♭ major.

    How it is joined

    1. B♭ beneath two singing voices

      The D♭-major phrase comes to rest on F. The bass moves to B♭, and the right hand adds D♭ below that F: the new phrase starts in B♭ minor, with two voices singing in thirds. The left hand keeps the same six-note sweep beneath them. Chopin marks the duet espressivo.

    2. A♭ resolves to D♭

      The bass reaches A♭, with C and G♭ making the dominant seventh of D♭. The right hand climbs towards F as that chord resolves onto a low D♭. The opening melody returns, marked a tempo and dolce; the left-hand semiquavers continue without a break.

    3. F falls to E

      The duet’s D♭–F pair changes to C♯–E. D♭ and C♯ are the same piano key, so the bass holds its sounding pitch while F falls a semitone. A♮ enters the left-hand arpeggio, making an A-major chord over C♯. The paired melody continues above the familiar semiquavers.

    4. A falling run finds F

      A right-hand run falls across the keyboard while the left hand reaches A♭ dominant-seventh harmony. The bass then drops to D♭, and F begins the familiar melody for the third time. Dolce replaces the forceful accents of the preceding passage.

    5. The bass stays on D♭

      A♭ dominant harmony resolves onto D♭ in the bass. The right hand answers with a high D♭ falling to G, then continues down in similar pairs, marked dolcissimo. Each changing chord retains D♭ underneath. The left hand still moves, but its lowest note stays put.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. Nocturne in D♭ major, Op. 27 No. 2Vladimir de Pachmann, piano (his own Baldwin) · 26 June 1925, Hayes
    4:36

    Pachmann plays the D♭-major Nocturne on his own Baldwin piano at Hayes in June 1925. The critic reviewing the disc that December rejoices in its phrasing — and in the famous pianist’s silence between the notes.

    Notes as it plays
    1. The piano begins alone

      For one bar the left hand has the whole piano to itself, spreading D♭ major into groups of six semiquavers. Then F begins the melody. Chopin asks for a slow, sustained song above the uninterrupted accompaniment.

    2. Two voices on one keyboard

      The right hand doubles its song in thirds as the bass moves to B♭. The new phrase is marked espressivo. Little rests and ornaments make the two voices catch their breath together.

    3. “What a fine builder”

      The critic signing K.K. in The Gramophone, December 1925, greets this record with relief: “he does not utter a word the whole time”. Then comes the praise: “What a fine builder he is!” Pachmann’s phrasing survives the critic’s complaints about the recorded piano.

    4. His own Baldwin

      Pachmann brings his own Baldwin to the Hayes session on 26 June 1925. The new electrical recording captures enough of the piano to trouble the December reviewer: some notes sound splendid, others clang. The critic nevertheless praises the pianist’s spirit.

    5. D♭ and the first tune

      A falling run brings back the low D♭ and the opening melody. The right hand starts its third statement with the same F. Before long, that tune flowers into a long cascade of small notes.

    6. A bass that stays

      The bass settles on D♭. The chords above it keep changing while pairs of notes in the right hand descend, marked dolcissimo. The left hand’s semiquavers still sweep across the keyboard.

    7. Sixths into the distance

      The right hand climbs in sixths, two ribbons of notes rising together. Dolcissimo e diminuendo: very sweetly and growing softer. After the climb, the last D♭-major chord sits quietly beneath a pause.
