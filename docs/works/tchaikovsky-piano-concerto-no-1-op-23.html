Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky / III. Romanticism

Piano Concerto No. 1, Op. 23

1874–75 · Op. 23 · Piano and orchestra

Hans von Bülow gave the première in Boston in 1875, after Nikolai Rubinstein had rejected the piece. The famous D♭ major opening tune disappears once the introduction is over.

3 recordings · 31 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro non troppo e molto maestoso — Allegro con spiritoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    18:47

    In the first movement of Tchaikovsky’s Piano Concerto No. 1 in B♭ minor, Op. 23, the piano opens as accompanist, playing huge chords beneath the strings’ D♭-major melody. That famous tune appears only in the introduction, and the rest of the movement is built from other melodies.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening theme, strings and piano

      The horns play the opening. The strings then take the D♭-major melody while the piano plays large chords beneath it, so the soloist enters as an accompanist.

    2. Letter to Anatoly Tchaikovsky, 1874

      In a letter of 21 November/3 December 1874, Tchaikovsky told his brother Anatoly that he was immersed in a piano concerto. He wrote that he had to “compel piano passages to come into my head”.

    3. Allegro con spirito, first theme

      The Allegro con spirito begins in B♭ minor with a short Ukrainian tune in clipped phrases, replacing the long string melody of the introduction. The piano and orchestra exchange the tune in quick alternation.

    4. Letter to Nadezhda von Meck, 1879

      The Allegro’s tune came from a blind street singer whom Tchaikovsky heard in Ukraine. In a letter to Nadezhda von Meck in May 1879, he recalled the singer’s accompanying instrument and said he had used part of the melody in this concerto.

    5. Three versions of the concerto

      The concerto exists in three versions: Tchaikovsky’s original of 1875, a revised score printed in 1879 and a third edition of 1889. The 1889 text is the one usually played today.

    6. Play-through for Rubinstein, 1875

      On 5 January 1875 Tchaikovsky played the concerto to Nikolay Rubinstein and Nikolay Hubert. Rubinstein condemned it and asked for changes, which Tchaikovsky refused to make. Hans von Bülow later took the new work to Boston.

    7. Development section

      The development takes the earlier themes through new keys. Piano octaves cut across the orchestra, and short fragments of the first subject break into the more lyrical phrases. The melody of the introduction does not return in this section.

    8. Dedication to Hans von Bülow, 1875

      The autograph score was first dedicated to Sergey Taneyev. His name was struck out and Hans von Bülow’s written in its place. In June 1875 Bülow thanked Tchaikovsky for the dedication and wrote of the concerto’s “such unsurpassed originality, such nobility, such strength”.

    9. Recapitulation of the first subject

      The Allegro’s first tune returns in shortened form. Tchaikovsky then brings back the wind melody and rebuilds the earlier climax, this time towards B♭ major.

    10. First performance, Boston, 1875

      Bülow gave the première on 25 October 1875 at the Music Hall in Boston, with Benjamin Johnson Lang conducting. The audience received it enthusiastically, and he played it again five days later. Within weeks it had been heard in New York, Saint Petersburg and Moscow.

    11. Cadenza

      The orchestra stops for the long cadenza. The pianist brings earlier melodies into the solo writing and later recalls the wind theme quietly in B♭ minor.

    12. Nikolay Rubinstein’s performances, 1878

      Nikolay Rubinstein, who had sharply criticised the concerto in January 1875, played it in Moscow on 22 March 1878 and again in Paris that September, at the Trocadéro. In 1880 Tchaikovsky dedicated his Second Piano Concerto to him for his “magnificent” playing of the First.

    13. Return of the second-group melody

      After the cadenza the orchestra returns with the broad melody from the second group, now in B♭ major.

    14. Coda in B♭ major

      Piano and orchestra take up themes from the Allegro in a short, vigorous coda. The movement ends in B♭ major with a plagal cadence, the chord of E♭ major moving to B♭.

    Joins

    1. From D♭ major to B♭ minor

      The D♭-major introduction grows quieter, and the Allegro con spirito begins in B♭ minor. After the strings’ broad tune, the piano takes up a short Ukrainian melody in clipped phrases and quick exchanges.

    2. Woodwind phrases open the second group

      The second group alternates a woodwind theme with a smoother string melody in A♭ major. When the wind tune returns, piano arpeggios accompany it and carry its shorter phrases into a larger orchestral build.

    3. Closing theme opens the development

      The exposition closes in A♭ major. In the development its lyrical closing theme becomes a rising sequence, interrupted by fragments of the first subject. Piano octaves alternate with orchestral replies, and the themes move away from their earlier keys.

    4. The Allegro’s first theme returns, shortened

      Winds and strings bring back the Allegro’s first theme. Tchaikovsky shortened its return and gave the piano octave writing on the way to the second group. The later orchestral climax turns towards B♭ major.

    5. Deceptive cadence into the solo cadenza

      A deceptive cadence cuts off the B♭-major climax and turns away from the expected home chord. The orchestra stops, and the pianist plays the long cadenza alone. Later, fragments of the wind theme return quietly in B♭ minor.

    6. B♭ major orchestral re-entry after cadenza

      The orchestra enters after the cadenza with the broad second-group melody in B♭ major. Sustained orchestral notes rejoin the piano, and the returning tune begins the approach to the coda.

    7. Coda and close in B♭ major

      Piano and orchestra gather speed and force on the Allegro’s first and second themes. The short coda ends in B♭ major on a plagal cadence, with E♭ major resolving to B♭.

  2. II. Andantino semplice — PrestissimoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    6:28

    This is the second movement of Tchaikovsky’s Piano Concerto No. 1 in B♭ minor, Op. 23, in D♭ major; the flute plays the theme over plucked strings, and the piano follows. The fast central section is built on a French song that Tchaikovsky’s brothers used to sing.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Flute and plucked strings

      Over pizzicato strings, the flute plays the D♭-major melody in bars 5–12. The piano takes over the tune in bars 13–20.

    2. Two-piano arrangement, January 1875

      Tchaikovsky had completed the concerto’s two-piano arrangement by 2 January 1875. Three days later he played the work to Nikolay Rubinstein and Nikolay Hubert. He finished the orchestration on 21 February.

    3. Prestissimo middle section

      The Prestissimo, a short scherzo in the middle of the slow movement, opens in F major with fast piano writing. The strings then take up a new tune in D major. The piano accompanies them.

    4. French song in the fast middle section

      The fast middle section borrows Il faut s’amuser, danser et rire (“one must have fun, dance and laugh”), a tune by Jean-Baptiste-Edouard Montaubry. Modest Tchaikovsky recalled singing it constantly with his brother Anatoly in the early 1870s. Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky put the refrain into his Piano Concerto No. 1.

    5. Reprise of the opening melody

      After the fast dance, the opening melody returns in D♭ major. The piano plays it with small ornaments, and the oboe continues it.

    6. Coda in D♭ major

      The coda is in D♭ major. Its last cadence is plagal, moving from G♭ major to D♭, and is marked ppp.

    Joins

    1. From Andantino to Prestissimo in F major

      At the end of the Andantino the piano holds a high F-major chord, pianissimo. The Prestissimo begins in the same key with fast solo writing for the piano. The strings then play the French song in D major, and the piano accompanies them.

    2. The piano returns to D♭

      A cadenza-like piano passage in bars 135–145 ends the fast episode. At bar 146 the soloist plays the opening D♭-major melody again at the slower tempo and adds ornaments to the line the flute played at the start.

    3. Oboe continuation and plagal close

      The oboe continues the returning melody and resolves it in D♭ major. A brief coda follows and ends on a G♭-major chord moving to D♭, marked ppp.

  3. III. Allegro con fuocoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    6:10

    The finale opens with an accented Ukrainian song. A broad violin melody repeatedly interrupts the dance, then returns in B♭ major at the close of the concerto.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Piano entry, Ukrainian song

      After a brief orchestral opening, the piano enters with a Ukrainian song, Go on, go on Ivan. The B♭-minor dance theme is built from short phrases with displaced accents.

    2. Second theme in D♭ major

      The violins introduce a broad second theme in D♭ major, and the piano then takes it up. Its long notes follow the short phrases of the dance theme. The melody returns in B♭ major near the end of the movement.

    3. Folk-song source, 1869

      In 1869 Tchaikovsky arranged the Russian folk song “I’m Coming to the Capital” for piano duet as No. 30 of his Fifty Russian Folksongs. A variant of its tune is thought to be the source of the finale’s broad violin melody.

    4. Second theme in E♭ major

      The broad melody returns in E♭ major, a tone above its first appearance. Descending scales lead back to the piano’s B♭-minor dance.

    5. Letter to Aleksandr Ziloti, 1889

      In January 1889 Tchaikovsky told the pianist Aleksandr Ziloti that he had altered a passage in this finale, “der verfluchte Stelle”, the accursed place. “I think it will be shorter and better,” he wrote. He had composed the concerto fourteen years earlier.

    6. Octave passage before B♭ major

      The piano plays rapid octaves across the keyboard during the long preparation for B♭ major. The broad tune does not return until this passage ends.

    7. Letter from Bülow, January 1876

      On 13 January 1876 Bülow replied to Tchaikovsky’s thoughts of revising the concerto that “no changes are necessary,” apart from some extra piano notes in a few tutti, which he had already added himself. His one comment on the finale concerned the tempo of the “triumphal 2nd motif” before the last stretta.

    8. Second theme in B♭ major

      The broad melody returns in B♭ major, played by piano and orchestra together. This violin tune first interrupted the dance, and it now carries the concerto’s closing pages.

    9. Coda, Allegro vivo

      The marking changes to Allegro vivo. Rapid piano figures lead into the final B♭-major chords, and the concerto closes on a dominant–tonic cadence.

    Joins

    1. Violins enter with the D♭-major melody

      The transition passes through F major. The violins then begin their broad melody in D♭ major. The piano takes up the tune after them in long notes, which replace the short accented figures of the refrain.

    2. Down the scales to B♭ minor

      Descending scales lead away from the D♭-major melody. The harmony moves through F major to B♭ minor, and the piano’s dance returns in a shortened form.

    3. The broad tune returns in E♭

      After the dance’s intervening episodes, the broad melody returns in E♭ major in bars 133–149. Its longer notes again stand apart from the piano’s quick writing. The tune now sits a tone above its first statement in D♭ major.

    4. The refrain returns after descending scales

      Descending scales lead the music from the E♭-major theme back towards B♭ minor. The piano plays the refrain again, then a longer succession of scales and arpeggios that leads into the coda.

    5. Approach to the octave passage

      The refrain’s cadence opens a long preparation for B♭ major. Scales and arpeggios in the piano lead into rapid octaves, and the broad tune’s final return is held back while the orchestra builds up behind the soloist.

    6. Dominant preparation to the B♭-major melody

      The long dominant preparation resolves into the broad melody in B♭ major. Piano and orchestra share the statement. The tune, first played quietly by the violins, is now taken up by the whole ensemble.

    7. Allegro vivo and final cadence

      The broad melody gives way to the Allegro vivo. The piano plays quick figures against emphatic orchestral chords, and the last bars close in B♭ major with a perfect cadence from F to B♭.
