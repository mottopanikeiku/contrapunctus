Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky / III. Romanticism

Piano Concerto No. 1, Op. 23

1874–75 · Op. 23 · Piano and orchestra

Hans von Bülow gave the première in Boston in 1875, after Nikolai Rubinstein had rejected the piece. The famous D♭ major opening tune disappears once the introduction is over.

3 recordings · 31 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro non troppo e molto maestoso — Allegro con spiritoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    18:47

    The piano begins by accompanying the strings, planting huge chords beneath their D♭-major melody. Tchaikovsky spends that famous tune in the introduction, then builds the rest of the movement from other songs.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The soloist accompanies

      Horns announce the opening. Then the strings sing the great D♭-major melody while the piano supplies enormous chords beneath it. The soloist makes a spectacular entrance as an accompanist.

    2. Piano passages by force

      In December 1874, Tchaikovsky tells his brother Anatoly that he is immersed in a piano concerto. The keyboard writing is resisting him: he has to “compel piano passages to come into my head”.

    3. A smaller, sharper tune

      The Allegro con spirito begins in B♭ minor with a short Ukrainian tune. Its clipped phrases replace the introduction’s long string melody; the piano turns the song into quick, insistent exchanges with the orchestra.

    4. A singer in Ukraine

      The Allegro’s tune comes from a blind street singer Tchaikovsky hears in Ukraine. In a letter to Nadezhda von Meck in May 1879, he remembers the singer’s accompanying instrument and says he uses part of the melody in this concerto.

    5. Three versions of the concerto

      The concerto exists in three versions: Tchaikovsky’s original of 1875, a revised score printed in 1879 and a third edition of 1889. The 1889 text is the one familiar today.

    6. Rubinstein says no

      On 5 January 1875, Tchaikovsky plays the concerto to Nikolay Rubinstein and Nikolay Hubert. Rubinstein condemns it and asks for changes. Tchaikovsky refuses. Hans von Bülow eventually takes the new work to Boston.

    7. Tunes caught in the octaves

      The development moves the earlier tunes through new keys. Piano octaves break across the orchestra; short pieces of the first subject interrupt the more lyrical phrases. The grand introductory melody stays outside this argument.

    8. Taneyev’s name struck out

      The autograph score is first dedicated to Sergey Taneyev; his name is struck out and Hans von Bülow’s written in its place. In June 1875 Bülow thanks Tchaikovsky for the dedication and finds in the concerto “such unsurpassed originality, such nobility, such strength”.

    9. The Ukrainian tune returns

      The Allegro’s first tune returns in shortened form. Tchaikovsky then revisits the wind melody and rebuilds the earlier climax, this time towards B♭ major.

    10. Boston, 1875

      On 25 October 1875 Bülow plays the première at Boston’s Music Hall, with Benjamin Johnson Lang conducting. The Boston audience receives it enthusiastically, and he repeats it five days later. Within weeks it is heard in New York, Saint Petersburg and Moscow.

    11. The piano has the room

      The orchestra stops for the long cadenza. The pianist brings earlier melodies into the solo writing, eventually recalling the wind theme quietly in B♭ minor.

    12. Rubinstein plays it himself

      Nikolay Rubinstein, who sharply criticised the concerto in January 1875, plays it in Moscow on 22 March 1878 and again in Paris that September, at the Trocadéro. In 1880 Tchaikovsky dedicates his Second Piano Concerto to him for his “magnificent” playing of the First.

    13. The orchestra answers softly

      The orchestra returns with the broad melody from the second group, now in B♭ major. After the pianist’s long solo, the tune has other players to breathe with again.

    14. A B♭-major finish

      Piano and orchestra bring the Allegro’s tunes into a short, vigorous coda. The movement ends in B♭ major, with a plagal cadence: the chord of E♭ major settles onto B♭.

    How it is joined

    1. D♭ major closes; B♭ minor begins

      The D♭-major introduction quietens before the Allegro con spirito begins in B♭ minor. The piano takes up a short Ukrainian melody, replacing the strings’ broad tune with clipped phrases and quick exchanges.

    2. Woodwind phrases open a second group

      The second group alternates a woodwind theme with a smoother string melody in A♭ major. Piano arpeggios accompany the wind tune when it returns, carrying its shorter phrases into a larger orchestral build.

    3. The closing tune begins the development

      The exposition closes in A♭ major. Its lyrical closing theme becomes a rising sequence in the development, interrupted by pieces of the first subject. Piano octaves and orchestral replies pull the familiar tunes away from their earlier keys.

    4. The Allegro tune returns in shortened form

      Winds and strings recover the Allegro’s first theme. Tchaikovsky shortens its return, then gives the piano octave writing on the way to the second group. The later orchestral climax turns towards B♭ major.

    5. A diverted cadence leaves the piano alone

      The B♭-major climax is cut short by a deceptive cadence, which turns away from the expected home chord. The orchestra stops, leaving the pianist alone for the long cadenza. Quiet fragments of the wind theme eventually return in B♭ minor.

    6. B♭ major brings the orchestra back

      The cadenza releases the broad second-group melody to the orchestra in B♭ major. Sustained orchestral notes rejoin the piano, and the returning tune begins the approach to the coda.

    7. The piano drives towards B♭ major

      Piano and orchestra gather speed and force around the Allegro’s first and second themes. The brief coda ends in B♭ major with a plagal cadence, E♭ major resolving to B♭.

  2. II. Andantino semplice — PrestissimoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    6:28

    A flute sings over plucked strings, and the piano answers as gently. Inside this D♭-major slow movement, Tchaikovsky hides a fast dance built around a French song his brothers used to sing.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Flute and plucked strings

      Plucked strings make a quiet space for the flute’s D♭-major melody. The piano takes the tune next. After the first movement’s huge opening chords, the soloist arrives here almost as another woodwind.

    2. Two pianos before the orchestra

      By 2 January 1875, Tchaikovsky has completed the concerto’s two-piano arrangement. Three days later he plays the work to Nikolay Rubinstein and Nikolay Hubert. The full orchestral score still lies ahead.

    3. The piano slips into a dance

      The Prestissimo starts in F major with quick piano writing. Soon the strings take a new tune in D major, and the pianist becomes their accompanist. A miniature scherzo has slipped into the middle of the slow movement.

    4. The brothers’ French song

      The fast middle borrows Il faut s’amuser, danser et rire — one must have fun, dance and laugh. Modest Tchaikovsky remembers singing it constantly with his brother Anatoly in the early 1870s. Their brother puts the refrain into a piano concerto.

    5. The piano remembers the flute

      The opening melody returns in D♭ major, now in the piano and dressed in small ornaments. The oboe continues it. The fast dance is over, and the flute’s first tune has become a conversation.

    6. Three pianissimos in D♭

      The coda settles quietly in D♭ major. Its last cadence moves from G♭ major to D♭, marked ppp: a gentle answer to the first movement’s closing chords.

    How it is joined

    1. An F-major chord starts running

      The piano holds a high F-major chord, pianissimo, at the end of the quiet section. The Prestissimo begins in that same key with fast solo writing. The strings then introduce the French song in D major over the piano’s accompaniment.

    2. The piano returns to D♭

      A cadenza-like piano passage ends the fast episode. The soloist brings back the opening D♭-major melody at the slower tempo, adding ornaments to the line first sung by the flute.

    3. The oboe completes the tune

      The oboe continues the returning melody and resolves it in D♭ major. A brief coda follows, ending with a G♭-major chord moving to D♭, marked ppp.

  3. III. Allegro con fuocoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    6:10

    The finale starts with a Ukrainian song whose accents keep the piano on its toes. A broad violin melody repeatedly interrupts the dance, then returns in B♭ major for the concerto’s grandest finish.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Ivan at the piano

      After a brief orchestral opening, the piano springs into a Ukrainian song, Go on, go on Ivan. Its short phrases and displaced accents keep the B♭-minor dance restless.

    2. The violins take a breath

      The violins introduce a broad melody in D♭ major, which the piano takes up in turn. The notes stretch out after the dance’s clipped phrases. This is the tune Tchaikovsky saves for the great final return.

    3. A second folk song

      In 1869 Tchaikovsky arranged the Russian folk song “I’m Coming to the Capital” as No. 30 of his Fifty Russian Folksongs, for piano duet. A variant of its tune is thought to be the finale’s broad violin melody.

    4. The song climbs a tone

      The broad melody returns in E♭ major, a tone above its first appearance. Descending scales reconnect it with the piano’s B♭-minor dance.

    5. Shorter and better

      In January 1889 Tchaikovsky tells the pianist Aleksandr Ziloti that he has altered a passage in this finale, “der verfluchte Stelle”, the accursed place. “I think it will be shorter and better,” he says. Fourteen years after composing the concerto, he is still working on it.

    6. Octaves before the song

      The piano’s rapid octaves help carry the long preparation towards B♭ major. The broad tune is held back while the soloist covers the keyboard.

    7. Bülow’s one comment

      On 13 January 1876 Bülow answers Tchaikovsky’s thoughts of revising the concerto: “no changes are necessary,” he writes, apart from some extra piano notes in a few tutti, which he has already added himself. His one comment on the finale concerns the tempo of the “triumphal 2nd motif” before the last stretta.

    8. The orchestra sings in B♭

      The broad melody returns in B♭ major, with piano and orchestra together. The violin tune that first interrupted the dance now takes over the concerto’s closing pages.

    9. Allegro vivo, one last sprint

      The marking becomes Allegro vivo. Quick piano figures lead into the final B♭-major chords, and an emphatic dominant-to-tonic cadence closes the concerto.

    How it is joined

    1. The violins open a longer phrase

      The transition passes through F major before the violins begin their broad D♭-major melody. The piano takes up the tune after them, exchanging the refrain’s short accented figures for longer notes.

    2. Down the scales to B♭ minor

      Descending scales leave the D♭-major melody behind. The transition turns through F major towards B♭ minor, where the piano’s dance returns in a shortened form.

    3. The broad tune returns in E♭

      After the dance’s intervening episodes, the broad melody returns in E♭ major. Its longer notes again stand out against the piano’s quick writing; the tune sits a tone above its first D♭-major statement.

    4. The dance returns through falling scales

      Descending scales bring the E♭-major song back towards B♭ minor. The piano recovers the refrain, then launches into a longer succession of scales and arpeggios on the way to the coda.

    5. The piano builds towards its octaves

      The refrain’s cadence opens a long preparation for B♭ major. Piano scales and arpeggios lead into rapid octaves, delaying the broad tune’s final return while the orchestra gathers behind the soloist.

    6. The song arrives in B♭ major

      The long dominant preparation resolves into the broad melody in B♭ major. Piano and orchestra share the statement. The tune first heard quietly in the violins now occupies the whole ensemble.

    7. Allegro vivo brings the final chords

      The broad melody releases into the Allegro vivo. Quick piano figures and emphatic orchestral chords carry the last bars to B♭ major, with a perfect cadence from F to B♭.
