Johann Sebastian Bach / I. The Baroque

St Matthew Passion, BWV 244

1727; revised 1736 · BWV 244 · Soloists, two choirs and orchestras, additional soprano ripieno

In the opening movement two choirs alternate in question and answer while a third group sings a chorale above them. Picander wrote the reflective poetry set around the text of Matthew’s Gospel.

2 recordings · 18 min

In the Hall

  1. No. 1. Kommt, ihr Töchter, helft mir klagenMünchener Bach-Chor and Münchener Chorknaben; Münchener Bach-Orchester; Karl Richter, conductor · June–August 1958, Herkulessaal, Munich
    9:49

    Karl Richter conducts “Kommt, ihr Töchter, helft mir klagen”, the opening chorus of Bach’s St Matthew Passion, BWV 244, in this 1958 recording. Two choirs alternate in E minor, and a third group of singers sings a hymn melody above them.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Instrumental introduction, two orchestras

      E repeats in the bass beneath groups of three notes. Two orchestras play together, each with flutes, oboes, strings and continuo. The instruments play alone for sixteen bars in E minor before the voices enter.

    2. Picander’s libretto, 1727

      Picander, the pen name of Christian Friedrich Henrici, assigned this chorus to the Daughters of Zion and the faithful. One group calls and the other asks questions. Bach gave each group its own choir and orchestra, so the dialogue passes between two bodies of singers.

    3. Entry of the first choir

      “Kommt, ihr Töchter, helft mir klagen” — “Come, daughters, help me mourn.” The first choir enters in four parts. The second choir and its orchestra rest, and the first orchestra accompanies the voices over the repeated bass.

    4. Karl Richter’s recording, 1958

      Karl Richter recorded the Passion in stereo, then a new process, over twenty-two sessions between 23 June and 21 August 1958, some of them running late into the night. Critics first remarked on his speed. One wrote that Klemperer’s recording lasted about forty minutes longer.

    5. Entry of the ripieno sopranos

      The ripieno sopranos enter with “O Lamm Gottes, unschuldig” — “O Lamb of God, innocent”. Their hymn in G major moves in long notes above the two choirs and the instruments. Beneath it the first choir starts “Kommt, ihr Töchter” again, and the second choir goes on with its short questions.

    6. Chorale “O Lamm Gottes, unschuldig”

      The boys sing “O Lamm Gottes, unschuldig”, a German version of the Agnus Dei by Nikolaus Decius, a monastery provost who left his cloister for the Reformation in 1522. It was first printed in 1531, in a Low German hymnbook from Rostock. The tune is set in long notes above both choirs.

    7. Autograph score, 1736

      In 1736 Bach prepared a careful new score of the Passion, now in the Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, and wrote the words of the Gospel in red ink. He also wrote the words and notes of this opening hymn in red, above the two choirs.

    8. Funeral music for Prince Leopold, 1729

      In March 1729 Bach wrote funeral music for his former employer, Prince Leopold. Ten of its movements were derived from, or served as models for, music in the Passion. Picander wrote the words for both.

    9. St Matthew Passion, Leipzig, 1727

      On Good Friday, 11 April 1727, Bach performed the St Matthew Passion at vespers in St Thomas’s Church, Leipzig. He had been the city’s Thomaskantor for four years. Leipzig had heard his St John Passion in 1724.

    10. Final lines and chorale plea

      The two choirs sing of Jesus carrying the wood of the cross himself, “aus Lieb und Huld” (“out of love and grace”). After the short questions, they now set the words in longer groups. Above them, the chorale reaches its final line: “Have mercy on us, O Jesus.”

    11. Berlin performances, March 1829

      On 11 March 1829 the twenty-year-old Felix Mendelssohn conducted the Passion with the Berlin Singakademie, with Eduard Devrient singing Christ. Most of the arias were left out. A second performance on 21 March was as crowded as the first, and the takings went to a sewing school for poor girls.

    12. Return of the opening text

      After the hymn, the two main choirs sing “Kommt, ihr Töchter” again (bars 82–90). A B pedal in the bass holds the music on the dominant of E minor. Both choirs join on “als wie ein Lamm” — “like a lamb” — before the cadence in E minor.

    Joins

    1. Choir I enters at bar 17

      The E-minor introduction leads straight into the first choir’s entry at bar 17. Choir I sings “Kommt, ihr Töchter”, and Orchestra II stops playing. The first orchestra continues the 12/8 figures and the repeated bass under the four vocal parts. The second choir rests.

    2. Chorale entry above the two choirs

      The ripieno sopranos enter with “O Lamm Gottes, unschuldig”, a G-major hymn in long notes above the E-minor music. Choir I returns to its opening words and Choir II has short questions. The orchestras continue underneath. The bass keeps its lilting rhythm while the new melody spans whole bars.

    3. The final lines of Picander’s text

      The short questions give way to the poem’s last sentence, about Jesus carrying the cross himself. Each choir sings in larger blocks, supported by its own orchestra. The continuo keeps moving beneath them while the ripieno sopranos finish their hymn with “Erbarm dich unser, o Jesu”.

    4. The opening words over low B

      The hymn has finished. The two choirs return to “Kommt, ihr Töchter” in bars 82–90, above repeated B in the continuo, the dominant of E minor. Flutes, oboes and strings continue their winding lines. The bass then moves from B to E for the closing E-minor cadence, and the choirs come together on the last words, “als wie ein Lamm”.

  2. No. 47. Erbarme dich, mein GottHertha Töpper, alto; Otto Büchner, solo violin; Münchener Bach-Orchester; Karl Richter, conductor · June–August 1958, Herkulessaal, Munich
    7:46

    This aria for alto with solo violin follows Peter’s denial in the St Matthew Passion. Hertha Töpper sang it, with Otto Büchner playing the violin solo, in Karl Richter’s 1958 recording.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Solo violin introduction, B minor

      The solo violin plays long phrases over the bass in 12/8. Bach marks the accompanying strings piano sempre — softly throughout — and the bass pizzicato, plucked. The voice enters after eight bars of this B-minor music.

    2. Alto entry

      “Erbarme dich, mein Gott” (“Have mercy, my God”). The alto enters after the fermata that ends the introduction. Her phrases break off between appeals, and the solo violin rests before each of its own phrases.

    3. Peter’s denial, preceding recitative

      In the preceding recitative, Peter denies Jesus three times. The cock crows, and Peter remembers the warning and goes out weeping. Picander’s aria takes the last word of that account, “bitterlich” (bitterly), into its own poem.

    4. Violinist at Weimar, 1703

      In 1703, at the age of eighteen, Bach spent half a year as a violinist in Duke Johann Ernst’s private orchestra at Weimar. His next post was as organist at Arnstadt. In this aria, written years later, a solo violin plays the introduction and, through the Dal Segno, the close.

    5. Violin interlude in F♯ minor

      The alto ends “um meiner Zähren willen” (“for the sake of my tears”) and rests. Above the continuing bass, the violin plays a short interlude in F♯ minor. The alto then enters on “Schaue hier”, which begins the second part of the poem, in which heart and eyes weep before God.

    6. Review by Heinrich Sievers, 1959

      In May 1959 the critic Heinrich Sievers reviewed Richter’s four stereo LPs. Of the arias with solo instruments he wrote that “the music is no longer projected but stands alive in the room”. Here Hertha Töpper sings, with Otto Büchner on solo violin.

    7. Return of “Erbarme dich”

      After “bitterlich”, the alto sings “Erbarme dich” again. The harmony moves back to B minor, and the solo violin continues its figuration beside the voice. Bach repeats these few words many times.

    8. Autograph score, 1736–40

      The aria survives in Bach’s own handwriting. His score of the revised Passion, written out between 1736 and 1740, is held by the Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin as Mus. ms. Bach P 25.

    9. Dal Segno return of the introduction

      After the alto finishes “for the sake of my tears”, a Dal Segno sends the instruments back to the eight-bar violin introduction, and the voice is silent. These eight bars end the aria at the final fermata.

    Joins

    1. Fermata, then the alto enters

      The solo violin’s eight-bar introduction closes in B minor under a fermata. After the pause the alto enters with “Erbarme dich”. The accompanying strings stay soft over the plucked bass. The solo violin rests for a short time, then replies to the voice. The 12/8 metre continues.

    2. Violin interlude before “Schaue hier”

      The alto ends “um meiner Zähren willen” with a cadence in F♯ minor and rests. The strings and bass continue, and the solo violin plays a short interlude. The voice then enters with “Schaue hier”, the second part of the poem, over the same lilting metre.

    3. Return to B minor

      The alto repeats “Erbarme dich”, and the music moves from F♯ minor back towards B minor. The bass carries the harmony back to B minor while the solo violin plays around the returning vocal phrase. The opening text returns in B minor before the final appeal for mercy.

    4. Dal Segno to the introduction

      The alto ends “um meiner Zähren willen” with a B-minor cadence. The instruction Dal Segno sends the instruments back to the opening sign, and the solo violin melody, soft string chords and plucked bass are repeated without the voice. The repeated introduction closes on its original fermata.
