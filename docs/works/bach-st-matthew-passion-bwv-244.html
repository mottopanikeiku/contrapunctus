Johann Sebastian Bach / I. The Baroque

St Matthew Passion, BWV 244

1727; revised 1736 · BWV 244 · Soloists, two choirs and orchestras, additional soprano ripieno

Two choirs question and answer one another in the opening movement while a third group sings a chorale above them. Picander supplied the reflective poetry around Matthew’s Gospel.

2 recordings · 18 min

In the Hall

  1. No. 1. Kommt, ihr Töchter, helft mir klagenMünchener Bach-Chor and Münchener Chorknaben; Münchener Bach-Orchester; Karl Richter, conductor · June–August 1958, Herkulessaal, Munich
    9:49

    Two choirs call across an E-minor lament, then a third group of singers raises a hymn above them. Karl Richter conducts the opening chorus of Bach’s St Matthew Passion in this 1958 recording.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Two orchestras, no voices

      E repeats in the bass beneath rocking groups of three notes. Two orchestras play together, each with flutes, oboes, strings and continuo. The voices wait through sixteen bars, while the instruments make their own long lament in E minor.

    2. Picander gives the choirs roles

      Picander, the pen name of Christian Friedrich Henrici, gives this chorus to the Daughters of Zion and the faithful. One group calls; the other questions it. Bach gives each group its own choir and orchestra, making the poem’s conversation travel between two bodies of singers.

    3. The first choir calls

      “Kommt, ihr Töchter, helft mir klagen” — “Come, daughters, help me mourn.” The first choir enters in four parts. The second choir and its orchestra wait while the first orchestra carries the voices over the repeated bass.

    4. A hymn above the argument

      The ripieno sopranos begin “O Lamm Gottes, unschuldig” — “O Lamb of God, innocent”. Their G-major hymn moves in long notes above the restless choirs and instruments. Under it, the first choir begins “Kommt, ihr Töchter” again; the second continues asking its short questions.

    5. The hymn in red ink

      In 1736 Bach prepares a careful new score of the Passion. He writes the words of the Gospel in red ink. This opening hymn receives the same treatment: its words and notes stand out in red above the two choirs.

    6. Good Friday in Leipzig

      On Good Friday, 11 April 1727, Bach performs the St Matthew Passion in Leipzig. It belongs to vespers at St Thomas’s Church. He has been the city’s Thomaskantor for four years; Leipzig has already heard his St John Passion in 1724.

    7. Carrying the wood himself

      The two choirs sing of Jesus carrying the wood of the cross himself, “aus Lieb und Huld” — “out of love and grace”. They give the words in larger groups now, after the clipped questions. Above them, the hymn reaches its final plea: “Have mercy on us, O Jesus.”

    8. The first words come back

      The hymn ends, leaving the two main choirs to sing “Kommt, ihr Töchter” again. B keeps sounding in the bass, holding the music on E minor’s dominant. The final appeal brings both choirs together on “als wie ein Lamm” — “like a lamb”.

    How it is joined

    1. The first choir enters alone

      The E-minor introduction reaches the first choir’s entry without a pause. Choir I sings “Kommt, ihr Töchter” while Orchestra II falls silent. The first orchestra keeps the rocking 12/8 figures and repeated bass moving beneath the four vocal parts. The second choir has yet to answer.

    2. Long notes above the choirs

      The ripieno sopranos enter with “O Lamm Gottes, unschuldig”, a G-major hymn in long notes above the E-minor music. Choir I returns to its opening words while Choir II asks its short questions. The orchestras continue underneath; the bass keeps its lilting rhythm as the new melody spans whole bars.

    3. The questions become a shared lament

      The short questions give way to the poem’s last sentence, about Jesus carrying the cross himself. Each choir sings in larger blocks, supported by its orchestra. The continuo keeps moving beneath them while the ripieno sopranos finish their hymn with “Erbarm dich unser, o Jesu”.

    4. The opening words over low B

      The hymn has finished. The two choirs return to “Kommt, ihr Töchter” above repeated B in the continuo, the dominant of E minor. Flutes, oboes and strings continue their winding lines. The bass finally leaves B for E, and the choirs meet on the last words, “als wie ein Lamm”.

  2. No. 47. Erbarme dich, mein GottHertha Töpper, alto; Otto Büchner, solo violin; Münchener Bach-Orchester; Karl Richter, conductor · June–August 1958, Herkulessaal, Munich
    7:46

    After Peter’s denial, an alto asks for mercy while a solo violin sings beside her. Hertha Töpper and Otto Büchner share the lament in this aria from Karl Richter’s 1958 St Matthew Passion.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A violin sings first

      The solo violin bends its long phrases over a gently rocking bass. Bach marks the accompanying strings piano sempre — softly throughout — and the bass pizzicato, plucked. The voice waits through eight bars of this B-minor music.

    2. The alto asks for mercy

      “Erbarme dich, mein Gott” — “Have mercy, my God.” The alto enters after the violin’s held chord. Her phrases keep breaking off between appeals, while the violin rejoins her with a flowing line of its own.

    3. Peter goes out weeping

      In the preceding recitative, Peter denies Jesus three times. The cock crows; Peter remembers the warning and goes out weeping. Picander’s aria carries the last word of that account into its own poem: “bitterlich”, bitterly. The alto’s grief grows from the narrator’s final sentence.

    4. Bach with a violin

      In 1703 Bach spends half a year as a violinist in Duke Johann Ernst’s private orchestra at Weimar. He is eighteen. His next job is at the organ in Arnstadt; here, years later, he gives a violin the first and last word.

    5. The violin fills the pause

      The alto ends “um meiner Zähren willen” — “for the sake of my tears” — and rests. The violin carries a short interlude in F♯ minor above the continuing bass. The next words, “Schaue hier”, begin the poem’s second thought: heart and eyes weep before God.

    6. The same plea returns

      After “bitterlich”, the alto calls “Erbarme dich” again. The opening plea gradually regains its B-minor home, and the violin resumes its curling phrases beside the voice. The words are few; Bach gives them room to be said over and over.

    7. The violin closes the aria

      The alto finishes “for the sake of my tears”. Dal Segno sends the instruments back to the opening violin music, and the voice falls silent. The same eight bars now carry the aria to its last fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. A fermata, then the alto

      The violin’s eight-bar introduction closes in B minor beneath a fermata. After the pause, the alto begins “Erbarme dich”. The accompanying strings remain soft over the plucked bass; the solo violin briefly rests, then answers the voice. The rocking 12/8 pulse continues.

    2. The violin carries the sentence on

      The alto closes “um meiner Zähren willen” in F♯ minor and rests. Over the continuing strings and bass, the violin carries a short interlude. The voice then begins “Schaue hier”, the second part of the poem, with the same lilting metre beneath it.

    3. Erbarme dich finds B minor again

      The alto’s repeated “Erbarme dich” turns the music back from F♯ minor towards B minor. The bass leads the harmony home while the solo violin winds around the returning vocal phrase. Both the opening words and their key come back before the final appeal for mercy.

    4. The voice leaves the violin

      The alto ends “um meiner Zähren willen” with a B-minor cadence. The instruction Dal Segno returns the instruments to the opening sign: violin melody, soft string chords and plucked bass. The voice has finished. The repeated introduction closes on its original fermata.
