Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart / II. Classical

Clarinet Concerto, K. 622

1791 · K. 622 · Basset clarinet and orchestra

Stadler’s clarinet reached four semitones below the ordinary instrument. Modern basset-clarinet versions restore the low passages that early publishers moved up or rewrote.

3 recordings · 29 min

In the Hall

  1. I. AllegroWilliam McColl, basset clarinet; University of Washington Symphony Orchestra, Abraham Kaplan · December 1987
    12:26

    Mozart gives the clarinet a graceful A major tune, then sends it through quick runs, low phrases and sudden turns into minor keys. William McColl plays a basset clarinet of his own construction with Abraham Kaplan and the University of Washington Symphony Orchestra.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The strings begin the conversation

      The strings introduce a graceful A major melody. Winds soon join their answers, and Mozart sets parts of the tune chasing one another in canon. The orchestra has a lively conversation of its own before the first independent solo.

    2. The melody gains a soloist

      The clarinet takes up the opening melody over soft strings. It soon extends the tune, adds new phrases and runs farther than the orchestra’s version. The wind section rests at the entry, giving the soloist room.

    3. First, a basset horn

      A surviving sketch shows Mozart first planning the concerto for a basset horn in G. He soon switches to the new basset clarinet in A, which reaches the octave below middle C, and Stadler plays it with great skill.

    4. Written for Anton Stadler

      Mozart writes the concerto for Anton Stadler and his extended-range clarinet. Its extra low notes open up another room in the instrument: the solo can descend below the ordinary clarinet’s compass, then climb back into its bright upper voice.

    5. A friend who never paid

      Stadler is a fellow Freemason and a habitual borrower. Mozart’s widow and sister-in-law later say he never repaid what he borrowed, and that he owed Mozart some 500 florins at his death. Mozart still seems to enjoy his company.

    6. The soloist accompanies the orchestra

      The clarinet’s quick broken chords make an accompaniment beneath the orchestra’s phrases. Mozart lets the soloist be useful in several ways: it can sing the melody, answer it, or keep a stream of notes moving underneath.

    7. The tune leaves familiar ground

      After the orchestra closes in E major, the clarinet begins the development with a familiar phrase. Changing keys draw it away from the opening’s easy balance. The soloist’s wide range gives these exchanges a deep bass voice as well as a singing top.

    8. The lost autograph

      Stadler says the portmanteau holding Mozart’s manuscripts was stolen in Germany; the widow is told it was pawned there for 73 ducats. The first edition moves the low passages up an octave for an ordinary clarinet and does not say which. Players on a basset clarinet reconstruct them by educated guesses.

    9. McColl’s handmade basset clarinet

      In December 1987, William McColl performs the concerto on a basset clarinet he has built himself. The University of Washington orchestra plays under Abraham Kaplan. The maker and the soloist are the same person.

    10. A major and the opening melody

      The clarinet brings the opening melody back in A major. Quiet strings surround it, with first violins answering the solo line. The familiar phrases have returned, though Mozart still gives the soloist plenty of room to elaborate them.

    11. Prague, October 1791

      The concerto is probably first heard in Prague on 16 October 1791, less than two months before Mozart dies at thirty-five. It is the last major instrumental work he completes.

    12. The orchestra takes the last turn

      Strings and winds take over the closing phrases in A major. Mozart gives this movement no large, separate cadenza: the clarinet’s freedom has been woven through the solos. The final orchestral cadence answers its last flourish.

    How it is joined

    1. The clarinet takes the opening tune

      The full orchestra closes in A major. Flutes, bassoons and horns then fall silent, and the clarinet begins its independent solo over quiet strings. The familiar melody carries across the change of players, with the violins supplying answers beneath it.

    2. Beyond the E major cadence

      The orchestra rounds off the exposition in E major. The clarinet resumes with a familiar melodic phrase over lighter string accompaniment. Repeated figures and changing bass notes carry the solo away from that cadence into the development.

    3. E prepares the return to A

      The dominant E prepares the return to A major. The clarinet restores the opening tune over quiet upper strings, with cellos and double basses resting at its entry. First violins answer the solo line as the opening phrases unfold again.

    4. The orchestra completes the soloist’s cadence

      The clarinet’s closing flourish leads into the orchestral ritornello in A major. Winds reinforce the strings, then the scoring briefly thins before the final full cadence. Mozart finishes with the orchestra together, without a separate cadenza breaking the approach.

  2. II. AdagioWilliam McColl, basset clarinet; University of Washington Symphony Orchestra, Abraham Kaplan · December 1987
    7:33

    The clarinet sings a D major melody, and the orchestra answers in phrases of its own. William McColl plays the Adagio on a basset clarinet he built himself, with Abraham Kaplan and the University of Washington Symphony Orchestra.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A song without words

      The clarinet begins over gently moving violins and held viola notes. Cellos provide a light bass; double basses and the orchestral winds wait. The melody rises, lingers and falls, with rests that give each phrase a singer’s breath.

    2. The orchestra answers in D

      The orchestra answers the solo phrase. Flutes, bassoons and horns join the strings, and the double basses add weight beneath them. The melody belongs to the violins now, with the clarinet doubling their line in the printed score.

    3. McColl builds his clarinet

      William McColl plays an instrument of his own construction in this December 1987 performance. The basset clarinet extends the ordinary clarinet’s low range, giving Mozart’s soloist more room beneath the stave.

    4. The singer grows more elaborate

      The middle section begins in A major. The clarinet decorates its line with quick runs and wider leaps, while the strings accompany softly. Long notes and little flurries alternate, like a singer finding more to say between breaths.

    5. An audience falls quiet

      On 7 October 1791, while finishing the concerto, Mozart writes to Constanze in Baden after another full house for The Magic Flute. What pleases him most is its “silent applause”: the audience’s absorbed attention during the music.

    6. The first melody returns

      A pause on the dominant prepares the return of the D major melody. The clarinet resumes over the softly moving strings; the double basses fall silent again. Mozart shortens the return, then lets the soloist add a more ornate closing passage.

    7. A last decorated phrase

      The clarinet adds curling figures and a trill above quiet strings. The melody stays close to D major as the phrases settle. Winds and horns return for the closing cadence, placing a soft orchestral frame around the soloist’s last notes.

    How it is joined

    1. The violins take up the song

      The solo phrase closes on A, the dominant of D major. Moving string notes lead into the orchestral answer in D. Flutes, bassoons, horns and double basses join, while the violins take the melody over the continuing accompaniment.

    2. A major and a freer solo

      The orchestral reply closes and the winds fall silent. The clarinet begins the A major middle section above soft strings, extending its phrases into runs and leaps. The accompaniment keeps the slow three-beat measure beneath these quicker notes.

    3. A pause on A, then D

      Orchestral chords alternate with clarinet runs, leading to a fermata on A major, the dominant. After the pause, the opening tune returns in D over the violins’ flowing accompaniment. Winds and double basses rest, restoring the light scoring of the beginning.

    4. The winds leave the closing solo

      The orchestral reply reaches D major, and the winds fall silent. A rising clarinet figure begins the coda over quiet strings. The soloist ornaments the closing phrases before winds and horns return for the final D major cadence.

  3. III. Rondo: AllegroWilliam McColl, basset clarinet; University of Washington Symphony Orchestra, Abraham Kaplan · December 1987
    8:51

    The clarinet’s skipping refrain returns between episodes of long-breathed melody and quick runs. William McColl plays this A major rondo on his own handmade basset clarinet, whose low notes give the solo line an unusually deep reach.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A light-footed clarinet tune

      The clarinet begins a skipping tune in six-eight above violins and violas. Cellos, double basses and orchestral winds wait for the answering tutti. The upper strings leave plenty of space beneath the soloist’s first phrases.

    2. Coffee, tobacco and Stadler’s rondo

      Vienna, 7 October 1791: Mozart has coffee brought in and enjoys a pipe of tobacco. Writing to Constanze in Baden, he follows these domestic pleasures with a day’s work: “Then I orchestrated almost the whole of Stadler’s rondo.”

    3. The melody takes longer breaths

      The first episode gives the clarinet a more flowing melody and carries it towards E major. Short string replies support the longer solo phrases. The refrain’s little skips have opened into something more expansive.

    4. A short visit from the refrain

      The A major refrain returns briefly. Its familiar rhythm is easy to recognise, but Mozart soon lets the orchestra carry the music away again. This homecoming is a short one.

    5. F♯ minor beneath the runs

      The central episode turns to F♯ minor. The clarinet moves through wide leaps and running figures above quiet strings, with brief wind replies. Its bright upper notes and deep lower phrases now belong to a darker harmony.

    6. The second tune arrives first

      Mozart brings back the earlier episode’s melody before restoring the full refrain. The soloist has its longer-breathed tune again, while the skipping opening still waits. The familiar ideas return in an unexpected order.

    7. The skipping tune comes home

      The clarinet finally recovers the full refrain in A major. Upper strings accompany the return; the orchestral winds wait. The soloist still has room for further runs before everyone joins the closing phrases.

    8. A concerto survives without its autograph

      Mozart’s manuscript of the completed concerto is lost. Early printed editions adapt the solo for an ordinary clarinet, moving its lowest phrases upwards. Modern basset-clarinet versions recover the deeper compass for which Mozart writes.

    9. Everyone joins the last cadence

      Flutes, bassoons and horns join the strings for the closing tutti. The clarinet’s last trill gives way to the orchestra’s A major cadence. After so much nimble solo writing, the last chords are brisk and unanimous.

    How it is joined

    1. A longer solo turns towards E

      The orchestral continuation of the A major refrain gives way to a longer clarinet melody over quieter strings. The solo phrases and changing bass lead towards E major. The six-eight pulse continues beneath the more sustained line.

    2. The clarinet restores A major

      The first episode leads back through E, the dominant of A. The clarinet restores the skipping refrain in A major, with the strings supporting its familiar short phrases. An orchestral continuation soon moves the music on again.

    3. The orchestra prepares F♯ minor

      The orchestral continuation turns away from A major towards F♯ minor. Winds reinforce the cadence, then the clarinet resumes over quiet strings. Its wide leaps and running figures extend the new minor-key passage.

    4. The longer melody returns before the refrain

      The central minor-key passage gives way to the earlier episode’s flowing clarinet melody. Quieter strings support the solo as it returns towards the home-key region. Mozart postpones the complete A major refrain, bringing the secondary tune back first.

    5. The clarinet steps back into A

      The connecting passage brings the harmony back to A major. The clarinet begins the refrain over violins and violas, while winds and lower strings rest. The returning six-eight tune restores the light scoring of the opening.
