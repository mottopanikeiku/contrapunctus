Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart / II. Classical

Clarinet Concerto, K. 622

1791 · K. 622 · Basset clarinet and orchestra

Stadler’s clarinet reached four semitones below the ordinary instrument. Modern basset-clarinet versions restore the low passages that early publishers moved up or rewrote.

3 recordings · 29 min

In the Hall

  1. I. AllegroWilliam McColl, basset clarinet; University of Washington Symphony Orchestra, Abraham Kaplan · December 1987
    12:26

    The first movement, Allegro, of Mozart’s Clarinet Concerto in A major, K. 622, sets an A major theme for the clarinet among quick runs, low phrases and sudden turns into minor keys. William McColl plays a basset clarinet he built himself, with Abraham Kaplan conducting the University of Washington Symphony Orchestra.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening orchestral exposition

      The strings play the first theme, in A major. The winds soon join, and Mozart sets parts of the tune in canon. The orchestra plays its own exposition before the clarinet’s first independent solo.

    2. First solo entry, clarinet

      The clarinet takes up the opening melody over soft strings. It soon extends the tune with new phrases and runs further than the orchestra’s version does. The wind section rests at the clarinet’s entry.

    3. Sketch for basset horn in G

      A surviving sketch shows that Mozart first planned the concerto for a basset horn in G. He soon changed to the new basset clarinet in A, which reached the octave below middle C. Stadler played it with great skill.

    4. Written for Anton Stadler

      Mozart wrote the concerto for Anton Stadler and his extended-range clarinet. The instrument’s extra low notes let the solo part descend below the ordinary clarinet’s compass before it climbs back into the upper register.

    5. Anton Stadler and Mozart’s money

      Stadler was a fellow Freemason and a habitual borrower. Mozart’s widow and sister-in-law later said that he never repaid what he borrowed, and that he owed Mozart some 500 florins when Mozart died.

    6. Clarinet accompanying the orchestra

      Later in the solo exposition the clarinet plays quick broken chords beneath the orchestra’s phrases. Elsewhere in the movement Mozart gives the soloist the melody, or answering phrases.

    7. Development, clarinet entry

      After the orchestra closes in E major, the clarinet begins the development with a phrase heard earlier in the movement. The harmony then modulates through several keys. In these exchanges the soloist uses both the low and the high ends of its range.

    8. The lost autograph

      Stadler said that the portmanteau holding Mozart’s manuscripts was stolen in Germany; the widow was told it had been pawned there for 73 ducats. The first edition moved the low passages up an octave for an ordinary clarinet without marking which. Basset clarinettists reconstruct them by educated guesses.

    9. McColl’s basset clarinet, 1987

      In December 1987 William McColl played the concerto on a basset clarinet he had built himself. The University of Washington orchestra accompanied him under Abraham Kaplan.

    10. Return of the opening theme

      The clarinet plays the opening melody again in A major, with quiet strings around it and first violins replying to the solo line. Mozart leaves the soloist room to elaborate the phrases.

    11. First performance, Prague, 1791

      The concerto was probably first heard in Prague on 16 October 1791, less than two months before Mozart died at thirty-five. It was the last major instrumental work he completed.

    12. Final ritornello in A major

      Strings and winds take over the closing phrases in A major. Mozart wrote no large, separate cadenza for this movement. The final orchestral cadence follows the clarinet’s last flourish.

    Joins

    1. The clarinet takes the opening tune

      The full orchestra closes in A major. Flutes, bassoons and horns then stop, and at bar 57 the clarinet begins its independent solo over quiet strings. The clarinet takes over the familiar melody from the orchestra, and the violins play responses beneath it.

    2. From exposition to development

      The orchestra closes the exposition with a cadence in E major. The clarinet re-enters with a phrase heard earlier in the movement, accompanied by lighter strings. Over repeated figures and a moving bass line the solo part leaves E major and leads into the development.

    3. Dominant E leads back to A major

      The harmony rests on the dominant, E, before the return to A major. The clarinet restores the opening tune over the upper strings at a quiet dynamic. Cellos and double basses rest at its entry. First violins reply to the solo line as the opening phrases return.

    4. Clarinet flourish into closing ritornello

      The clarinet’s closing flourish leads into the orchestral ritornello in A major. The winds reinforce the strings, and the scoring then thins briefly before the final full cadence. The movement ends with the full orchestra and no separate cadenza before the close.

  2. II. AdagioWilliam McColl, basset clarinet; University of Washington Symphony Orchestra, Abraham Kaplan · December 1987
    7:33

    The Adagio of the Clarinet Concerto in A major, K. 622, is in D major. The clarinet plays the melody and the orchestra replies with phrases of its own. William McColl plays it on a basset clarinet he built himself, with Abraham Kaplan and the University of Washington Symphony Orchestra.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening of the Adagio, solo clarinet

      The clarinet enters over moving violins and held viola notes, with the cellos playing a light bass. The double basses and orchestral winds rest. The melody rises and falls, and each phrase is separated by rests.

    2. First tutti in D

      The orchestra follows the solo phrase in D. Flutes, bassoons and horns join the strings, with the double basses beneath them. The violins take the melody, and in the printed score the clarinet doubles their line.

    3. McColl’s basset clarinet, December 1987

      In this December 1987 performance William McColl played an instrument he had built himself. The basset clarinet extends the low range of the ordinary clarinet and gives Mozart’s soloist more notes below the stave.

    4. Middle section, A major

      The middle section begins in A major. The clarinet decorates its line with quick runs and wider leaps, and the strings accompany softly. Long notes alternate with short runs.

    5. Letter to Constanze, October 1791

      On 7 October 1791, while finishing the concerto, Mozart wrote to Constanze in Baden after another full house for The Magic Flute. He wrote that what pleased him most was its “silent applause”, the audience’s absorbed attention during the music.

    6. Return of the first melody

      A pause on the dominant prepares the return of the D major melody. The clarinet resumes over the softly moving strings, and the double basses rest again. Mozart shortens the return, and the soloist then plays a more ornate closing passage.

    7. Coda, final clarinet phrase

      The clarinet plays ornamental figures and a trill above quiet strings. The melody stays close to D major. Winds and horns return for the closing cadence and accompany the soloist’s last notes.

    Joins

    1. The violins take over the melody

      The solo phrase closes on A, the dominant of D major. Moving notes in the strings lead into the orchestral tutti in D. Flutes, bassoons, horns and double basses join, and the violins take over the melody above the continuing accompaniment.

    2. The A major middle section

      The orchestral passage closes and the winds stop. Above soft strings, the clarinet begins the A major middle section and extends its phrases into runs and leaps. Beneath these quicker notes the accompaniment keeps to the slow three-beat measure.

    3. Fermata on A, return in D

      Orchestral chords alternate with clarinet runs and lead to a fermata on A major, the dominant. After the pause the opening tune returns in D over a flowing accompaniment in the violins. The winds and double basses rest, and the scoring is again as light as at the beginning.

    4. Winds rest before the closing solo

      The orchestral reply reaches D major, and the winds stop. A rising clarinet figure begins the coda over quiet strings. The soloist ornaments the closing phrases, and winds and horns return for the final cadence in D major.

  3. III. Rondo: AllegroWilliam McColl, basset clarinet; University of Washington Symphony Orchestra, Abraham Kaplan · December 1987
    8:51

    The third movement of the Clarinet Concerto in A major, K. 622, is a rondo marked Allegro, in which the clarinet’s refrain returns between episodes of melody and quick runs. William McColl plays it on his own handmade basset clarinet, whose low notes extend the range of the solo line.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Clarinet theme in six-eight

      The solo clarinet plays a skipping tune in six-eight above violins and violas. Cellos, double basses and the orchestral winds rest until the tutti, where they enter.

    2. Letter to Constanze, October 1791

      On 7 October 1791 in Vienna, Mozart had coffee brought in and smoked a pipe of tobacco. He told Constanze, who was in Baden, about this in a letter and went on to describe his work that day. “Then I orchestrated almost the whole of Stadler’s rondo.”

    3. First episode, towards E major

      The first episode gives the clarinet a more flowing melody and moves towards E major. Short string phrases accompany the longer solo lines.

    4. First return of the refrain

      The A major refrain returns briefly, in its original rhythm. The orchestra then takes over in a tutti that leads away from it, before the minor-key episode.

    5. Central episode in F♯ minor

      The central episode moves to F♯ minor. The clarinet plays wide leaps and running figures over quiet strings, with brief replies from the wind, and uses both its high and low registers.

    6. Return of the episode theme

      Mozart brings back the melody of the earlier episode before the full refrain returns. The clarinet plays its longer-phrased tune again. The opening theme of the refrain comes back after it.

    7. Final return of the refrain

      The clarinet plays the full refrain in A major, accompanied by the upper strings. The orchestral winds rest. The soloist has further runs before the full orchestra enters for the closing phrases.

    8. Lost autograph and early editions

      Mozart’s manuscript of the completed concerto is lost. Early printed editions adapted the solo for an ordinary clarinet and moved its lowest phrases upwards. Modern basset-clarinet versions restore the deeper compass for which Mozart wrote, though the details of individual passages differ between editions.

    9. Closing tutti and final cadence

      Flutes, bassoons and horns join the strings for the closing tutti. After the clarinet’s last trill, the orchestra plays the cadence in A major.

    Joins

    1. A longer solo turns towards E

      The orchestra continues the A major refrain, then gives way to a longer clarinet melody over quieter strings. The solo phrases and a changing bass lead towards E major. The six-eight pulse continues beneath the more sustained clarinet line.

    2. Refrain returns in A major

      The first episode leads back through E, the dominant of A. The clarinet plays the refrain in A major, its short phrases accompanied by the strings. An orchestral continuation follows soon after and leads away from the refrain.

    3. Orchestral lead-in to F♯ minor

      The orchestral continuation moves away from A major towards F♯ minor. Winds reinforce the cadence, and the clarinet then resumes over quiet strings, extending the new minor-key passage with wide leaps and running figures.

    4. The second theme returns before the refrain

      The central minor-key passage gives way to the flowing clarinet melody of the earlier episode. The strings play more quietly beneath the solo as the music moves back towards the home-key region. Mozart holds back the complete A major refrain and brings the secondary tune back first.

    5. Return to A major and the refrain

      The connecting passage brings the harmony back to A major. The clarinet begins the refrain in six-eight over violins and violas, and the winds and lower strings rest. The scoring is the same light scoring as at the opening.
