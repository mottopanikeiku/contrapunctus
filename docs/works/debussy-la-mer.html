Claude Debussy / V. The French Line

La Mer

1905 · Orchestra

Three symphonic sketches: dawn to noon on the sea, the play of the waves, dialogue of the wind and the sea. Begun in Burgundy, finished in March 1905, first played that October.

4 recordings · 31 min

In the Hall

  1. I. De l’aube à midi sur la merDetroit Symphony Orchestra; Paul Paray · December 1955, Detroit
    8:14

    A low B, two harps and the soft roll of timpani open La Mer; by noon, horns and trombones are singing a chorale. Paul Paray conducts the Detroit Symphony Orchestra.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Dawn on B

      Double basses and timpani hold a low B. The two harps answer one another; muted cellos and violas begin to move above them. Debussy writes Très lent — very slow. The violins wait.

    2. The sea from Burgundy

      Debussy begins La Mer in the summer of 1903, while staying with his parents-in-law in Burgundy. The sea is far away. He has the memory of childhood holidays at Cannes, and the score grows from there.

    3. D♭ begins to flow

      The main movement changes to D♭ major and 6/8, marked Modéré, sans lenteur — moderate, without slowness. Divided strings keep the semiquavers running while the woodwinds pass short phrases between them.

    4. Two against three

      Twos and threes keep crossing in this movement. One part divides the beat into triplets while another plays even notes. The harps and divided strings can be busy with several rhythms beneath a single wind phrase.

    5. Sixteen cellos

      Debussy asks for sixteen cellos, divided into four groups. They sing a new tune in interlocking parts, with the horns answering above them. Très rythmé, he writes: the long melody still has a spring in its step.

    6. Hokusai’s wave

      Debussy chooses Hokusai’s The Great Wave off Kanagawa for the cover of the published score. The Japanese print is cropped and redrawn for Durand’s 1905 edition.

    7. Horns, trombones, noon

      Très lent again. Bassoons, horns and trombones sing the chorale, with triplets in the flutes and flowing harp figures below. The music is broad without standing still. Its final chords bring D♭ major into full daylight.

    How it is joined

    1. B opens into D♭

      The introduction accelerates into Modéré, sans lenteur. B-centred harmony opens into D♭ major, and 6/4 becomes 6/8. The bassoons and harps punctuate the change while divided second violins and violas set the semiquavers running.

    2. The cellos take four parts

      The preceding chords subside into quiet timpani and strings. At Très rythmé, sixteen cellos divide into four parts, their tune rising over plucked double basses. Horn calls answer the cello phrases; the high woodwinds fall silent.

    3. The brass opens out

      The quiet cor anglais and cello phrases lead to Très lent. Bassoons, horns and trombones take up the chorale above a low D♭. Harps keep moving beneath them, and the flutes play triplets. A crescendo carries this hushed opening to the full-orchestra D♭-major close.

  2. II. Jeux de vaguesDetroit Symphony Orchestra; Paul Paray · December 1955, Detroit
    5:59

    In the middle movement of La Mer, woodwind phrases flicker above plucked strings and quick harp figures. The biggest waves break apart; the ending belongs to a solo flute and a few high string harmonics.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A few bright drops

      Soft chords in cor anglais, clarinets and bassoons hover above plucked cellos and basses. Harp figures and little bell-like notes punctuate the gaps. The violins answer in short chords, divided into several parts.

    2. A supple rhythm

      Debussy puts a qualification beside Allegro: dans un rythme très souple, in a very supple rhythm. Short accents and triplets keep tilting the phrases across the beat. The little figures are quick; the pulse has room to bend.

    3. Proofs at Eastbourne

      In July 1905 Debussy takes the proofs of La Mer to the Grand Hotel in Eastbourne. Writing to his publisher Jacques Durand, he finds that “the sea unfurls itself with an utterly British correctness”.

    4. Paris, October 1905

      Camille Chevillard conducts the Lamoureux Orchestra in the première of La Mer on 15 October 1905. The Paris audience receives it coolly. Three years later, Debussy conducts the work himself and wins an enthusiastic reception.

    5. A tune changes hands

      A short phrase can move from strings to woodwinds before it has settled into a tune. Debussy keeps altering its rhythm as it passes. The harps and percussion add small, bright replies between the orchestral phrases.

    6. Harps after the swell

      The strings soften and the wind phrases grow shorter. Harp glissandos rise and fall around a quiet clarinet solo, marked très doux — very gentle. The closing pages keep shedding instruments.

    7. Flute and harmonics

      On the last page, a solo flute leaves a small curling phrase above the held strings. Six first violins play harmonics, barely touched; a harp and a few bell notes glint around them. The bass stays quiet to the end.

    How it is joined

    1. The harps emerge

      The late crescendo recedes under En retenant, holding back. The double basses sustain while the upper strings fall silent. At rehearsal 39, the harps sweep in opposite directions, horns hold quiet notes, and a solo clarinet begins a gentle phrase. The bass continues underneath this much smaller ensemble.

  3. III. Dialogue du vent et de la merDetroit Symphony Orchestra; Paul Paray · December 1955, Detroit
    7:31

    The finale of La Mer begins with low strings churning beneath timpani and bass drum. Calls from the first movement return, and a brass chorale crowns the last rush towards D♭ major.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The storm starts low

      Animé et tumultueux — animated and tumultuous. Timpani and bass drum rumble softly while cellos and basses climb in short, twisting figures. The tam-tam adds a dark ring. Most of the orchestra is still silent.

    2. A muted trumpet calls

      A muted trumpet sounds above the low strings, beside oboe and clarinet phrases. For part of this passage the double basses bow close to the bridge, giving their held notes a thin, grainy edge.

    3. Lalo misses the sea

      Reviewing the 1905 première in Le Temps, Pierre Lalo writes: “I do not hear, I do not see, I do not smell the sea.” Debussy’s new score has disappointed one of his former admirers.

    4. A tune over triplets

      The longer wind phrases stretch over much quicker string figures. Groups of two and three cross beneath them. Debussy can make a melody seem to float while the accompaniment is working furiously.

    5. The dawn calls back

      The first movement’s trumpet call belongs to this finale too. Debussy brings back its shape among the stormier exchanges, and later restores the broad chorale. The music of dawn has survived into a rougher sea.

    6. Flutes above two harps

      In the quieter middle pages, flutes and oboes share little phrases above the two harps. The horns hold long notes, and the first violins sustain high above the moving inner strings. There is suddenly room around each line.

    7. Debussy conducts his sea

      In January 1908 Debussy stands before the Paris orchestra to conduct La Mer himself. The work that met such resistance in 1905 now brings him his greatest public success since Pelléas et Mélisande.

    8. The chorale returns

      The closing pages recover the first movement’s brass chorale. Horns and trombones hold the broad line against repeated wind notes and short string figures. Then Très animé sends the orchestra into its final rush.

    9. One last plucked blow

      The final D♭-major chords have a sharp edge. After the orchestra’s last sustained blast, timpani strike once more with plucked strings. Debussy marks the attack sec — dry.

    How it is joined

    1. The harps come through

      The brass exchanges subside into a passage for quieter winds and strings. Flutes and oboes share the tune above the two harps, while horns and double basses hold long notes. The first violins sustain high above the quicker viola and cello figures.

    2. The chorale crowns the sea

      The harmony turns towards D♭ major as the closing brass chorale emerges. Its broad notes stand over short, repeated string figures and a low brass foundation. The crescendo leads to Très animé, where the strings break into continuous running notes for the last rush.

  4. I. De l’aube à midi sur la merUnited States Air Force Band, Concert Band
    9:20

    The United States Air Force Band plays the first movement of La Mer in Lawrence Odom’s concert-band arrangement. A low B, barely stirring, grows into the broad D♭-major chords of noon.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Dawn on B

      A low B lies beneath the opening. Small phrases emerge above it, with long spaces between them. Debussy writes Très lent — very slow — and lets the first few notes arrive almost one by one.

    2. The sea from Burgundy

      In the summer of 1903 Debussy is staying with his parents-in-law in Burgundy, far from the coast. He begins La Mer there, drawing on memories of childhood summers at Cannes.

    3. Six quavers, without heaviness

      The main movement settles into D♭ major and 6/8. Debussy marks it Modéré, sans lenteur: moderate, without slowness. The little rising phrases now have a running accompaniment beneath them.

    4. Hokusai on the cover

      Debussy chooses Hokusai’s The Great Wave off Kanagawa for the cover of the published score. The curling wave becomes the first image readers see when they pick up La Mer.

    5. Two against three

      Groups of two notes and groups of three keep crossing through this movement. One phrase divides the beat evenly while another leans across it. Even a quiet passage can have this small rhythmic tug inside it.

    6. Four lines, one tune

      A new melody opens out in four parts, marked Très rythmé — strongly rhythmic. Its lines rise and answer one another, making a much longer sentence than the little calls of the opening.

    7. Debussy takes the podium

      Camille Chevillard conducts the première in Paris on 15 October 1905, to a cool reception. In January 1908 Debussy conducts it himself. This time the Paris audience is won over.

    8. The noon chorale

      The closing chorale moves in broad notes, with quicker figures still flowing underneath. The score returns to Très lent. D♭ major, first reached after the introduction, now fills the ending.

    How it is joined

    1. B opens into D♭

      The opening’s B-centred harmony shifts into D♭ major. Debussy changes from 6/4 to 6/8 and writes Modéré, sans lenteur. The sustained bass and widely spaced calls become short phrases over continuous semiquavers; the pulse begins to flow.

    2. The tune becomes a chorale

      At Très lent, the chorale stretches across the bar in broad, sustained notes. Quicker figures continue below it, so the movement carries two speeds at once. The closing progression gathers around D♭ major and ends with short, emphatic chords.
