Claude Debussy / V. The French Line

La Mer

1905 · Orchestra

Three symphonic sketches: dawn to noon on the sea, the play of the waves, dialogue of the wind and the sea. Begun in Burgundy, finished in March 1905 and first performed that October.

4 recordings · 31 min

In the Hall

  1. I. De l’aube à midi sur la merDetroit Symphony Orchestra; Paul Paray · December 1955, Detroit
    8:14

    “De l’aube à midi sur la mer”, the first movement of La Mer, opens on a low B with two harps and a soft timpani roll, and horns and trombones later play a chorale. Paul Paray conducts the Detroit Symphony Orchestra.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, low B

      Double basses and timpani hold a low B. The two harps alternate, and muted cellos and violas begin to move above them. Debussy marks the opening Très lent, very slow.

    2. Composition in Burgundy, 1903

      Debussy began La Mer in the summer of 1903, while staying with his parents-in-law in Burgundy, far from the sea. As a child he had spent holidays at Cannes.

    3. Main section in D♭ major, 6/8

      The main movement changes to D♭ major and 6/8, marked Modéré, sans lenteur (moderate, without slowness). Divided strings play continuous semiquavers, and the woodwinds pass short phrases between them.

    4. Letter to André Messager, 1903

      On 12 September 1903 Debussy told André Messager of “three symphonic sketches” titled La Mer: “Mer belle aux Sanguinaires”, “Jeu de vagues” and “le vent fait danser la mer”. Published, they became De l’aube à midi sur la mer, Jeux de vagues and Dialogue du vent et de la mer.

    5. Duplets against triplets

      Duplets and triplets cross throughout this movement. One part divides the beat into triplets while another plays even notes. Beneath a single wind phrase, the harps and divided strings can play several rhythms at once.

    6. Dedication and first performance, 1905

      Debussy dedicated La Mer to his publisher, Jacques Durand. Camille Chevillard conducted the first performance with the Lamoureux Orchestra in Paris on 15 October 1905, and Durand published the score that November.

    7. Divided cellos, rehearsal 9

      Debussy calls for sixteen cellos, divided into four groups. They play a new melody in interlocking parts, and the horns reply above them. The marking is Très rythmé.

    8. Cover of the first edition, 1905

      Debussy chose Hokusai’s The Great Wave off Kanagawa for the cover of the published score. The Japanese print was cropped and redrawn for the 1905 edition.

    9. Letter to Jacques Durand, 1906

      Writing to his publisher Durand from Le Puys, near Dieppe, on 8 August 1906, Debussy complained about bathers: “all those arms, those legs moving in such ridiculous rhythms, it’s enough to make the fish weep.” He wrote that there should be nothing in the sea but Sirens.

    10. Brass chorale and close in D♭ major

      Très lent again. Bassoons, horns and trombones play the chorale over triplets in the flutes and harp figures. The movement ends on chords of D♭ major.

    11. Recording, Detroit, December 1955

      Paul Paray and the Detroit Symphony recorded La Mer in the city’s Old Orchestra Hall on 3–4 December 1955, for Mercury’s Living Presence series. One Schoeps microphone in the centre and two Neumann U-47s left and right fed three-track half-inch tape.

    Joins

    1. From B to D♭ major

      The introduction accelerates into Modéré, sans lenteur. The harmony moves from a B centre to D♭ major, and the metre changes from 6/4 to 6/8. Bassoons and harps mark the change, and divided second violins and violas begin the running semiquavers.

    2. The cellos divide into four parts

      The preceding chords die away to soft timpani and strings. At Très rythmé, sixteen cellos divide into four parts and play a rising tune over plucked double basses. Horn calls follow the cello phrases. The high woodwinds stop.

    3. Brass chorale at Très lent

      Phrases for cor anglais and cellos lead to Très lent at rehearsal 14. Bassoons, horns and trombones take up the chorale above a low D♭. The harps keep moving beneath them, and the flutes play triplets. A crescendo builds to the close in D♭ major for full orchestra.

  2. II. Jeux de vaguesDetroit Symphony Orchestra; Paul Paray · December 1955, Detroit
    5:59

    “Jeux de vagues” is the middle movement of La Mer. Woodwind phrases play above plucked strings and quick harp figures, the largest climaxes break up, and the movement ends with a solo flute and a few high string harmonics.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Woodwind chords over pizzicato strings

      Soft chords in cor anglais, clarinets and bassoons sound above plucked cellos and basses. Harp figures and short notes on the glockenspiel (celesta is permitted as an alternative) fill the gaps. The violins, divided into several parts, follow in short chords.

    2. Tempo marking, Allegro

      Debussy qualified the Allegro with dans un rythme très souple (in a very supple rhythm). Short accents and triplets shift the phrases across the beat.

    3. Proofs at Eastbourne, 1905

      In July 1905 Debussy took the proofs of La Mer to the Grand Hotel in Eastbourne. In a letter from there to his publisher Jacques Durand, he wrote that “the sea unfurls itself with an utterly British correctness”.

    4. First performance, Paris, 1905

      Camille Chevillard conducted the Lamoureux Orchestra in the première of La Mer on 15 October 1905. The Paris audience received it coolly. Three years later, in January 1908, Debussy conducted the work himself in Paris and had an enthusiastic reception.

    5. Short phrase passed between sections

      A short phrase passes from strings to woodwinds before it has formed a complete tune. Debussy alters its rhythm at each change of instrument. Harps and percussion play brief figures between the orchestral phrases.

    6. Harp glissandos and clarinet solo

      The strings play more softly and the wind phrases become shorter. Harp glissandos rise and fall around a clarinet solo marked très doux — very gentle. In the closing pages, instruments drop out one by one.

    7. Final page, solo flute

      On the last page a solo flute plays a short phrase above held strings. Six first violins play harmonics, with harp and a few bell notes. The bass stays quiet to the end.

    Joins

    1. Harps enter at rehearsal 39

      Under En retenant the late crescendo falls away. The double basses sustain and the upper strings stop. At rehearsal 39 the harps play in contrary motion, the horns hold soft notes and a solo clarinet begins a phrase. The bass continues beneath this smaller ensemble.

  3. III. Dialogue du vent et de la merDetroit Symphony Orchestra; Paul Paray · December 1955, Detroit
    7:31

    The finale of La Mer, ‘Dialogue du vent et de la mer’, opens with low strings playing beneath timpani and bass drum. Calls from the first movement return, and a brass chorale sounds in the final fast passage, which ends in D♭ major.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening of movement III, low instruments

      Animé et tumultueux — animated and tumultuous. Timpani and bass drum roll softly while cellos and basses climb in short, twisting figures, and the tam-tam sounds. Most of the orchestra is still silent.

    2. Muted trumpet, low strings

      A muted trumpet plays above the low strings, beside phrases for oboe and clarinet. For part of this passage the double basses bow close to the bridge, which gives their held notes a thin, grainy edge.

    3. Pierre Lalo’s review, 1905

      Reviewing the 1905 première in Le Temps, Pierre Lalo, formerly an admirer of Debussy, wrote: “I do not hear, I do not see, I do not smell the sea.”

    4. Wind theme over duplets and triplets

      In the finale, the wind play a sustained theme in long phrases over much quicker string figures. Beneath it, groups of two and three notes are superimposed.

    5. Returns from the first movement

      The trumpet call of the first movement also belongs to the finale. Debussy brings back its shape among the stormier exchanges and later restores the broad chorale.

    6. Flutes and oboes above two harps

      In the middle pages, the flutes and oboes exchange short phrases above the two harps. The horns hold long notes and the first violins sustain a high line over the moving inner strings.

    7. Debussy conducts La Mer, Paris, 1908

      On 19 and 26 January 1908 Debussy conducted La Mer himself with the Paris orchestra. The work had met resistance in 1905, and these performances brought him his greatest public success since Pelléas et Mélisande.

    8. Return of the brass chorale

      The brass chorale from the first movement returns in the closing pages. Horns and trombones hold the broad line against repeated wind notes and short string figures. At Très animé the orchestra moves into the final fast section.

    9. Final chords in D♭ major

      After the orchestra’s last sustained chord in D♭ major, the timpani strike once more with the strings playing pizzicato. Debussy marks this final attack sec — dry.

    Joins

    1. Brass exchanges give way to harps

      The brass exchanges die away into a passage for quieter winds and strings. Flutes and oboes share the tune above the two harps, and horns and double basses hold long notes. The first violins sustain high notes above quicker figures in the violas and cellos.

    2. Brass chorale into Très animé

      The key signature returns to five flats and the harmony moves towards D♭ major as the closing brass chorale enters. The chorale’s long notes are held over short, repeated string figures and sustained low brass. A crescendo leads to Très animé, where the strings take up continuous running notes for the final section.

  4. I. De l’aube à midi sur la merUnited States Air Force Band, Concert Band
    9:20

    The United States Air Force Band plays the first movement of La Mer, “De l’aube à midi sur la mer”, in Lawrence Odom’s concert-band arrangement. The movement opens on a low B and moves to the broad D♭-major chords of noon.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening over a low B

      A low B is held beneath the opening. Short phrases enter above it, separated by long rests. Debussy marks the passage Très lent (very slow), and the first notes enter one at a time.

    2. Composition in Burgundy, 1903

      In the summer of 1903 Debussy stayed with his parents-in-law in Burgundy, far from the coast. He began La Mer there, drawing on memories of childhood summers at Cannes.

    3. Main section in D♭ major, 6/8

      The main section of the movement is in D♭ major and 6/8. Debussy marks it Modéré, sans lenteur (moderate, without slowness). The short rising phrases now have a running accompaniment beneath them.

    4. Hokusai print on the score cover

      Debussy chose Hokusai’s The Great Wave off Kanagawa for the cover of the published score of La Mer.

    5. Twos against threes

      Groups of two notes and groups of three cross throughout the movement. One phrase divides the beat evenly while another runs across it, and the cross-rhythm continues in the quieter passages. Jacob Greenberg identified this interplay of twos and threes as a feature of the whole first movement.

    6. Four-part melody, rehearsal 9

      A new melody enters in four parts, marked Très rythmé (strongly rhythmic). Its lines rise in turn and overlap, forming a phrase much longer than the short figures of the opening.

    7. Paris performances, 1905 and 1908

      Camille Chevillard conducted the première in Paris on 15 October 1905, and it was coolly received. Debussy conducted the work himself in Paris on 19 and 26 January 1908, and the audience received it more warmly.

    8. Closing chorale in D♭ major

      The closing chorale moves in broad notes over quicker figures underneath. The score returns to Très lent. D♭ major, first reached after the introduction, returns for the ending.

    Joins

    1. From B to D♭ major

      The harmony of the opening, centred on B, moves to D♭ major. The metre changes from 6/4 to 6/8, and the new section is marked Modéré, sans lenteur. The sustained bass and widely spaced calls of the introduction give way to short phrases over continuous semiquavers.

    2. The tune becomes a chorale

      At Très lent, the chorale is set in broad, sustained notes that stretch across the bar. Quicker figures continue below it, so the movement has two speeds at once. The closing progression centres on D♭ major and ends with short, emphatic chords.
