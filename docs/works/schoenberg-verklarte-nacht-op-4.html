Arnold Schoenberg / VI. Modernism

Verklärte Nacht (Transfigured Night)

1899 · Op. 4 · Two violins, two violas and two cellos; later arranged for string orchestra

A woman confesses that she carries another man’s child; her lover accepts them both. The sextet follows Dehmel’s poem from D minor to D major.

2 recordings · 29 min

In the Hall

  1. LP side 1 (bars 1–228)Hollywood String Quartet (Felix Slatkin and Paul Shure, violins; Paul Robyn, viola; Eleanor Aller, cello); Alvin Dinkin, viola; Kurt Reher, cello · 21–22 August 1950
    14:43

    The Hollywood String Quartet and two guests play the original six-string version of Verklärte Nacht, Op. 4, in which two people walk through a cold wood and the woman tells her companion she is carrying another man’s child. The LP’s first side (bars 1–228) ends before his answer.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, violas and cellos

      The second viola and second cello repeat a low D. Above them, the first viola and first cello begin a descending phrase marked immer leise (always quiet). The violins rest until later.

    2. Dehmel’s poem, the woman’s confession

      Richard Dehmel’s poem places two people beneath bare trees and a bright moon. The woman speaks first: “Ich trag ein Kind, und nit von Dir” — “I carry a child, and it is not yours.” Her companion has not yet replied.

    3. Payerbach, September 1899

      In September 1899 Schoenberg spent a holiday in Payerbach with Alexander Zemlinsky and Zemlinsky’s sister Mathilde, who later became Schoenberg’s wife. He composed Verklärte Nacht there in three weeks. The finished manuscript is dated 1 December.

    4. Schoenberg’s announcement, 1902

      In 1902 Schoenberg explained his aim of bringing the poetic programme of orchestral music into chamber music. No singer takes the parts of Dehmel’s speakers. Two violins, two violas and two cellos carry the whole story.

    5. First violin theme in E major

      The music moves to E major, marked Etwas ruhiger (a little calmer). The first violin plays the melody over moving figures in the second viola and first cello. In Schoenberg’s account of the poem, this theme belongs to the woman’s longing for motherhood.

    6. End of the woman’s confession

      The woman’s confession is over. In Dehmel’s poem she walks awkwardly beside the man she loves and looks up at the moon. His reply begins by lifting the burden from her unborn child.

    7. E♭-minor ending, low B♭

      The strings settle in E♭ minor and stop until only a low B♭ remains in the second cello. The first side of the LP ends on that note, at bars 228–229. Side two opens in D major.

    Joins

    1. A violin melody in E major

      The sextet broadens at Breiter and moves into the E-major episode. At Etwas ruhiger the first violin plays the long melody, with the second viola and first cello moving beneath it and the second cello playing the slower bass line.

    2. Second cello’s held B♭, end of side one

      Over E♭ minor the upper strings thin out and stop. The second cello continues to hold B♭ after the other parts have stopped. That B♭ connects the first half to the broad D-major section on the next side.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  2. LP side 2 (bars 229–418)Hollywood String Quartet (Felix Slatkin and Paul Shure, violins; Paul Robyn, viola; Eleanor Aller, cello); Alvin Dinkin, viola; Kurt Reher, cello · 21–22 August 1950
    13:49

    Side 2 of the Hollywood String Quartet’s recording of Verklärte Nacht, Op. 4, for six players, covers bars 229–418. It begins with the D major section, in which the man accepts the woman and her child, and continues through the muted passages to the quiet close.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Section in D major

      Violas and cellos play a broad chord of D major, marked Sehr breit und langsam — very broadly and slowly. The first cello then plays an expressive line. In Dehmel’s poem, the man accepts the child his companion is carrying.

    2. Muted strings in F♯ major

      The strings put on mutes. Quick violin runs cross held harmonics, notes made by touching the strings lightly. The key is F♯ major; in his 1950 programme notes Schoenberg identified this passage with the beauty of the moonlight.

    3. Schoenberg’s programme note, 1950

      In 1950 Schoenberg wrote that the sextet “does not illustrate any action or drama”. He described it as a portrayal of nature and an expression of human emotion, and said that heard in this way it “can make you forget the poem” that inspired it.

    4. Duet for violin and cello

      A duet for violin and cello follows the muted passage. Earlier themes return around it with new accompaniments and are developed. Schoenberg described the duet and these returns in his programme notes of 1950, examples 12–16.

    5. Letter from Dehmel, 12 December 1912

      Dehmel wrote to Schoenberg on 12 December 1912 after hearing the sextet. He had meant to follow his poem through the music, but he soon forgot to and listened only to the six strings.

    6. New themes after the duet

      After the climax of the duet, a new melody enters for what Schoenberg called the “warmth that flows from one of us into the other”. Earlier themes return in reworked form. A second new theme follows for the man’s resolve.

    7. First Vienna performance

      In his 1950 programme note Schoenberg recalled that the sextet “was hissed and caused riots and fist fights” at its first Vienna performance. “But very soon it became very successful.”

    8. Original sextet scoring

      Schoenberg later arranged Verklärte Nacht for string orchestra, adding double basses in 1917 and revising the arrangement in 1943. Here the original six players are kept, and each violin, viola and cello part is played by one person.

    9. Second new theme, the man’s resolution

      In his 1950 programme note Schoenberg described a second new theme as the man’s “dignified resolution”. The warmth between them “will transfigure your child”, so that it becomes “my own”.

    10. Climax and coda

      An ascent leads to the climax, where the second half of the man’s theme returns. This is the broad D-major melody that opened this side, “a man whose generosity is as sublime as his love”. A long coda follows.

    11. Coda, opening theme in major

      In the coda the opening walking theme returns in the major, set in counterpoint with the man’s theme, the principal theme of the fourth section. Dehmel’s final stanza returns to the night walk beneath the same moon.

    12. Hollywood String Quartet recording, 1950

      Capitol’s sleeve called this the first recording of the original sextet version and said it “was prepared under the personal supervision of the composer.” The Hollywood String Quartet made it on 21 and 22 August 1950, joined by Alvin Dinkin and Kurt Reher.

    13. Final bars in D major

      The first violin holds long notes above running figures in the second violin, with violas and cellos filling the space between them. At the soft close of the piece the inner parts keep moving until all six instruments come to rest on D major.

    Joins

    1. Mutes after the pause

      A ritardando and a fermata slow the sextet before the music reaches F♯ major. The violins then play muted runs, and the violas and cellos play harmonics. Before the join, the D-major passage is bowed.

    2. Duet for violin and cello

      Above the accompaniment, a new melody develops into a duet for violin and cello. Schoenberg gives the longer phrases to the two instruments, and the remaining strings keep the accompaniment moving.

    3. The opening walking motif returns

      In the coda the opening walking motif returns in the major and is combined with the man’s answering melody. The six strings bring back phrases from both halves of the piece in the closing pages.

    4. Second violin figures continue to the close

      The first violin plays long notes above rapid figures in the second violin. Viola and cello parts, marked quiet, fill out the harmony. On the last page harmonics return briefly, and the running figures stop on the held D-major chord.

    Open in the Concert Hall
