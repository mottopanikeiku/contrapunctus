Arnold Schoenberg / VI. Modernism

Verklärte Nacht (Transfigured Night)

1899 · Op. 4 · Two violins, two violas and two cellos; later arranged for string orchestra

A woman confesses that she carries another man’s child; her lover accepts them both. Dehmel’s poem leads the six strings from D minor into D major.

2 recordings · 29 min

In the Hall

  1. LP side 1 (bars 1–228)Hollywood String Quartet (Felix Slatkin and Paul Shure, violins; Paul Robyn, viola; Eleanor Aller, cello); Alvin Dinkin, viola; Kurt Reher, cello · 21–22 August 1950
    14:43

    Two people walk through a cold wood; the woman tells her companion she is carrying another man’s child. The Hollywood String Quartet and two guests play the original six-string Verklärte Nacht, with the LP’s first side ending just before his answer.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Footsteps in the low strings

      A second viola and second cello repeat low D. Above them, the first viola and first cello begin a descending phrase, marked immer leise — always quiet. The violins wait before joining the walk.

    2. A child, another man

      Richard Dehmel’s poem puts two people beneath bare trees and a bright moon. “Ich trag ein Kind, und nit von Dir” — “I carry a child, and it is not yours.” The woman speaks; her companion has yet to answer.

    3. September in Payerbach

      In September 1899 Schoenberg spends a holiday in Payerbach with Alexander Zemlinsky and his sister Mathilde. He composes Verklärte Nacht in three weeks. Mathilde will become his wife; the finished manuscript bears the date 1 December.

    4. A poem for six strings

      In 1902 Schoenberg explains his ambition: to bring the poetic storytelling of orchestral music into chamber music. Dehmel’s speakers have no singer here. Two violins, two violas and two cellos tell the whole story.

    5. The violin in E major

      E major opens a gentler passage. The first violin stretches its melody over moving figures in the second viola and first cello. Etwas ruhiger, Schoenberg writes: a little calmer. In his account of the poem, this theme belongs to the woman’s longing for motherhood.

    6. She waits for his answer

      The woman’s confession is over. In Dehmel’s poem she walks awkwardly beside the man she loves and looks up at the moon. His reply begins by lifting the burden from her unborn child.

    7. One low B♭ remains

      The strings settle into E♭ minor and withdraw until a low B♭ remains in the second cello. The LP side ends with that note still awaiting its answer. Side two opens in D major.

    How it is joined

    1. A violin melody in E major

      The sextet broadens, marked Breiter, then enters the gentler E-major episode. At Etwas ruhiger, the first violin carries the long melody while the second viola and first cello keep moving beneath it; the second cello supplies the slower bass.

    2. The second cello keeps B♭

      The upper strings taper away over E♭ minor. The second cello keeps B♭ after the other parts have stopped; that surviving bass note connects the first half to the broad D-major answer on the next side.

  2. LP side 2 (bars 229–418)Hollywood String Quartet (Felix Slatkin and Paul Shure, violins; Paul Robyn, viola; Eleanor Aller, cello); Alvin Dinkin, viola; Kurt Reher, cello · 21–22 August 1950
    13:49

    D major is the man’s answer: he accepts the woman and her child. The second side of the Hollywood String Quartet’s Verklärte Nacht carries the six players from that broad declaration into muted moonlight and a quiet close.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. An answer in D major

      Violas and cellos open a broad D-major chord, marked Sehr breit und langsam — very broadly and slowly. The first cello begins an expressive line. In Dehmel’s poem, the man accepts the child his companion is carrying.

    2. Moonlight under the mutes

      The strings put on mutes. Quick violin runs cross the still, pale notes of harmonics, made by touching the strings lightly. The key is F♯ major; Schoenberg identifies this passage with the beauty of the moonlight.

    3. Nature and feeling, not a plot

      Schoenberg writes in 1950 that the sextet “does not illustrate any action or drama”. It portrays nature and expresses human emotion, and heard that way, he says, it “can make you forget the poem” that inspired it.

    4. Violin and cello together

      A violin and cello duet grows out of the muted passage. Earlier phrases reappear around it, with new accompaniments. The six players have room both for the man’s broad reply and for the woman’s remembered distress.

    5. The poet forgets his poem

      After hearing the sextet, Dehmel writes to Schoenberg on 12 December 1912. He has meant to trace his poem through the music, but soon forgets the task: the six strings hold his attention on their own.

    6. The warmth that flows between them

      After the duet reaches its peak, a new melody carries what Schoenberg calls the “warmth that flows from one of us into the other”. Earlier themes return and are reworked. Then a second new theme arrives for the man’s resolve.

    7. Hissed in Vienna

      Schoenberg reminds his readers in 1950 that the sextet “was hissed and caused riots and fist fights” at its first Vienna performance. “But very soon it became very successful.”

    8. Six players before the orchestra

      Schoenberg later makes Verklärte Nacht into a work for string orchestra, adding double basses in 1917 and revising the arrangement in 1943. Here the original six players remain: every violin, viola and cello part belongs to one person.

    9. The man’s resolve

      Schoenberg hears a second new theme as the man’s “dignified resolution”: the warmth between them “will transfigure your child”, so that it becomes “my own”.

    10. A climb to the man’s theme

      An ascent leads to the climax, where the second half of the man’s theme returns: the broad D-major melody that opened this side, “a man whose generosity is as sublime as his love”. A long coda follows.

    11. The walk in major

      The coda brings back the opening walking theme in the major, with the man’s answering theme set against it. The two people in Dehmel’s poem are still walking beneath the same moon.

    12. Under the composer’s supervision

      Capitol’s sleeve calls this the first recording of the original sextet version and says it “was prepared under the personal supervision of the composer.” The Hollywood String Quartet makes it on 21 and 22 August 1950, joined by Alvin Dinkin and Kurt Reher.

    13. A high violin, still moving

      The first violin holds long notes above the second violin’s running figures. Violas and cellos fill the space between them. Even at the softest end of the piece, the inner parts keep flowing until all six instruments settle into D major.

    How it is joined

    1. Mutes after the pause

      A ritardando and fermata hold back the sextet before F♯ major. The violins begin muted runs; violas and cellos answer with harmonics. The broad, bowed D-major declaration has become a passage of lightly touched strings.

    2. Violin and cello find a duet

      Above the shimmering accompaniment, a new melody develops into a duet for violin and cello. Schoenberg lets the two instruments carry the longer phrases while the remaining strings keep the accompaniment moving.

    3. The opening walk comes back

      The coda recalls the opening walking motif in the major and combines it with the man’s answering melody. The six strings bring phrases from both halves of the piece into the closing pages.

    4. The second violin keeps flowing

      The first violin stretches its notes above the second violin’s rapid figures. Quiet viola and cello parts fill out the harmony; the last page briefly brings back harmonics before the running figures stop on the held D-major chord.
