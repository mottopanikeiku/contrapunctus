Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Kindertotenlieder (Songs on the Death of Children)

1901–04 · Voice and orchestra; poems by Friedrich Rückert

Five settings of poems that Rückert wrote after the deaths of two of his children. Mahler completed them three years before his own daughter died.

5 recordings · 25 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Nun will die Sonn’ so hell aufgeh’nDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    5:13

    “Nun will die Sonn’ so hell aufgeh’n” is the first of the Kindertotenlieder, a song in which the sun rises after a child’s death. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings it with the Berlin Philharmonic under Rudolf Kempe. An oboe and a horn play the opening alone.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Oboe and horn duet

      The oboe rises by small steps while a horn plays below it, and the strings rest. Mahler marks the music Langsam und schwermütig; nicht schleppend, slow and melancholy, without dragging. Fischer-Dieskau sings the first line, about the rising sun, over this wind duet.

    2. Deaths of Rückert’s children

      Friedrich Rückert’s children Luise and Ernst died on 31 December 1833 and 16 January 1834. In his grief he wrote more than four hundred poems, none of them meant for print. Mahler later chose five of them.

    3. Second couplet, return of opening

      The second couplet of Rückert’s poem reads “Das Unglück geschah nur mir allein” (the disaster happened to me alone). The opening tune returns. The flute plays above the voice, and the bassoon repeats its small upward steps.

    4. Third couplet

      The third couplet is addressed to the father himself, who must sink his night into eternal light. Horns take up the earlier vocal melody and the voice enters with a new line. After the words end, the violins continue the rising line.

    5. Footnote in the full score, 1905

      A footnote on the first page of the score asks that the five songs be treated as one inseparable whole. Mahler added that their continuity was to be kept even against the applause that would normally follow a “number”.

    6. Return of the opening melody

      At “Ein Lämplein verlosch in meinem Zelt” (a little lamp went out in my tent), Fischer-Dieskau returns to the opening melody. Oboe and flute bring back their short phrases, and the glockenspiel sounds in the gaps between them.

    7. First performance, Vienna, 1905

      On 29 January 1905 Mahler conducted the first performance of the cycle in the Kleiner Musikvereinssaal in Vienna, with Friedrich Weidemann as soloist. The concert sold out days beforehand, and tickets for the afternoon dress rehearsal were put on sale. A repeat followed on 3 February.

    8. Closing bars, harp and glockenspiel

      The baritone’s final word is “Welt” (world). The harp continues its rocking figure, with soft glockenspiel notes above it. Mahler leaves the last bar empty under a fermata.

    Joins

    1. The oboe returns for the second couplet

      The oboe returns over the harp’s rocking D-minor figure and the glockenspiel notes, which continue. The introduction to the second couplet is shorter than the first. The flute joins the vocal line and the bassoon takes the repeated C♯–D steps below. The singer’s rhythm changes so that the stress falls on “mir” (me).

    2. The horn takes over the vocal tune

      After “allgemein”, the horn extends the closing phrase over low notes in the clarinet and bassoon. The oboe follows with a more urgent phrase. In the next passage the horn plays the first vocal melody while the voice sings a countermelody against it. D minor and D major alternate as the text turns towards eternal light.

    3. Flute and oboe find home

      Marked zurückkehrend — returning — the flute’s running notes soften around the oboe’s opening phrase. Horns hold below; glockenspiel notes punctuate the retreat. The strings fall silent before the last couplet restores the D-minor vocal melody.

  2. II. Nun seh’ ich wohl, warum so dunkle FlammenDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    4:51

    The second song of the Kindertotenlieder sets Rückert’s sonnet about a father who sees the fire of his children’s eyes in the stars. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings it with Rudolf Kempe and the Berlin Philharmonic, recorded in Berlin’s Jesus-Christus-Kirche in June 1955.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Introduction, cellos

      The cellos begin alone, and their melody rises through an octave across the introduction. Winds and pizzicato strings join them. Fischer-Dieskau sings the same rising shape at “Nun seh’ ich wohl” (now I see clearly).

    2. Rückert’s sonnet, “Nun seh’ ich wohl”

      Rückert’s poem is a sonnet, with two groups of four lines followed by two of three. The “dunkle Flammen” (dark flames) are the children’s eyes. In their remembered looks the father searches for what he failed to understand while they were alive.

    3. Visit from C. F. Kahnt, Leipzig, 1904

      Mahler was in Leipzig to conduct his Third Symphony when the owner of C. F. Kahnt called on him on 28 November 1904. He wrote to Alma straight away about a publisher “eager to acquire my new Lieder and Ballads”. Kahnt published the cycle the following year.

    4. D major, rehearsal 3

      At rehearsal 3 the music moves to D major over harp arpeggios. The father imagines the shining eyes saying “Wir möchten nah dir immer bleiben gerne”. Mahler marks the voice warm.

    5. Varied return, final stanzas

      From rehearsal 4, earlier phrases return, varied, around the baritone. In the poem fate denies the children’s wish, and the speaker looks ahead to nights when those eyes will be stars. The flute plays above the viola and cello lines.

    6. Recording, Berlin, June 1955

      Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau and Rudolf Kempe recorded the cycle with the Berlin Philharmonic on 20 and 21 June 1955 in the Jesus-Christus-Kirche. Three years earlier, in London’s Kingsway Hall, Fischer-Dieskau had recorded Mahler’s Lieder eines fahrenden Gesellen with Wilhelm Furtwängler.

    7. Closing harp arpeggio

      After Fischer-Dieskau’s last words, the harp rises through an A♭ seventh chord, which resolves to C minor. Flutes, clarinets and bassoon hold the chord as the strings stop.

    Joins

    1. The harp introduces D major

      A ritardando slows “mit eurem”. At Tempo I the key signature changes to two sharps and the harp plays a spread D-major chord. Cellos and basses play D pizzicato below it, and flute, clarinet and bassoon hold sustained notes above. The voice reaches “Leuchten” (shining), marked warm.

    2. D major turns through A7 to G minor

      The D-major passage moves through A7 into G minor. The flute plays the high line and the violas hold sustained notes below. After the vocal phrase the cellos change from pizzicato to arco, and horn and bassoon carry fragments into the next stanza.

    3. Harp arpeggio to the closing C-minor chord

      After the voice finishes, the strings play shorter, softer phrases. Over plucked low strings, the harp plays upward through an A♭ seventh chord, a chord that could lead to D♭. The harmony resolves to C minor. Flutes, clarinets and bassoon hold the last chord, marked morendo (dying away).

  3. III. Wenn dein MütterleinDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    4:34

    “Wenn dein Mütterlein” is the third of the Kindertotenlieder. In it a mother comes through the door, and the father looks down for the little face beside her. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings it with Rudolf Kempe and the Berlin Philharmonic, and the orchestra in this song has no violins.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, cor anglais and bassoons

      Cor anglais and bassoons play above plucked cellos. Mahler marks the opening “Schwer, dumpf” — heavy, muffled. When Fischer-Dieskau enters, the orchestra has no violins, and they are left out for the whole song.

    2. Rückert’s text, first stanza

      In Rückert’s poem the father turns towards his wife, then looks below her face, “näher nach der Schwelle” (nearer the threshold). That is where the daughter used to appear when she came in beside her mother.

    3. Children’s-song melody and accompaniment

      The voice line follows the pattern of a children’s song, and at times of a chanted counting rhyme. Beneath it the orchestra plays linear parts at twice the speed of the voice. These parts recall the first song.

    4. Second stanza, rehearsal 3

      The cor anglais begins again, now over harp and pizzicato double bass. In the second stanza the mother carries a candle, and the child is pictured entering the room behind her. In Fischer-Dieskau’s closing lament, she is called a light extinguished too soon.

    5. Venues and string section, 1905

      Mahler preferred small rooms for these songs. In 1905 he chose the Kleiner Musikvereinssaal for the Vienna performances and then the Stephaniesaal in Graz. His string section had about ten first violins at most, and this song has no violins.

    6. Alma Mahler on the <i>Kindertotenlieder</i>

      Alma Mahler could not understand how her husband could bewail the deaths of children within an hour of kissing his own, who were “in the best of health and spirits”. She exclaimed: “For Heaven’s sake, don’t tempt Providence!” Mahler completed the cycle in the summer of 1904.

    7. Return of the opening, closing bars

      After the voice has finished, the cor anglais repeats the opening. The cellos now play arco, with the horns close beside them. The song ends on a G major chord, the dominant of C minor.

    Joins

    1. End of first stanza into reprise

      The first stanza descends to its low “Töchterlein” and closes in C minor. After the ritardando, Wie zu Anfang restores the opening tempo and the cor anglais tune. Harp and plucked double bass replace the cello pulse. The bass clarinet holds one note across the join.

    2. Bowed cellos replace the plucked pulse

      At Wieder wie zu Anfang the cor anglais returns, with horns and bowed cellos beneath it. The wind phrases lengthen and slow. Harp and low strings support the final chord, G major, the dominant of C minor, held under fermatas.

  4. IV. Oft denk’ ich, sie sind nur ausgegangenDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    3:06

    “Oft denk’ ich, sie sind nur ausgegangen” is the fourth song of the Kindertotenlieder, and its text says the children have only gone out and will soon come home. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings it with the Berlin Philharmonic under Rudolf Kempe.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, horn and violins

      The horn and violins play above short cello figures, with harp and pizzicato bass on the beat. In the first lines, sung by Fischer-Dieskau, the children have only gone out, and the day is fine. Mahler marks the music Ruhig bewegt, ohne zu eilen, calmly moving, without haste.

    2. Second stanza, baritone

      “Ja wohl, sie sind nur ausgegangen”: yes, surely, they have only gone out. At rehearsal 3, a tempo, the voice returns to the thought. Much of the orchestra is silent, and clarinets and bassoon accompany the baritone’s familiar phrase.

    3. Separate publication, 10 May 1905

      On 10 May 1905 this became the only song of the five to appear by itself, in a version for voice and piano printed as the music supplement of the Neue Zeitschrift für Musik. Mahler had wanted the five kept together. Kahnt, the periodical’s publisher, issued the whole cycle later that year.

    4. Last stanza, “vorausgegangen”

      In the last stanza “ausgegangen” (gone out) becomes “vorausgegangen” (gone ahead), so the parents will follow the children. Fischer-Dieskau sings the familiar tune over a pulse in the harp and a moving line in the cellos.

    5. Final vocal phrase and postlude

      The voice sings the last line, about the sunny heights, and the instruments complete the phrase. The horn plays softly and the harp resumes its steady notes. The strings descend to a closing chord marked “morendo” (dying away).

    Joins

    1. Clarinets take the phrase at “Ja wohl”

      A short poco ritardando ends the first stanza. At a tempo the strings drop out and the clarinets take the moving phrase, with the bassoon below. The voice starts the tune again on “Ja wohl”. The harp does not play at the join.

    2. Third stanza: a tempo with harp

      The third stanza begins a tempo, Nicht eilen (in tempo, without hurrying). The harp plays steady notes, and bowed cellos continue the small running figure. Above them the tune returns in the clarinets. The voice is marked schlicht (plain) for the changed words “Sie sind uns nur vorausgegangen”.

  5. V. In diesem Wetter, in diesem BrausDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    6:49

    “In diesem Wetter, in diesem Braus” is the fifth and last song of Kindertotenlieder: a father rages at the weather, then sings his children to sleep, and the storm music gives way to a lullaby with celesta. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings with the Berlin Philharmonic under Rudolf Kempe.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening of the storm

      The winds play short phrases, the cellos tremble and the harp strikes accented chords. Much of the storm begins softly. Mahler marked the passage “ruhelos schmerzvollem Ausdruck” (restless, painful expression) before Fischer-Dieskau has sung a word.

    2. Opening march in D minor

      The storm is set as a march in D minor. Its key and melodic style recall the first movement of Mahler’s Third Symphony, written in 1895–96. No other song in the cycle opens with a march.

    3. First stanza, Rückert’s text

      The father in Rückert’s poem says repeatedly that he would never have sent his children out in such weather. He then corrects himself: “man hat sie getragen hinaus” — they were carried out. “Ich durfte nichts dazu sagen” — I was allowed no say in it.

    4. Scoring of the fifth song

      Only this last song calls for a piccolo, a contrabassoon and a third and fourth horn. The cycle has no trumpets or trombones at all.

    5. Transition to the final stanza

      A high piccolo note and the small bell continue as the storm music slows. The string phrases fall in shorter waves. Mahler marks “Allmählich langsamer” (gradually slower) across the bridge into the final stanza.

    6. Closing lullaby in D major

      The final section is marked “Langsam. Wie ein Wiegenlied” — slow, like a lullaby. Fischer-Dieskau repeats the words about the weather, now over celesta and rocking strings in D major. The children rest “als wie in der Mutter Haus” — as in their mother’s house.

    7. Remark to Guido Adler, 1907

      The cycle was finished in 1904. In July 1907 Mahler’s elder daughter, Maria, died of scarlet fever and diphtheria. He told Guido Adler that he had imagined “a child of mine had died”; having truly lost a daughter, he said, he “could not have written these songs any more.”

    8. Orchestral postlude after the voice

      The baritone ends on “Haus”. The violas continue their rocking figure, and bassoon and horns play soft phrases above divided cellos.

    9. Closing bars in D major

      The celesta’s short figures descend through the last bars. Low strings, horns and clarinets hold a D major chord marked ppp, with the music marked morendo and the celesta gänzlich verklingend.

    10. Cello allusion to the Third Symphony

      In the last five bars (bars 129–133) a cello melody alludes to the first theme of the Third Symphony’s finale, a movement titled “What love tells me”. The musicologist Andreas Dorschel took it as the cycle’s last word: “that death is powerful, yet love is even stronger.”

    Joins

    1. Piccolo high D at rehearsal 8

      At rehearsal 8 the piccolo holds a high D above the orchestra as it falls back. The small bell repeats its notes over harp chords and low bassoon. At Allmählich langsamer the falling string phrases slow down, and the D-minor storm music thins out.

    2. D minor turns to D major

      The piccolo holds through the change to a key signature of two sharps, and D minor becomes D major. Under the marking Langsam. Wie ein Wiegenlied, celesta and soft strings take up the rocking figure. The low strings play little around the voice when it re-enters softly.

    3. The violas continue after “Haus”

      The voice ends on “Haus” and the violas carry on with their rocking figure. Divided cellos hold the bass. Horn and bassoon lines enter above, marked weich (soft). The D-major postlude follows on from the lullaby without a pause.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  5. V. In diesem Wetter, in diesem BrausMarian Anderson, contralto; San Francisco Symphony Orchestra; Pierre Monteux, conductor · 1950
    6:11

    Marian Anderson sings “In diesem Wetter, in diesem Braus”, the storm song and lullaby that end Kindertotenlieder, with Pierre Monteux and the San Francisco Symphony. After the parent’s repeated protest, the song turns to a lullaby of shelter, accompanied by celesta and rocking strings.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, harp and cello tremolos

      The harp plays sharp accents while flutes and clarinets pass short phrases over the muted strings, with the cellos in tremolo beneath. Mahler marked the opening Mit ruhelos schmerzvollem Ausdruck (with restless, painful expression). Anderson’s part is set above this texture.

    2. Opening march in D minor

      The song opens with a march in D minor, the only song in the cycle to do so. Its key and melodic style recall the first movement of Mahler’s Third Symphony, written in 1895–96.

    3. The fears that come too late

      Rückert’s parent worries that the children might fall ill, then that they might die tomorrow. Each fear collapses as soon as it is spoken. “Das sind nun eitle Gedanken” — those are idle thoughts now. The children have already been carried away.

    4. Scoring of the fifth song

      Only this last song calls for a piccolo, a contrabassoon and a third and fourth horn. The cycle has no trumpets or trombones.

    5. Rehearsal 8, piccolo and Glöckchen

      At rehearsal 8 the piccolo holds a high note above the orchestra, and a small bell (Glöckchen) sounds in the gaps. Under the marking Allmählich langsamer, the string phrases become slower and shorter. This bridge leads from the storm music into Anderson’s final stanza.

    6. Final stanza, “Wie ein Wiegenlied”

      Mahler marks the final stanza “Wie ein Wiegenlied” — like a lullaby. Anderson repeats the weather words of the opening over celesta and rocking strings. In Rückert’s text the children now rest as safely as in their mother’s house, “von Gottes Hand bedecket” — covered by God’s hand.

    7. Mahler to Guido Adler, 1907

      Mahler finished the cycle in 1904. In July 1907 his elder daughter, Maria, died of scarlet fever and diphtheria. He told Guido Adler that he had imagined “a child of mine had died”, and that after truly losing a daughter he “could not have written these songs any more.”

    8. Orchestral postlude

      The violas continue the cradle rhythm after Anderson’s last words. Bassoon and horns play softly over divided cellos, and the celesta has the short descending figures of the last page. The final chord fades, Morendo, under a fermata.

    9. Performance instruction in the score, 1905

      In a footnote on the first page of the score, Mahler described the five songs as “ein einheitliches, untrennbares Ganzes” — one unified, inseparable whole. He asked that their continuity be preserved, including through the pauses between songs, and that there be no applause.

    10. Cello allusion to the Third Symphony

      In the last five bars (bars 129–133) a cello melody alludes to the first theme of the Third Symphony’s finale, a movement titled “What love tells me”. The musicologist Andreas Dorschel took the allusion to state the cycle’s conclusion, “that death is powerful, yet love is even stronger.”

    Joins

    1. The piccolo holds above the bell

      The piccolo holds a note, written D, above repeated taps on the small bell. Harp chords and low bassoon notes continue below. Under Allmählich langsamer (gradually slower), the strings play falling figures that grow smaller with each wave, and the lullaby follows. The passage comes at rehearsal 8 of the Kahnt full score, pp. 78–79.

    2. Celesta entry at the lullaby

      The piccolo holds across the change from D minor to D major. At Langsam. Wie ein Wiegenlied, the celesta joins the rocking strings, and the low strings rest beneath Anderson’s quiet return. The melody keeps the opening words about the weather.

    3. Horns take over the lullaby

      Anderson’s last “Haus” ends while the violas continue their rocking figure. Divided cellos sustain beneath them. Horn and bassoon enter with soft phrases marked weich and carry the major-key lullaby into the instrumental ending without a break.
