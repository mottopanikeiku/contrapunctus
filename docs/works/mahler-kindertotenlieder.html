Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Kindertotenlieder (Songs on the Death of Children)

1901–04 · Voice and orchestra; poems by Friedrich Rückert

Five settings of poems Rückert wrote after losing two children. Completed three years before Mahler lost his own daughter.

5 recordings · 25 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Nun will die Sonn’ so hell aufgeh’nDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    5:13

    The sun rises after a child’s death, as though nothing has happened. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings the opening Kindertotenlied with Rudolf Kempe and the Berlin Philharmonic; an oboe and horn begin it alone.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Oboe above a horn

      The oboe edges upwards by small steps; a horn answers below it. Mahler marks the music slow and melancholy. The strings wait. Fischer-Dieskau’s first line places the rising sun inside this spare wind duet.

    2. “Nur mir allein”

      “Das Unglück geschah nur mir allein” — the disaster happened to me alone. Rückert puts the whole distance between the father and the bright morning into those last three words. The opening tune returns, with the flute above the voice and the bassoon repeating its small upward steps.

    3. Night inside the father

      The third couplet speaks to the father himself: he must sink his night into eternal light. Horns take up the earlier vocal melody, and the voice answers with a new line. The violins carry the climb onwards after the words run out.

    4. The extinguished lamp

      “Ein Lämplein verlosch in meinem Zelt” — a little lamp went out in my tent. Fischer-Dieskau returns to the opening melody with this small domestic image. Oboe and flute bring back their short phrases, and the glockenspiel sounds through the gaps.

    5. Harp and small bells

      The baritone’s final word is “Welt” — world. The harp keeps its little rocking figure going, with soft glockenspiel notes above it. Mahler leaves the last bar empty under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The oboe starts again

      The harp’s rocking D-minor figure and glockenspiel notes continue beneath the oboe’s return. The second couplet shortens the introduction: flute joins the vocal line, while the bassoon takes the repeated C♯–D steps below. The singer’s rhythm changes to put the stress on “mir” — me.

    2. The horn takes the tune

      After “allgemein”, the horn stretches the closing phrase over low clarinet and bassoon notes. The oboe answers more urgently. In the passage that follows, the horn takes the first vocal tune while the singer moves against it; D minor and D major alternate as the words turn towards eternal light.

    3. Flute and oboe find home

      Marked zurückkehrend — returning — the flute’s running notes soften around the oboe’s opening phrase. Horns hold below; glockenspiel notes punctuate the retreat. The strings fall silent before the last couplet restores the D-minor vocal melody.

  2. II. Nun seh’ ich wohl, warum so dunkle FlammenDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    4:51

    A father remembers the fire in his children’s eyes and sees it again in the stars. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings Rückert’s sonnet with Rudolf Kempe and the Berlin Philharmonic, recorded in Berlin’s Jesus-Christus-Kirche in June 1955.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The cellos climb

      The cellos begin alone, their melody climbing through an octave across the introduction. Winds and plucked strings gather around them. Fischer-Dieskau takes up the same rising shape for “Nun seh’ ich wohl” — now I see clearly.

    2. Fourteen lines about eyes

      Rückert writes a sonnet: two groups of four lines, then two of three. Its “dunkle Flammen” — dark flames — are the children’s eyes. The father searches their remembered looks for something he failed to understand while they were alive.

    3. The children answer

      D major opens beneath the harp’s sweeping arpeggios. The father imagines what the shining eyes were saying: “Wir möchten nah dir immer bleiben gerne” — we would gladly stay close to you always. Mahler marks the voice warm.

    4. Eyes become stars

      Fate refuses the children’s wish. Earlier phrases return around the baritone, and the poem looks ahead to nights when those eyes will be stars. The flute sings above the darker viola and cello lines.

    5. The harp climbs away

      After Fischer-Dieskau’s last words, the harp climbs through a chord that seems to promise another key. It settles into C minor. Flutes, clarinets and bassoon hold the chord as the strings disappear.

    How it is joined

    1. The harp opens D major

      A ritardando holds back “mit eurem”. At Tempo I, the key signature changes to two sharps and the harp spreads a D-major chord. Plucked cellos and basses sound D below; flute, clarinet and bassoon sustain above. The voice opens out on “Leuchten” — shining — marked warm.

    2. The bright answer darkens

      The D-major passage moves through A7 into G minor as the children’s imagined wish meets its refusal. The flute carries the high line; violas sustain below. After the vocal phrase, cellos change from plucking to bowing, and horn and bassoon carry fragments into the next stanza.

    3. An unexpected C-minor landing

      The strings shorten and soften their phrases after the voice finishes. Over plucked low strings, the harp climbs through an A♭ seventh chord, which could lead to D♭. It resolves into C minor instead. Flutes, clarinets and bassoon hold the last chord, marked morendo — dying away.

  3. III. Wenn dein MütterleinDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    4:34

    A mother comes through the door; the father looks down for the little face beside her. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings this scene with Rudolf Kempe and the Berlin Philharmonic, in a song whose orchestra has no violins.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. An orchestra without violins

      Cor anglais and bassoons wind around one another above plucked cellos. Mahler marks the opening “Schwer, dumpf” — heavy, muffled. Fischer-Dieskau joins an orchestra stripped of violins for the whole song.

    2. At the child’s height

      Rückert’s father turns towards his wife, then looks below her face: “näher nach der Schwelle” — nearer the threshold. His eyes still expect the daughter who used to come in beside her. The room remembers a habit the father cannot stop.

    3. The doorway again

      The cor anglais begins again, now with harp and plucked double bass underneath. In the second stanza the mother carries a candle, and the child seems to slip into the room behind her. Fischer-Dieskau’s closing lament calls her a light extinguished too soon.

    4. The cellos take their bows

      The cor anglais recalls the opening after the voice has finished. The cellos now bow their notes, with horns close beside them. The song comes to rest on G major, the chord that would normally lead back to C minor.

    How it is joined

    1. A new bass beneath the doorway

      The first stanza descends to its low “Töchterlein” and closes in C minor. After the ritardando, Wie zu Anfang restores the opening pace and cor anglais tune. Harp and plucked double bass replace the cello pulse; a held bass-clarinet note crosses the join.

    2. Bowed cellos replace the plucked pulse

      Wieder wie zu Anfang brings the cor anglais back, with horns and bowed cellos beneath it. The wind phrases lengthen and slow. Harp and low strings support a final G-major chord, C minor’s dominant, held under fermatas.

  4. IV. Oft denk’ ich, sie sind nur ausgegangenDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    3:06

    The children have only gone for a walk; soon they will come home. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau sings the fourth Kindertotenlied with Rudolf Kempe and the Berlin Philharmonic, as the same tune slowly changes what those comforting words mean.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A walk in the sunshine

      The horn and violins sing above little cello figures, with harp and plucked bass marking the steps. Fischer-Dieskau’s first thought is a familiar one: the children are out somewhere, and the day is fine. Mahler marks the music calmly moving, without haste.

    2. Saying it again

      “Ja wohl, sie sind nur ausgegangen” — yes, surely, they have only gone out. The voice tries the thought again. Much of the orchestra falls silent, leaving clarinets and bassoon around the baritone’s familiar phrase.

    3. One word changes

      “Ausgegangen” becomes “vorausgegangen”: gone out becomes gone ahead. The children will stay where they are; the parents will catch up with them. Fischer-Dieskau sings the familiar tune over the harp’s pulse and the cellos’ moving line.

    4. The distant sunny heights

      The voice reaches the sunny heights of the last line, then the instruments complete the phrase. The horn sings softly, the harp resumes its steady notes, and the strings sink into a closing chord marked “morendo” — dying away.

    How it is joined

    1. Clarinets carry the second promise

      A brief poco ritardando closes the first stanza. At a tempo, the string parts empty out and clarinets take the moving phrase, with bassoon below. The voice restarts the tune on “Ja wohl”; the harp is silent at the join.

    2. The harp restores the pulse

      The third stanza begins a tempo, Nicht eilen — in tempo, without hurrying. The harp marks steady notes while bowed cellos keep the small running figure moving. Clarinets recall the tune above, and the voice is marked schlicht — plain — for the changed words “Sie sind uns nur vorausgegangen”.

  5. V. In diesem Wetter, in diesem BrausDietrich Fischer-Dieskau, baritone; Berlin Philharmonic; Rudolf Kempe, conductor · 20–21 June 1955
    6:49

    A father rages at the weather, then sings his children to sleep. Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau, Rudolf Kempe and the Berlin Philharmonic close Kindertotenlieder with a storm that leaves behind a celesta and a lullaby.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The storm starts quietly

      The winds dart in short phrases, cellos tremble, and the harp strikes accented chords. Much of this storm begins softly. Mahler asks for “ruhelos schmerzvollem Ausdruck” — restless, painful expression — before Fischer-Dieskau has sung a word.

    2. Carried out of the house

      Rückert’s father keeps saying he would never send his children out in such weather. Then comes the unbearable correction: “man hat sie getragen hinaus” — they were carried out. “Ich durfte nichts dazu sagen” — I was allowed no say in it.

    3. A piccolo above the storm

      A high piccolo note and the small bell remain as the storm loses speed. String phrases fall away in shorter waves. Mahler writes “Allmählich langsamer” — gradually slower — across the bridge into the final stanza.

    4. The father sings a lullaby

      “Langsam. Wie ein Wiegenlied” — slow, like a lullaby. Fischer-Dieskau sings the weather words again, now over celesta and rocking strings in D major. The children rest “als wie in der Mutter Haus” — as in their mother’s house.

    5. The cradle keeps rocking

      The baritone finishes on the mother’s house. The violas keep rocking; bassoon and horns begin their own soft phrases above divided cellos. Mahler lets the orchestra stay with the sleeping children after the parent falls silent.

    6. D major, almost gone

      The celesta’s little figures descend through the last bars. Low strings, horns and clarinets settle into D major, with the final chord marked as softly as the instruments can sustain it. The cycle that began before sunrise ends here.

    How it is joined

    1. A high D survives the storm

      At rehearsal 8 a high D in the piccolo hangs above the retreating orchestra. The small bell repeats its notes, with harp chords and low bassoon underneath. Allmählich langsamer slows the falling string phrases until the D-minor storm has almost emptied out.

    2. D minor turns to D major

      The piccolo holds across the change to a two-sharp key signature. D minor becomes D major. Celesta and soft strings take up the rocking figure under Langsam. Wie ein Wiegenlied; the low strings leave space around the singer’s quiet return.

    3. The violas continue after “Haus”

      The voice ends on “Haus” while the violas continue their rocking figure. Divided cellos sustain the bass; horn and bassoon lines enter above, marked weich — soft. The D-major postlude continues the lullaby without a pause.

  1. I. Nun will die Sonn’ so hell aufgeh’nMarian Anderson, contralto; San Francisco Symphony Orchestra; Pierre Monteux, conductor · 1950
    4:40

    Marian Anderson sings the first Kindertotenlied with Pierre Monteux and the San Francisco Symphony in 1950. Between the oboe and horn at the start and the small bells at the end, Rückert’s father watches a morning that has no knowledge of his loss.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Two winds, then Anderson

      A horn moves slowly beneath the oboe’s questioning little steps. Anderson joins them with the sun’s return: “Nun will die Sonn’ so hell aufgeh’n” — now the sun will rise so brightly. The first page has almost nothing between the contralto and these two wind instruments.

    2. The sun belongs to everyone

      The second couplet sets the father’s “mir allein” — me alone — against a sun that shines on everyone. Mahler brings the opening back with a flute beside Anderson’s line and the bassoon below. The melody now puts its stress on the bereaved person.

    3. The horn remembers

      The horn carries the melody the voice sang earlier. Anderson has a new line against it, and the oboe presses upwards. At “ew’ge Licht” — eternal light — Mahler lets the phrase spread through the violins and outlast the sung words.

    4. A lamp in the tent

      The last couplet brings the loss down to the size of a lamp: “Ein Lämplein verlosch in meinem Zelt” — a little lamp went out in my tent. The opening melody returns, with oboe and flute close around the contralto.

    5. Small bells after the voice

      Anderson’s last phrase salutes the world’s joyful light. Harp and glockenspiel keep going after the voice stops. The score ends with an entire bar of silence, held under fermatas.

    How it is joined

    1. The oboe returns over the harp

      The oboe begins its opening phrase again while the harp rocks on D-minor notes. Glockenspiel taps continue above. The shortened introduction leads to a changed vocal rhythm, with the flute doubling Anderson and the bassoon repeating C♯–D below.

    2. Horn and contralto exchange phrases

      The horn prolongs the second couplet’s closing phrase over low clarinet and bassoon. Oboe figures climb into the next passage. The horn then takes the earlier vocal tune, while the contralto answers it and the harmony moves between D minor and D major.

    3. The oboe’s first phrase, softly

      The flute softens its running notes around the oboe’s returning melody. Horns sustain below, and the glockenspiel marks the spaces between phrases. Mahler writes zurückkehrend — returning — before the last couplet takes up the D-minor tune again.

  2. II. Nun seh’ ich wohl, warum so dunkle FlammenMarian Anderson, contralto; San Francisco Symphony Orchestra; Pierre Monteux, conductor · 1950
    3:58

    Marian Anderson takes the parent’s part in this song about the last looks of dying children, with Pierre Monteux and the San Francisco Symphony. A rising cello phrase becomes the voice’s first attempt to make sense of those eyes.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Cellos before the contralto

      The cellos’ upward phrase begins quietly and gathers winds and plucked strings around it. Anderson’s opening words inherit that climb. Mahler writes “Ruhig, nicht schleppend” — calm, without dragging.

    2. A look understood too late

      “Doch ahnt’ ich nicht” — yet I did not suspect. Rückert’s parent remembers a brightness in the children’s eyes that now seems like a farewell. The poem is a sonnet, its fourteen lines ending far from the room, among the stars.

    3. A wish to stay

      Anderson now gives the children their imagined reply: they want to remain close. The harp opens into broad D-major arpeggios, with soft winds above. Mahler’s direction for the voice is simply “warm”.

    4. The stars to come

      The reply ends with fate’s refusal. The flute carries a high, quiet line while viola and cello phrases move below Anderson’s voice. The last stanza turns the children’s eyes into stars shining on future nights.

    5. Winds after Anderson

      The harp rises after the contralto’s final phrase. Its chord takes an unexpected turn into C minor; flutes, clarinets and bassoon sustain while the strings fade. The score’s last instruction is “morendo” — dying away.

    How it is joined

    1. D major beneath “Leuchten”

      The voice holds back before “Leuchten” — shining. At Tempo I, the harp opens a D-major arpeggio above plucked D notes in cellos and basses. Flute, clarinet and bassoon sustain, and Anderson’s line broadens under the direction warm.

    2. The wish meets its refusal

      D major darkens through A7 into G minor. The flute continues above the words about fate, with soft violas underneath. The cellos put bow to string after their plucked notes; horn and bassoon recall earlier phrases between the vocal sentences.

    3. The harp turns towards C minor

      After the voice, a rising harp figure outlines an A♭ seventh chord over plucked low strings. The expected D♭ resolution turns into C minor. The strings fall away, leaving flutes, clarinets and bassoon to sustain the final chord under morendo.

  3. III. Wenn dein MütterleinMarian Anderson, contralto; San Francisco Symphony Orchestra; Pierre Monteux, conductor · 1950
    4:12

    Marian Anderson sings of a mother entering with a candle and a child who seems, for an instant, to follow her. Pierre Monteux’s San Francisco players accompany her with cor anglais and low strings; Mahler leaves the violins out entirely.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Low strings around Anderson

      Plucked cellos mark the pulse beneath cor anglais and bassoons. Their lines keep moving as Anderson enters with the mother at the door. The violins are absent throughout; the score asks for a heavy, muffled sound.

    2. The missing little face

      The father’s glance falls where his daughter’s face should be: “dein lieb Gesichtchen” — your dear little face. Rückert fills the poem with diminutives, from “Mütterlein” to “Töchterlein”. The family’s everyday words survive the child.

    3. A candle through the door

      The opening tune returns above harp and double bass. Anderson’s second stanza adds the candle’s glimmer and the imagined child slipping into the room. Mahler repeats words and lengthens syllables so that this shorter stanza occupies the first stanza’s musical shape.

    4. Cor anglais without words

      After Anderson’s lament, the cor anglais sings its opening phrase once more. Horns and bowed cellos replace the earlier plucked steps. The ending rests on G major, leaving the song’s C-minor home a chord away.

    How it is joined

    1. The cor anglais returns with harp

      The descending vocal phrase closes in C minor. A ritardando leads into Wie zu Anfang — as at the beginning — and the cor anglais recovers its tune. This time harp and plucked double bass carry the pulse, while the bass clarinet sustains across the barline.

    2. The plucked steps become sustained notes

      The cor anglais returns under Wieder wie zu Anfang, surrounded by horn lines and bowed cellos. A ritardando lengthens its last descending phrase. Harp and low strings settle on G, and the final G-major chord hangs under fermatas.

  4. IV. Oft denk’ ich, sie sind nur ausgegangenMarian Anderson, contralto; San Francisco Symphony Orchestra; Pierre Monteux, conductor · 1950
    3:03

    Marian Anderson sings a parent’s repeated assurance that the children will soon be home, with Pierre Monteux and the San Francisco Symphony. Harp and cellos keep walking beneath a tune whose final stanza admits that the children have gone ahead for good.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Horn above the walking bass

      Harp and plucked double bass keep a steady tread beneath the horn and violins. The cellos have a quicker, winding figure of their own. Anderson’s first stanza imagines the children taking a long walk on a beautiful day.

    2. “O sei nicht bang”

      “O sei nicht bang” — oh, do not be afraid. Rückert repeats the reassurance, rearranging the same few words. Anderson sings the returning tune with clarinets and bassoon close beside her; the strings leave more space around the voice.

    3. Gone ahead of us

      The last stanza changes the promise. “Sie sind uns nur vorausgegangen” — they have only gone ahead of us. Anderson carries the same melody into this new thought, with harp notes underneath and the cellos still turning through their short figures.

    4. The horn’s last phrase

      The sunny heights of the poem draw the voice upwards before the little instrumental ending. Horn and bassoon continue after Anderson, with harp and soft strings. The score marks the last chord “morendo” — dying away.

    How it is joined

    1. The strings leave room for clarinets

      Poco ritardando slows the first stanza’s ending. The return to a tempo leaves the harp and most strings silent. Clarinets carry the moving line over bassoon notes as Anderson starts the promise again: “Ja wohl”.

    2. Cellos keep walking beneath the words

      The harp’s repeated notes return with the bowed cello figure. Clarinets answer above as the voice begins the last stanza, marked schlicht — plain. Mahler restores the tempo but adds Nicht eilen, keeping the words about going ahead at the earlier unhurried pace.

  5. V. In diesem Wetter, in diesem BrausMarian Anderson, contralto; San Francisco Symphony Orchestra; Pierre Monteux, conductor · 1950
    6:11

    Marian Anderson sings the storm and the lullaby that end Kindertotenlieder, with Pierre Monteux and the San Francisco Symphony. The parent’s repeated protest finally becomes a song of shelter, accompanied by celesta and rocking strings.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Restless winds, trembling cellos

      The harp strikes sharply while flutes and clarinets toss short phrases across the muted strings. The cellos tremble beneath them. Mahler’s opening direction asks for restless pain; Anderson’s part must speak through this weather.

    2. The fears that come too late

      Rückert’s parent worries that the children might fall ill, then that they might die tomorrow. Each fear collapses as soon as it is spoken. “Das sind nun eitle Gedanken” — those are idle thoughts now. The children have already been carried away.

    3. The bell in the clearing

      The piccolo holds high above the orchestra, and a small bell sounds in the gaps. The string phrases grow slower and shorter. This bridge carries Anderson’s final stanza out of the storm.

    4. “Wie ein Wiegenlied”

      “Wie ein Wiegenlied” — like a lullaby. Anderson returns to the same weather words over celesta and rocking strings. The children now rest as safely as in their mother’s house, “von Gottes Hand bedecket” — covered by God’s hand.

    5. After Anderson’s last words

      The violas keep the cradle moving after Anderson finishes. Bassoon and horns sing softly over divided cellos, and the celesta has the small descending figures of the last page. The final chord dwindles under a fermata.

    6. Five songs, no applause

      On the score’s first page, Mahler calls the five songs “ein einheitliches, untrennbares Ganzes” — one unified, inseparable whole. He asks for their continuity to be preserved, including through the silence between songs, without applause.

    How it is joined

    1. The piccolo holds above the bell

      A held piccolo note stands above the small bell’s repeated taps. Harp chords and low bassoon notes remain below. The strings fall in diminishing waves under Allmählich langsamer — gradually slower — until the storm leaves room for the lullaby.

    2. The celesta takes up the cradle

      The piccolo sustains across the change from minor to major. At Langsam. Wie ein Wiegenlied, the celesta joins the rocking strings, with the low strings resting beneath Anderson’s quiet return. The melody keeps the opening words about the weather.

    3. Horns take over the lullaby

      Anderson’s last “Haus” ends while the violas continue rocking. Divided cellos sustain beneath them. Horn and bassoon begin soft phrases marked weich, carrying the major-key lullaby into the instrumental ending without a break.
