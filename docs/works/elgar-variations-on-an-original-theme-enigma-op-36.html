Edward Elgar / IV. Fin de siècle

Variations on an Original Theme “Enigma”, Op. 36

1898–99 · Op. 36 · Orchestra; optional organ in the revised finale

Alice opens the gallery of friends, and Elgar closes it with his own portrait. Jaeger persuaded him to expand the ending after the first performance; the revised finale brings in an optional organ.

14 recordings · 26 min

In the Hall

  1. Theme and I. “C.A.E.” — Andante; L’istesso tempoHallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    3:20

    Elgar’s hesitant G-minor theme flowers into a portrait of his wife, Alice, without a break. The Hallé and John Barbirolli play the opening pair of his Enigma Variations.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A rest before the phrase

      Only strings. The first violins begin after a crotchet rest, with two short notes and two longer ones; the next bar reverses their order. These small hesitations give the G-minor tune its questioning voice.

    2. Alice at the piano

      On 21 October 1898, after a day’s teaching, Elgar sits down at the piano. A tune catches Alice’s attention. He begins trying out versions of it as their friends might play them: the evening’s amusement becomes fourteen variations.

    3. Alice keeps the pulse

      Caroline Alice Elgar’s portrait keeps the theme’s pace: L’istesso tempo. Oboes and bassoons sing together in G minor, while violins and cellos weave quick notes around them. The melody has company now.

    4. Ruling the bar lines

      Alice’s support takes practical forms. She rules bar lines on Edward’s score paper and walks miles in all weathers to post manuscripts and proofs. A general’s daughter, she marries the music teacher in 1889 against her relatives’ wishes.

    5. Violins climb into silence

      The brass withdraws and the strings slow. In the closing bars the violins send one last run upwards. The strings settle on a G-major chord, marked ppp, under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. Alice takes up the theme

      L’istesso tempo keeps the pulse unchanged. Oboes and bassoons take up the G-minor melody together, marked largamente, over running first violins and cellos. Plucked basses support them; the second violins remain muted.

    2. The violins leap upwards

      The second violins remove their mutes. The first violins leap into their high register, marked largamente, and descend in a long run while the horns sustain their notes. The basses return after a rest, and the timpani roll beneath the phrase.

    3. G major, almost whispered

      The brass falls silent as the strings and woodwinds finish their phrases. Elgar marks a ritardando. A last violin run climbs above the divided violas and cellos; the basses return with the final G-major chord, held at ppp.

  2. II. “H.D.S.-P.” (Allegro)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    0:42

    Before a piano trio, Hew David Steuart-Powell runs his fingers over the keys. Elgar turns that familiar warm-up into a little orchestral toccata, adding more chromatic notes than his pianist friend would enjoy.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Fingers become violin bows

      The first violins begin a staccato run; the seconds take it up. Sharps and naturals crowd the little figures, turning a pianist’s everyday warm-up into something mischievously awkward. The woodwinds join the game.

    2. Three friends at music

      Steuart-Powell plays piano trios with Elgar on violin and Basil Nevinson on cello. Both colleagues receive a variation: the pianist gets this teasing Allegro; the cellist gets a long singing solo in the twelfth variation.

  3. III. “R.B.T.” (Allegretto)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    1:18

    Richard Baxter Townshend plays an old man in amateur theatricals, his bass voice suddenly shooting up into a squeak. Elgar casts the oboe and bassoons in this small comedy.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Oboe and grumbling bassoons

      The oboe bends the theme into a cheeky little phrase, marked scherzando. Bassoons answer below it while the strings pluck. A theatrical old man has acquired an orchestra: high interjections, low grumbles, and hardly a straight face.

    2. A don on stage

      Townshend is an Oxford don and the author of the Tenderfoot books. Elgar remembers his amateur acting: an old man whose low voice keeps flying into soprano. His brother-in-law, William Meath Baker, is the subject of the very next variation.

  4. IV. “W.M.B.” (Allegro di molto)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    0:28

    William Meath Baker, squire of Hasfield, gets the shortest of Elgar’s portraits. His brisk announcements become hard accents; his hurried exit supplies the punchline.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Baker takes the floor

      Strings and woodwinds speak in clipped, heavily accented phrases, with horns, trumpets and timpani behind them. The marking is Allegro di molto. Almost before this bustling little speech has begun, the orchestra is racing towards its last chord.

    2. Carriages for the guests

      Baker has a houseful of guests and carriages to arrange. With a slip of paper in his hand, he reads out the day’s plans forcefully and hurries from the music room, accidentally banging the door. Elgar includes the guests’ teasing response in the middle of the variation.

  5. V. “R.P.A.” (Moderato)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    1:59

    Richard Penrose Arnold can never keep a serious conversation serious for long. Elgar gives him a solemn tune in the basses, then lets the woodwinds break in with jokes.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A solemn beginning

      Cellos and double basses set out the theme in C minor, marked sostenuto. The violins wind around it on their lowest strings. Arnold begins his conversation with a properly serious face.

    2. The poet’s son at the piano

      Arnold, son of the poet Matthew Arnold, teaches himself the piano. Elgar enjoys the way he gets round the difficult bits while still conveying the music’s feeling. His conversation has the same freedom: earnest talk keeps breaking into whimsical remarks.

    3. The conversation carries on

      The wind chatter subsides. Small string figures continue over quiet timpani and low brass, leading straight into Isabel Fitton’s portrait, Ysobel. Elgar writes attacca at the end of the page.

    How it is joined

    1. The flute cracks a joke

      The C-minor bass melody settles on C, and E♮ brightens the harmony into C major. A solo flute runs upwards while oboes and clarinets exchange short replies. The strings fall to pianissimo; the Moderato pace holds, though the conversation has become much less solemn.

    2. Low brass under the farewell

      A falling clarinet run leads into the closing link. The strings keep a small curling figure moving while timpani hold a quiet roll. Trombones and tuba enter pianissimo, and the double basses sustain their note beneath them. The page ends attacca, carrying Arnold’s last sentence into Ysobel.

  6. VI. “Ysobel” (Andantino)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    1:05

    Elgar gives Isabel Fitton, his viola pupil, a portrait built from a string-crossing exercise. The awkward leap becomes a tender melody for her own instrument.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A bow crosses strings

      The violas’ opening figure dips low and leaps back up across the strings. A long note follows the leap, giving the phrase room to sing. Bassoons and clarinets answer in little fragments while the violas keep returning to their exercise.

    2. Isabel brings her viola

      Isabel Fitton belongs to a musical family in Malvern and studies viola with Elgar. Crossing from one string to another is a beginner’s difficulty he knows from teaching. For her portrait he takes that small piece of daily practice and builds an entire Andantino around it.

  7. VII. “Troyte” (Presto)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    0:53

    Elgar turns architect Arthur Troyte Griffith’s struggles at the piano into a furious orchestral joke. Timpani and low strings supply the awkward fingers.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Timpani and awkward fingers

      Timpani, cellos and double basses hammer out a lopsided figure. Elgar writes one semibreve beat to each bar and marks the music Presto; the winds pile in as the bass keeps worrying at its few notes.

    2. A lesson goes wrong

      Troyte Griffith is a Malvern architect and one of Elgar’s closest friends. In Elgar’s account, the piano lesson gets steadily worse: the pupil blunders, his teacher tries to impose order, and a final despairing slam admits defeat. The teacher is Elgar himself.

  8. VIII. “W.N.” (Allegretto)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    1:28

    A pair of clarinets opens the door to Winifred Norbury’s portrait. Elgar remembers her eighteenth-century house and slips her laugh into its polite woodwind conversation.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Two clarinets call

      Two clarinets begin together, over a softly moving cello line. Flutes answer, then the violins join the conversation. The rocking six-eight metre and G major make a gentle entrance after the scramble of Troyte.

    2. Winifred’s laugh

      Winifred Norbury helps run the Worcestershire Philharmonic Society. At Sherridge, the eighteenth-century house she shares with her sister, Elgar finds the manners and atmosphere for this variation. Winifred’s connection with his musical life earns her the initials above it; her distinctive laugh finds its way into the music.

  9. IX. Nimrod — AdagioHallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    2:54

    August Jaeger talks Elgar out of giving up composition by singing him Beethoven. Nimrod remembers that friendship in a string melody that grows until the whole orchestra is singing.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Violins on one string

      The first violins sing very softly on the D string. Divided violas and second violins fill the chords beneath them. The Adagio moves in three beats, but the tune stretches across the bar lines in one long breath.

    2. Jaeger, the hunter

      August Jaeger works for Novello, Elgar’s publisher. He reads the scores closely, praises them, argues with their composer and sometimes demands changes. His surname means hunter in German; Elgar names his variation after the mighty hunter Nimrod in the Book of Genesis.

    3. Jaeger sings Beethoven

      In 1904 Elgar tells Dora Penny how Jaeger talks him out of giving up composition. Jaeger points to Beethoven’s troubles, sings the slow movement of the Pathétique Sonata and insists: “And that is what you must do.” The opening of Nimrod carries a hint of that melody.

    4. The orchestra sings together

      Elgar writes legatissimo: the notes joined as closely as possible. Woodwinds double the rising violin tune, brass fill the chords, and timpani support the bass. Even at its fullest, the music keeps the long bow stroke of the opening.

    How it is joined

    1. Winds join the strings

      The E♭-major Adagio takes another breath. Flute and clarinet share the violin melody, with bassoons and horns filling the chords below. The cellos divide, and the basses move under the rising phrase. A new crescendo grows from pianissimo; the three-beat pace stays unchanged.

    2. The bass gathers the orchestra

      Quiet oboe and clarinet phrases lead to a rising cello line. Bassoons and horns join the crescendo, then the E♭-major melody opens out at legatissimo. Timpani and double basses anchor it below; trumpets, trombones and tuba fill out the sustained chords.

    3. E♭ major falls quiet

      At the final ritardando, the violin line descends and the orchestra drops from fortissimo to pianissimo. Brass and timpani stop. Clarinets, bassoons and strings hold the last E♭-major chord over a low E♭, with a pause above the final bar.

  10. X. “Dorabella” (Intermezzo: Allegretto)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    2:05

    Dora Penny’s portrait dances in little hesitations, with a long viola melody hidden among the skipping notes. Elgar calls her Dorabella after the young woman in Mozart’s Così fan tutte.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Small hesitations

      Muted violins skip into the bar late, with little rests between their phrases. The cellos and basses pluck underneath. Dora Penny’s slight stammer is part of this affectionate portrait: Elgar makes its hesitations dance.

    2. Dora keeps the memories

      Dora is the daughter of the rector of Wolverhampton and a close friend of the Elgars. Years later, as Mrs Richard Powell, she writes Edward Elgar: Memories of a Variation, preserving the conversations behind this circle of musical portraits.

    3. A tune inside the dance

      The flute carries a sustained tune while the muted violins keep up their short, broken figures. Earlier the solo viola has this singing role. Elgar lets one instrument finish a sentence while the rest of the orchestra keeps interrupting.

    How it is joined

    1. The flute lengthens the phrase

      The G-major dance keeps its Allegretto pace. Over plucked cello and bass notes, the flute sustains its melody across the bar lines; oboes interject, and muted violins continue their little off-beat figures. The longer tune has room to sing without slowing the dancers.

  11. XI. “G.R.S.” (Allegro di molto)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    0:50

    George Robertson Sinclair, organist of Hereford Cathedral, has a bulldog called Dan. Elgar gives the dog a spectacular entrance: a tumble down the bank into the River Wye.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A bulldog goes swimming

      The strings plunge downwards. Bassoons and double basses paddle onwards in quavers. Elgar’s account supplies the action: Dan falls into the Wye, swims upstream to find a landing place, then scrambles out with a delighted bark.

    2. Sinclair sets a challenge

      Sinclair challenges Elgar to put the river incident into music. The composer is already fond of Dan: on visits to the organist’s house, he writes little musical sketches of the dog’s moods in the visitors’ book.

  12. XII. “B.G.N.” (Andante)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    2:16

    Basil Nevinson is the cellist in Elgar’s piano trio, and the cello opens and closes his portrait. Between those two solos, a roomful of friends becomes an orchestra.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Nevinson’s own instrument

      A solo cello begins molto espressivo, free to linger over its first two bars. Quiet violas and the other cellos support it. Then the whole cello section takes up the tune, still broad and singing.

    2. The trio’s generous cellist

      Nevinson plays chamber music with Elgar on violin and Hew David Steuart-Powell at the piano. Elgar’s written tribute praises more than his playing: Nevinson puts his scientific and artistic skills at his friends’ disposal, wholeheartedly.

    3. A private farewell

      The orchestra dwindles, and the cello has the last phrase. Elgar writes a long pause, then lets the soloist reach upwards once more over held lower strings. The final instruction is attacca: the Romanza follows without a break.

    How it is joined

    1. The cello keeps singing

      The G-minor Andante contracts around the cello. Elgar writes diminuendo molto across the winds and strings; the flutes and oboes stop, then the bassoons and horns. The basses fall silent while the orchestral cellos hold a low note beneath the soloist’s last upward phrase and its long pause.

  13. XIII. “***” (Romanza: Moderato)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    2:12

    A clarinet quotes Mendelssohn’s Calm Sea and Prosperous Voyage over the distant throb of a liner’s engines. Elgar leaves three asterisks where his traveller’s name should be.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Clarinet over little waves

      A clarinet sings over rising and falling second violins and cellos. The first violins join, then the winds widen the phrase. Elgar calls this a Romanza; the opening has the ease of a song in G major.

    2. A ship’s engines

      The clarinet quotes Calm Sea and Prosperous Voyage by Mendelssohn, above a whispering timpani roll. Elgar asks for side-drum sticks on the timpani. His commentary identifies the sound beneath the tune as the engines of a distant liner.

    3. The woman behind the asterisks

      Lady Mary Lygon of Madresfield Court supports local music-making and sails to Australia in 1899. Her initials appear in Elgar’s sketches; three asterisks replace them in print. The missing name keeps this portrait’s secret alive while the clarinet sends its borrowed tune out to sea.

    How it is joined

    1. The Romanza puts out to sea

      Poco rallentando leads to tranquillo. The upper strings fade out while divided violas keep a small rising-and-falling figure moving. A soft timpani roll and held cello notes support the clarinet’s Mendelssohn quotation. The score puts quotation marks around the borrowed phrase.

    2. Brass interrupt the crossing

      Trumpets, trombones and tuba enter softly, then swell over repeated low-string figures. The timpani change from their roll to measured notes. Elgar quickly withdraws the brass again: diminuendo molto, then Come prima, with the clarinet and the moving strings of the G-major Romanza.

  14. XIV. “E.D.U.” (Finale: Allegro)Hallé Orchestra, John Barbirolli · 1947
    4:23

    Alice calls her husband Edoo, and Elgar gives that name to his own swaggering finale. Her music and Jaeger’s return inside it: the two people who keep him composing are there at the finish.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Edoo keeps us waiting

      Elgar’s own variation enters on tiptoe. Short phrases pass between first violins, cellos and bassoons above detached notes in the bass. Even the trombones and tuba play very softly; the grand finale has yet to show its face.

    2. Alice’s name for him

      E.D.U. is Elgar’s spelling of Edoo, Alice’s pet name for him. He places himself at the end of the fourteen portraits, in music full of the confidence he struggles to muster while teaching violin and waiting for recognition.

    3. Alice and Jaeger return

      Elgar makes room in his self-portrait for two other people. The finale recalls Alice’s variation, C.A.E., and Jaeger’s, Nimrod. Their broad melodies sit among Edoo’s quick figures; his wife and his most demanding friend both have a say.

    4. Cellos beneath the conversation

      The bustle clears, leaving quiet wind phrases above divided cellos. Their soft quavers keep the Allegro moving. When the first violins return, Elgar marks them pianississimo, non legato — very soft, with the notes kept separate.

    5. Jaeger asks for more

      After the London première, Jaeger persuades Elgar to enlarge the ending. Elgar adds ninety-six bars and an organ part. He conducts the expanded version himself at the Worcester Three Choirs Festival on 13 September 1899.

    6. The last G major

      The full orchestra plants its feet on G major. The bass holds G while the upper parts finish their last phrases; a timpani roll grows beneath the final chord. Elgar lets the sound ring, then cuts it off.

    How it is joined

    1. Divided cellos keep the pulse

      The rushing string phrases subside into repeated soft quavers in the divided cellos and basses. Oboes, clarinets and bassoons answer above them, marked espressivo. The Allegro continues; at rehearsal 73, very quiet violins pick up the melody over pizzicato bass notes.

    2. Animando sets the strings moving

      Low brass and timpani sustain their soft chord while the violins’ short figures diminish. At animando, the cellos climb, the basses resume bowing, and woodwinds and horns take up the rising phrase. The score begins a fresh crescendo.

    3. G in the bass

      The closing phrases settle into G major, with the brass sustaining the chords and the strings finishing their last runs. A held G anchors the bass. Elgar writes ritardando, then grows a final crescendo from the low winds and timpani before the orchestra’s last attack.
