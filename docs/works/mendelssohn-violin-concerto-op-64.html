Felix Mendelssohn / III. Romanticism

Violin Concerto, Op. 64

1844 · Op. 64 · Violin and orchestra

Written in consultation with Ferdinand David, who gave the première in 1845 under Niels Gade. The cadenza arrives before the first movement’s main theme returns.

3 recordings · 29 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro molto appassionatoSoojin Han, violin; Hankyung Arte Philharmonic; Seokwon Hong, conductor · 23 September 2022
    13:53

    After one bar of orchestra, Soojin Han begins the long melody of Mendelssohn’s E minor concerto. Later, the cadenza turns inside out: her arpeggios become the accompaniment while the orchestra takes back the tune.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One bar, then the violin

      One bar of murmuring strings, then the solo violin begins its E-minor melody. Clarinets and bassoons hold their notes; the basses pluck underneath. Mendelssohn gives the soloist the first word, quietly, before the orchestra has had a tune of its own.

    2. A tune without peace

      On 30 July 1838, Mendelssohn writes to Ferdinand David, leader of Leipzig’s Gewandhaus Orchestra: “I’d like to write a violin concerto for you next winter; one in E minor sticks in my head, the beginning of which will not leave me in peace.”

    3. The soloist plays the bass

      The violin descends to its open G string and stays there. Flutes and clarinets sing the new G-major tune above it, marked tranquillo. For this first phrase the soloist supplies the bass; the orchestral strings are silent.

    4. The opening starts to wander

      After a held chord, the violin picks up its opening melody again over quiet strings and plucked basses. Soon it is running through scales while the woodwinds answer. The two tunes of the exposition now meet inside the development.

    5. David plays; Gade conducts

      Leipzig, 13 March 1845: Ferdinand David gives the concerto’s first performance with the Gewandhaus Orchestra. Mendelssohn is ill, so Niels Gade conducts. The promise of a concerto “next winter” has taken nearly seven years to reach the platform.

    6. The cadenza takes over

      In the cadenza, the violin has the stage to itself: chords, wide leaps, trills and arpeggios. Mendelssohn places this solo passage in the middle of the movement, before the opening tune returns. Its last arpeggios will keep going when the orchestra comes back.

    7. The solo becomes an accompaniment

      The opening melody returns in flutes, oboes and first violins. Beneath it, the soloist’s arpeggios keep rippling across the strings, pianissimo. The cadenza has carried straight into the recapitulation; the violin that began alone now accompanies its own tune.

    8. The wind tune comes home

      The second tune returns in E major. The violin holds a low E while the woodwinds sing; oboes and bassoons now join the flutes and clarinets. G♯ warms the home key before the movement turns back to E minor.

    9. Faster, then faster again

      The closing stretch returns to E minor and gathers speed. Mendelssohn writes Più presto, then Sempre più presto, and finally Presto. The violin’s runs cut through wind chords and the strings’ insistent off-beat accents.

    10. One bassoon refuses to stop

      The first movement closes in E minor, but the first bassoon holds on to B after the other instruments finish. That single note carries into the Andante. It will rise a semitone to C as the slow movement begins.

    How it is joined

    1. The violin’s ascent summons the orchestra

      The violin’s rising scales lead to full orchestral chords in E minor. Flutes, oboes and first violins take up the opening melody at fortissimo; lower strings strike repeated chords beneath them. The solo violin rests while its tune fills the orchestra.

    2. The violin descends beneath the woodwinds

      From a high C, the solo violin descends to its lowest note, open G. The orchestral strings stop, leaving that G as the bass of the new G-major tune. Flutes and clarinets sing above it, pianissimo and tranquillo.

    3. A held chord, then plucked basses

      A pianissimo G-major chord with a fermata ends the exposition. The soloist resumes the opening melody over quiet strings; plucked bass notes mark the pulse. As the violin rises, the accompaniment grows and the wind instruments begin to answer.

    4. A held B opens the cadenza

      Over B in the bass, the orchestra grows from pianissimo to fortissimo, marked poco a poco crescendo. The dominant of E minor remains unresolved as the orchestra drops out. The violin continues alone under the heading Cadenza ad libitum, with the return of the opening theme still ahead.

    5. Arpeggios carry across the join

      The cadenza settles into continuous semiquaver arpeggios. Flutes, oboes and first violins enter with the E-minor opening melody while the soloist keeps playing, now pianissimo. Low strings restore the accompaniment beneath both. There is no pause between solo display and the return.

    6. The violin rests on low E

      The violin descends as the orchestra diminishes, sempre più tranquillo. Its sustained low E becomes the bass under the woodwinds’ second theme. The tune first heard in G major now comes in E major, with oboes and bassoons added to the flutes and clarinets.

    7. E minor begins the final chase

      E minor returns for the closing stretch. The solo violin runs above off-beat string chords while the winds recall the opening tune. Mendelssohn then tightens the pace through Più presto, Sempre più presto and Presto, sending the violin into another chain of scales.

  2. II. AndanteSoojin Han, violin; Hankyung Arte Philharmonic; Seokwon Hong, conductor · 23 September 2022
    8:23

    One bassoon note carries Mendelssohn’s concerto into this C-major Andante, where Soojin Han’s violin sings over softly moving strings. In the middle, the same violin must play a melody and its shivering accompaniment at once.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The bassoon carries on

      The first bassoon keeps its B after the first movement ends, then rises a semitone to C. A flute joins it, and the low strings begin to move. That narrow step takes the concerto from E minor into the Andante’s C major.

    2. A song above the strings

      The violin’s C-major melody stretches over a gently rising and falling string accompaniment. The winds rest through its opening phrases. Mendelssohn leaves the soloist room to sing, with the orchestra moving quietly beneath each held note.

    3. A few days in July

      In July 1839, Mendelssohn tells Ferdinand David that he needs only “a few days in a good mood” to produce the promised concerto. The days prove elusive. He works seriously on it in July 1844 and finishes the score that September.

    4. One violin, two jobs

      Trumpets and timpani darken the middle section, in A minor, above trembling strings. When the solo violin takes up the tune, it also supplies the quick repeated notes beneath it. The bow must keep the melody singing while the lower notes shiver.

    5. The song returns in C

      The violin recovers its first melody in C major. The strings keep a quick, quiet accompaniment beneath it, carrying some of the middle section’s trembling into the return. Flute and clarinet add small answers around the solo line.

    6. A label for Milstein

      In March 1948, Columbia librarian Helene F. Chmura signs a label order for ML 4001: Nathan Milstein playing this concerto, with Bruno Walter conducting the New York Philharmonic. The company releases it that June as Columbia launches the long-playing record.

    7. The violin comes down

      Sempre più piano — softer all the time. The violin’s last runs descend towards a quiet C-major chord, with the strings plucking beneath the final wind notes. A pause is written over the last chord; the next page already has the finale’s linking phrase.

    How it is joined

    1. The bassoon hands over to the violin

      The bassoon’s held B rises to C, carrying the music out of E minor. Flute and other winds begin the Andante over low strings. They fall silent for the violin’s C-major song, leaving the strings’ pianissimo accompaniment to move beneath it.

    2. Trumpets and timpani disturb the song

      The strings’ repeated notes swell towards the A-minor middle section. Trumpets and timpani join the wind instruments’ new theme at fortissimo, with the low strings bowing beneath them. The soloist then takes that tune into double-stopping, playing its own quick accompaniment below the melody.

    3. The violin sings above the tremble

      The violin’s restless double-stopping diminishes, marked sempre diminuendo. The strings take over the quick repeated notes, leaving the soloist free to bring back the C-major melody. The bass reaches C beneath it; flute and clarinet answer softly.

    4. The violin settles over C

      The solo violin descends through its last runs, sempre più piano. Low C anchors the close while the string parts change to pizzicato and the winds finish their phrases. A C-major chord with a fermata ends the Andante; the following Allegretto non troppo starts in E minor.

  3. III. Allegretto non troppo — Allegro molto vivaceSoojin Han, violin; Hankyung Arte Philharmonic; Seokwon Hong, conductor · 23 September 2022
    6:39

    A quiet E-minor link leads to the trumpet calls and flying violin notes of Mendelssohn’s finale. Soojin Han and the Hankyung Arte Philharmonic finish the concerto in E major, with the soloist still running when the last chords arrive.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Fourteen bars between movements

      The solo violin and strings make a short bridge out of the Andante. Fourteen bars, in E minor, marked Allegretto non troppo. The winds and brass wait while the violin sings one more measured phrase.

    2. Trumpets open the door

      Two clipped fanfares from bassoons, horns, trumpets and timpani start the Allegro molto vivace. Between them, the violin darts upward. Soon the strings join its quick E-major tune, keeping their bows light.

    3. A short tune in B

      The solo violin has a second tune, brief and light, in B major. It soon opens into arpeggios that climb and fall across the strings. The orchestra keeps answering in short phrases, leaving the soloist plenty of room to move.

    4. The orchestra borrows the tune

      The orchestra plays its own version of the opening tune, then the development turns towards G major. The violin keeps up its quick notes while short wind phrases appear around it. This is a compact detour; the finale is soon on its way back.

    5. The strings have another tune

      The opening tune returns to the solo violin, with a new melody answering it in the strings. Mendelssohn marks the quiet playing carefully: sempre pp, always pianissimo. The violin’s little leaps and runs have company now.

    6. Mendelssohn takes the baton

      On 23 October 1845, Mendelssohn finally conducts the concerto himself, with Ferdinand David again playing the violin. Illness has kept him from the première seven months earlier. David has helped shape the solo part through years of letters and advice.

    7. Trills under the woodwinds

      The violin settles into long trills while the woodwinds recall the main tune. Plucked strings mark the pulse underneath. After so much racing about, the soloist stays on one spot and lets the tune pass overhead.

    8. The last sprint in E

      The trills open into more running notes, and the coda drives towards E major. The orchestra answers the violin’s scales with sharply accented chords. A final upward rush brings the soloist and orchestra together for the closing chords.

    How it is joined

    1. The strings wait on B

      The E-minor link swells with the solo violin and strings alone. Its bass settles on B, the dominant of E, as the strings diminish to pianissimo. The violin holds above it, and a fermata suspends the last chord.

    2. A fanfare breaks the pause

      After the fermata, bassoons, horns, trumpets and timpani strike a fanfare at fortissimo. The marking changes to Allegro molto vivace. The solo violin answers with a rising figure; the strings join as the E-major main tune gets under way.

    3. The violin finds a B-major tune

      The violin’s rising runs and an orchestral chord lead into B major, the dominant key. The soloist begins a quieter phrase, marked leggiero, over short string chords and plucked basses. Woodwinds answer between its quick notes.

    4. The orchestra takes a turn

      The solo violin finishes its B-major arpeggios with another upward scale. The orchestra answers with a fortissimo version of the opening tune. As the violin rejoins, the music turns towards G major for a short development, with light string chords beneath its runs.

    5. The returning violin gains a partner

      The quick solo runs lead back to the opening tune. Under the violin, the strings add a second melody, marked sempre pp, with plucked bass notes beneath. The return heads towards E major while the woodwinds keep their short replies.

    6. The second tune stays in E

      The short violin tune that first appeared in B major returns in E major. Strings keep their clipped accompaniment and the basses punctuate the phrases. The violin soon resumes its rising and falling arpeggios, with woodwind answers above.

    7. The trill becomes a scale

      Over plucked strings, the violin’s long trill accompanies the woodwind tune. It breaks into descending scales, then repeated notes as the orchestra gathers. The final E-major stretch alternates running violin figures with accented orchestral chords, ending in one last upward scale.
