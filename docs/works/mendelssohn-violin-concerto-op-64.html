Felix Mendelssohn / III. Romanticism

Violin Concerto, Op. 64

1844 · Op. 64 · Violin and orchestra

Written in consultation with Ferdinand David, who gave the première in 1845 under Niels Gade. The cadenza comes before the return of the first movement’s main theme.

3 recordings · 29 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro molto appassionatoSoojin Han, violin; Hankyung Arte Philharmonic; Seokwon Hong, conductor · 23 September 2022
    13:53

    Soojin Han plays the first movement, Allegro molto appassionato, of Mendelssohn’s Violin Concerto in E minor, Op. 64. She enters with the long melody after one bar of orchestra, and at the end of the cadenza her arpeggios become the accompaniment while the orchestra plays the tune again.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening and solo violin entry

      After one bar of string accompaniment, the solo violin begins the melody in E minor. Clarinets and bassoons hold their notes, and the basses play pizzicato. The soloist has the theme before the orchestra has played a tune of its own.

    2. Orchestral statement of the opening theme

      A rising violin run leads to fortissimo chords, and the full orchestra plays the opening melody in E minor while the soloist rests. Until this point the violin has carried the melody over accompaniment for about a minute.

    3. Transition to the second theme

      A chromatic transition follows. The orchestra gives out a transition theme, and the soloist carries it in running notes through crescendos and diminuendos over sustained chords. The music moves away from E minor towards G major and the second theme.

    4. Letter to Ferdinand David, 1838

      On 30 July 1838 Mendelssohn wrote to Ferdinand David, leader of Leipzig’s Gewandhaus Orchestra, that he would like to write him a violin concerto the next winter. He added that “one in E minor sticks in my head, the beginning of which will not leave me in peace.”

    5. G-major tune over open G

      The violin descends to its open G string and holds it. Flutes and clarinets play the new G-major tune above it, marked tranquillo. For this first phrase the soloist supplies the bass, and the orchestral strings are silent.

    6. Autograph score, Kraków, 1844

      Mendelssohn finished the autograph on 16 September 1844; it is kept in the Jagiellonian Library in Kraków. There the movement is headed “Allegro con fuoco” (with fire). While preparing the première he changed the heading to the printed “Allegro molto appassionato” and lengthened the cadenza.

    7. Development section

      After a held chord, the violin resumes its opening melody over quiet strings and pizzicato basses. It moves into running scales, with the woodwinds playing in alternation. In the development the two themes of the exposition are combined.

    8. Development, first fortissimo climax

      The development reaches its first climax. Strings and winds hold fortissimo chords with long trills, and the soloist, marked con forza, trills with them. A diminuendo follows in the whole orchestra.

    9. Letter to Ferdinand David, 1844

      The autograph is dated 16 September 1844. On 17 December, three months before the première, Mendelssohn asked David whether the lengthened cadenza was written smoothly, then added: “Do not laugh at me too much, I feel ashamed in any case, but I cannot help it; I am just groping around.”

    10. First performance, Leipzig, 1845

      Ferdinand David gave the first performance on 13 March 1845, at the last Gewandhaus concert of the winter in Leipzig. Niels Gade, the concerts’ music director, conducted. Mendelssohn was living in Frankfurt and led none of that season’s concerts; he had promised David a concerto “next winter” in a letter of 30 July 1838.

    11. Cadenza

      The solo violin plays the cadenza alone, with chords, wide leaps, trills and arpeggios. Mendelssohn placed it in the middle of the movement, before the opening theme returns. The violin continues its arpeggios when the orchestra re-enters.

    12. Cadenza

      Mendelssohn wrote the cadenza out in full. In the printed score its rhythm moves in stages from broken-chord quavers to triplets to semiquaver arpeggios. The violin diminishes to pianissimo as the orchestra returns.

    13. Recapitulation, opening theme in orchestra

      The cadenza leads directly into the recapitulation. The opening melody returns in flutes, oboes and first violins, and beneath it the soloist continues the semiquaver arpeggios across the strings, pianissimo.

    14. Cadenza placement and later influence

      Mendelssohn wrote the cadenza out and placed it at the end of the development, before the recapitulation. Donald Tovey called the concerto “the original type of the majority of modern concertos”. Tchaikovsky later used the same plan in his Violin Concerto.

    15. Second subject in E major

      The second subject returns in E major. The violin holds a low E while the woodwinds play the theme, with oboes and bassoons now joining the flutes and clarinets. G♯ colours the tonic before the movement turns back to E minor.

    16. Closing passage in E minor

      The closing stretch returns to E minor and speeds up. Mendelssohn writes Più presto, then Sempre più presto, and finally Presto. The violin plays runs over wind chords and accented off-beats in the strings.

    17. Bassoon link to the Andante

      The first movement closes in E minor, and the first bassoon holds B after the other instruments stop. The note carries over into the Andante. As the slow movement begins, it rises a semitone to C.

    Joins

    1. Solo scales lead to the orchestral tutti

      The solo violin plays rising scales that lead to full orchestral chords in E minor. Flutes, oboes and first violins take up the opening melody at fortissimo, and the lower strings play repeated chords beneath them. The solo violin rests.

    2. The violin descends beneath the woodwinds

      From a high C, the solo violin descends to its lowest note, open G. The orchestral strings stop, and the violin holds that G as the bass of the new G-major tune. Flutes and clarinets play the tune above it, pianissimo and tranquillo.

    3. A held chord, then plucked basses

      The exposition ends on a pianissimo G-major chord with a fermata. The soloist resumes the opening melody over quiet strings, and plucked bass notes mark the pulse. As the violin rises, the accompaniment grows and the wind instruments enter.

    4. Crescendo over B into the cadenza

      Over B in the bass, the orchestra grows from pianissimo to fortissimo, marked poco a poco crescendo. The orchestra drops out with the dominant of E minor unresolved. The violin continues alone under the heading Cadenza ad libitum. The opening theme returns after the cadenza.

    5. Cadenza arpeggios continue into the recapitulation

      The cadenza moves into continuous semiquaver arpeggios. Flutes, oboes and first violins enter with the E-minor opening melody, and the soloist continues the arpeggios pianissimo. Low strings bring back the accompaniment beneath both. The music runs from the end of the cadenza into the recapitulation without a pause.

    6. The violin holds low E

      The violin descends as the orchestra diminishes, sempre più tranquillo. Its sustained low E becomes the bass under the woodwinds’ second theme. The tune first heard in G major returns in E major, with oboes and bassoons added to the flutes and clarinets.

    7. E minor returns for the coda

      E minor returns for the closing stretch. The solo violin plays running figures above off-beat chords in the strings, and the winds restate the opening theme. Mendelssohn then marks successive increases in tempo, Più presto, Sempre più presto and Presto, and the violin plays another chain of scales.

  2. II. AndanteSoojin Han, violin; Hankyung Arte Philharmonic; Seokwon Hong, conductor · 23 September 2022
    8:23

    The Andante in C major, second movement of Mendelssohn’s Violin Concerto in E minor, Op. 64, is joined to the first movement by a single bassoon note. Soojin Han is the soloist, playing over the strings; in the middle section the violin plays the melody and its accompaniment at the same time.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Bassoon link to the Andante

      The first bassoon holds its B after the first movement ends, then rises a semitone to C. A flute enters and the low strings begin to move. The semitone step takes the concerto from E minor into the C major of the Andante.

    2. Solo melody in C major

      The solo violin plays a melody in C major above a rising and falling accompaniment in the strings, marked pianissimo. The winds rest through its opening phrases.

    3. Form, and Tovey’s view, 1936

      The Andante has three parts: the violin’s C-major melody, an A-minor middle section and the melody’s return. Donald Tovey found “true breadth and dignity” in it, and in the middle section “agitation and anxiety” without fussiness.

    4. Movements linked without a break

      Mendelssohn linked all three movements without a break. Weber’s Konzertstück had already done this, though no work called a concerto had. Steven Ledbetter added that the Liszt concertos and many others are hard to imagine without Mendelssohn’s example.

    5. Letter to Ferdinand David, 1839

      Ferdinand David pressed Mendelssohn for a concerto and asked that it be brilliant. In an 1839 letter Mendelssohn replied, “How can anyone like me do this?”, and added that the whole first solo was to lie on the E string. The concerto, promised in 1838, was finished in 1844.

    6. Letter to Ferdinand David, 1839

      In July 1839 Mendelssohn told Ferdinand David that he needed only “a few days in a good mood” to produce the promised concerto. He did not work on it seriously until July 1844, and he finished the score that September.

    7. Middle section in A minor

      Trumpets and timpani enter in the middle section, in A minor, above string tremolos. When the solo violin takes up the tune, it also plays the quick repeated notes beneath it, so the one bow carries both the melody and the tremolo accompaniment.

    8. Middle section, orchestral crescendo

      In the middle section the orchestra builds chord by chord to fortissimo, with sforzandi and long crescendos. Under it the soloist plays continuous semiquavers.

    9. Return of the melody in C major

      The violin plays its first melody again in C major. Beneath it the strings keep up a quick, quiet accompaniment of repeated notes, carried over from the middle section. Flute and clarinet add short responses around the solo line.

    10. Columbia ML 4001, 1948

      In March 1948 the Columbia librarian Helene F. Chmura signed a label order for ML 4001, a recording of this concerto by Nathan Milstein with the New York Philharmonic under Bruno Walter. Columbia released it that June, when the company launched the long-playing record.

    11. Close of the movement

      Sempre più piano — softer all the time. The violin’s last runs descend to a soft C-major chord, and the strings play pizzicato beneath the final wind notes. A pause is written over the last chord, and the finale’s linking phrase follows on the next page.

    Joins

    1. Bassoon link into the Andante

      The bassoon holds B and then rises to C, which takes the music out of E minor. Flute and the other winds open the Andante over the low strings. The winds stop when the violin enters with its C-major melody, and the strings continue the accompaniment pianissimo beneath it.

    2. Trumpets and timpani at the A-minor section

      The strings’ repeated notes grow louder towards the A-minor middle section. Trumpets and timpani join the wind instruments’ new theme at fortissimo, and the low strings bow beneath them. The soloist then takes over the tune in double-stopping and plays a quick accompaniment below the melody.

    3. Return of the C-major melody

      The violin’s double-stopping grows softer, marked sempre diminuendo. The strings take over the quick repeated notes, and the soloist brings back the C-major melody. The bass reaches C beneath it, and flute and clarinet then play softly.

    4. Andante closes on C major

      The solo violin descends through its last runs, sempre più piano. A low C sounds under the close while the strings change to pizzicato and the winds finish their phrases. The Andante ends on a C-major chord with a fermata. The Allegretto non troppo that follows starts in E minor.

  3. III. Allegretto non troppo — Allegro molto vivaceSoojin Han, violin; Hankyung Arte Philharmonic; Seokwon Hong, conductor · 23 September 2022
    6:39

    The finale of Mendelssohn’s Violin Concerto in E minor, Op. 64, begins with a short Allegretto non troppo link in E minor; the Allegro molto vivace, in E major, has trumpet calls and fast violin passagework that continues up to the closing chords. Soojin Han plays with the Hankyung Arte Philharmonic.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Bridge from the Andante

      The solo violin and strings play a short bridge out of the Andante, fourteen bars in E minor marked Allegretto non troppo. The winds and brass rest while the violin plays one more phrase.

    2. Opening fanfares and solo entry

      Two short fanfares for bassoons, horns, trumpets and timpani open the Allegro molto vivace. Between them the violin plays a rising figure. The strings then accompany its quick tune in E major, playing lightly.

    3. Finale as “fairy music”

      Steven Ledbetter described this finale as one of those brilliantly light and fleet-footed examples of “fairy music” that Mendelssohn made so uniquely his own. The strings play with light bows beneath the solo violin’s fast notes.

    4. Second theme in B major

      The solo violin plays a short, light second theme in B major. Arpeggios follow, rising and falling across the strings. The orchestra plays short phrases between the soloist’s passages.

    5. Development in G major

      The orchestra plays its own version of the opening tune, and the development moves towards G major. The violin continues in quick notes while short wind phrases enter around it.

    6. Return of the theme with countermelody

      The opening theme returns in the solo violin, and the strings add a new countermelody beneath it. Mendelssohn marks the passage sempre pp, always pianissimo.

    7. Gewandhaus concert, 23 October 1845

      On 23 October 1845 Mendelssohn conducted the Gewandhaus’s third concert of the season, and Ferdinand David played the concerto there for the second time. Mendelssohn had conducted none of the previous winter’s concerts, including the première. Over several years David had helped shape the solo part through letters and advice.

    8. Solo trills under the woodwinds

      The violin holds long trills while the woodwinds play the main tune. Pizzicato strings mark the pulse underneath.

    9. Coda in E major

      After the trills the violin returns to running notes, and the coda moves towards E major. The orchestra plays sharply accented chords against the violin’s scales. A final upward run leads soloist and orchestra into the closing chords together.

    Joins

    1. Dominant pedal on B before the finale

      The E-minor link is scored for solo violin and strings alone, with a crescendo. Its bass settles on B, the dominant of E, and the strings diminish to pianissimo. The solo violin holds above the bass, and a fermata stands over the last chord.

    2. Fanfare after the fermata

      After the fermata, bassoons, horns, trumpets and timpani play a fanfare at fortissimo. The marking changes to Allegro molto vivace. The solo violin follows with a rising figure, and the strings enter as the E-major main theme begins.

    3. Rising runs into the B-major theme

      The violin’s rising runs and an orchestral chord lead into B major, the dominant key. The soloist begins a quieter phrase, marked leggiero, over short string chords and plucked basses. Woodwinds play in the gaps between its quick notes.

    4. Orchestral tutti and turn to G major

      The solo violin ends its B-major arpeggios with a further rising scale. The orchestra then plays the opening tune fortissimo. When the violin re-enters, the harmony moves towards G major for a short development, and the strings play light chords under the solo runs.

    5. Solo return with string countermelody

      The solo runs lead back to the opening tune. Under the violin, the strings add a second melody, marked sempre pp, with plucked bass notes beneath. The return moves towards E major, and the woodwinds continue their short replies.

    6. The second tune returns in E

      The short violin tune first heard in B major returns in E major. The strings keep their clipped accompaniment and the basses punctuate the phrases. The violin soon resumes its rising and falling arpeggios, with woodwind phrases above.

    7. Trill, scales and the E-major close

      The strings play pizzicato while the woodwind have the tune, and the violin holds a long trill under it. The trill gives way to descending scales and then to repeated notes as the orchestra builds up. In the final stretch in E major, running figures in the violin alternate with accented chords in the orchestra. The movement ends with an upward scale.
