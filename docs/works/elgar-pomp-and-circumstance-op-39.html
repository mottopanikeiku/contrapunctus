Edward Elgar / IV. Fin de siècle

Pomp and Circumstance, Op. 39

1901 · Orchestra

Recordings

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. March No. 1 in DRoyal Albert Hall Orchestra, Edward Elgar · 27 April 1926, Queen’s Hall, London; issued by Victor in 1927
    4:24

    Elgar conducts the Royal Albert Hall Orchestra in his first electrical recording of the march, made in 1926. The march is quick and marked “with much fire”, and the well-known trio comes in its middle.

    Listening notes
    1. Opening in E♭

      Elgar began his march in D with an off-beat outburst in E♭. The music then moves down a semitone before the march proper begins. Above the first bar he wrote Allegro, con molto fuoco (fast, with much fire).

    2. First recording, 26 June 1914

      Elgar’s first recording of this march, made on 26 June 1914, was cut to just over four minutes. It began with the trio tune so that none of the trio was lost, and some thirty London players made up the orchestra. The 1926 recording heard here was his first complete one.

    3. Trio in G major

      The trio opens in G major, marked Largamente. First violins, clarinets and horns share a long melody over a steady accompaniment. This is the tune later set as Land of Hope and Glory.

    4. Abbey Road studios opening, 1931

      In November 1931, at the opening of the Abbey Road studios, Elgar spoke to the London Symphony Orchestra before conducting this tune: “Please play this tune as though you’ve never heard it before.” Pathé filmed the request.

    5. Return of the march, D major

      At A tempo the strings return to their short, accented phrases in D major. Running notes fill the spaces between the heavier beats, and the bass takes up its leaping figure again.

    6. Return of the trio in D major

      The trio returns in D major, the home key of the march, marked Molto maestoso. The melody is now high above the bass. The full orchestra plays under its long notes, which were quiet when the theme was first heard.

    7. Closing bars

      After the long tune ends, quick string figures return and the woodwinds pass short notes down through the orchestra. Elgar closes with a sharp chord followed by a final held D.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  2. March No. 1 in DChicago Symphony Orchestra, Frederick Stock; Walter P. Zimmerman, organ · 22 December 1926, Orchestra Hall, Chicago; issued by Victor in July 1927
    4:25

    Elgar’s Pomp and Circumstance March No. 1 in D, Op. 39, played by the Chicago Symphony under Frederick Stock, with Walter P. Zimmerman at the organ. The organ enters at the return of the trio tune.

    Listening notes
    1. Chicago recording, 1926

      On 22 December 1926 Frederick Stock and the Chicago Symphony recorded the march for Victor in Orchestra Hall, with Walter P. Zimmerman at the organ. Victor made four takes. The third was issued.

    2. String melody and bass, rehearsal A

      At rehearsal A the strings play two firm accents beside a quick run of notes. The bass has a figure of its own, sometimes dissonant against the tune above. The passage, in D, is marked Allegro, con molto fuoco.

    3. First trio, G major

      The first trio is in G major. Four horns play the long tune with the first violins and clarinets, and the bassoons and lower strings play an even accompaniment. Elgar marks the melody legato e cantabile.

    4. American première, Chicago, 1902

      Theodore Thomas conducted the American première of the march with the Chicago Orchestra at the Auditorium on 28 November 1902. In 1926 Stock took the same orchestra into the recording session heard here.

    5. Return of the trio, organ pedals

      The trio returns in D major, marked Molto maestoso. Elgar wrote the organ part for pedals alone at first, specifying sixteen- and thirty-two-foot stops. Zimmerman’s hands join later on the manuals, adding chords as the tune continues.

    6. Coda

      After the great tune, Elgar returns to quick notes. The strings play running figures and the woodwinds exchange short phrases. The march ends with a clipped accent and a held D, and the organ pedals join the final chord.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  3. March No. 1 in D — concert-band arrangementUnited States Marine Band
    5:46

    The United States Marine Band plays Elgar’s Pomp and Circumstance March No. 1 in D, Op. 39, in an arrangement for concert band. The march sets a long, singing tune between quick, sharp-edged sections, and in this arrangement woodwind and brass play both.

    Listening notes
    1. Opening, Allegro, con molto fuoco

      Elgar marked the march Allegro, con molto fuoco (fast, with much fire). The opening phrases begin off the beat and continue in short bursts. In this recording the United States Marine Band plays the orchestral music on woodwind and brass.

    2. Trio melody

      The trio has the melody later sung as Land of Hope and Glory. Its phrases rise, hold long notes and are followed by falling steps. Beneath it the accompaniment keeps a steady two-beat pulse.

    3. Honorary doctorate, Yale, 1905

      At Yale on 28 June 1905 Elgar received an honorary doctorate and wore the university’s blue robes. The orchestra played this march as the guests left. Other universities adopted it, and the tune became part of American graduation ceremonies.

    4. Return of the quick march

      The march returns with its short, accented phrases and runs of quick notes. The long melody is absent for the whole section, and the bass and tune resume their brisk exchange.

    5. Benson’s words, 1902

      In 1902 A. C. Benson wrote words to the trio melody for Elgar’s Coronation Ode, then revised them for the separate song Land of Hope and Glory. Clara Butt sang the solo version. Benson’s text includes an appeal for wider boundaries for the British Empire.

    6. Coda

      After the broad tune ends, the march returns for a few quick phrases, marked a tempo and Più mosso from rehearsal T to the end. Short notes run through the closing passage. A sharp accent comes before the final held chord.
