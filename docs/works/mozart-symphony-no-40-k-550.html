Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart / II. Classical

Symphony No. 40, K. 550

1788 · Orchestra

4 recordings · 23 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Molto allegroMusopen Symphony (Commons credit; ensemble attribution disputed)
    5:59

    The violas are already moving when the violins begin their restless G minor tune. A falling semitone gets under the skin of the whole movement, returning in fragments and in a second theme that comes home changed.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Already in motion

      The violas set a quiet, restless pulse. Over it the violins repeat notes and fall a semitone, then do it again. Mozart begins in mid-thought: no grand opening chord, just that small phrase unable to leave itself alone.

    2. Semitones in B♭ major

      Strings and woodwinds exchange the second theme in B♭ major. It has longer breaths than the opening, but small chromatic steps still run through the melody. The change of key brings warmth without quite bringing rest.

    3. A room with a garden

      In June 1788 Mozart moves to cheaper lodgings outside central Vienna. Writing to Michael Puchberg, he welcomes fewer visitors, more time to work and a garden. A hired carriage can still take him into town when business calls.

    4. An abrupt F♯ minor

      The development opens with the familiar violin tune in F♯ minor, far from the B♭ major close. Soon only its first few notes remain. Strings and winds pass those scraps among themselves, beginning new entries before the previous ones have finished.

    5. The violins recover G minor

      The opening tune returns over the moving viola accompaniment. Its route onward is longer now: Mozart expands the connecting passage and keeps working the little fragments between the first and second themes.

    6. The second tune comes home

      The second theme now belongs to G minor. Strings and winds still trade its small, winding phrases, but the changed notes take away the warmth of its first appearance. The two themes finally share a key.

    7. Winds hold, strings answer

      Quiet wind chords hang above the answering strings near the close. The orchestra then returns to forceful chords and running notes for the last G minor cadences.

    How it is joined

    1. F major, then a breath

      The transition gathers on F major, the dominant of B♭, and breaks off. Quiet strings and woodwinds begin the second theme in B♭ major after this pause. Their longer, chromatic phrases replace the preceding orchestral rush; the tempo stays Molto allegro.

    2. B♭ major to F♯ minor

      The exposition closes in B♭ major. Mozart immediately shifts to F♯ minor, where the violins recover the first theme over soft wind chords. Its repeated notes and falling semitone remain familiar in this remote key; the melody soon breaks into shorter, overlapping entries.

    3. Fragments settle over D

      The harmony settles on D, the dominant of G minor, while the remaining fragments grow quieter. The bassoon descends above a held horn D. The violins then recover their full opening phrase over the moving violas, back in G minor.

    4. The pause leads to G minor

      The expanded transition reaches D major, the dominant of G minor. Another break releases the second theme, now in G minor. Strings and woodwinds retain their quiet exchange, with B♭ supplying the minor third above G.

  2. II. AndanteMusopen Symphony (Commons credit; ensemble attribution disputed)
    7:36

    The violas begin this Andante with repeated notes, and the violins join them one group at a time. The little figures that flutter between its phrases grow surprisingly fierce in the middle.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The violas start it

      The violas repeat a note; second violins join above them, then first violins higher still. Each part starts before the previous one has finished. A melody grows out of these overlapping entries, with the woodwinds adding their own replies.

    2. Three symphonies in a summer

      Mozart enters his last three symphonies in his own catalogue on 26 June, 25 July and 10 August 1788: the E♭ major, this G minor work and the Jupiter. All three are dated within forty-five days.

    3. Quick notes between phrases

      Tiny, quick figures slip between the longer notes in strings and winds. They give the Andante a nervous edge: even its most graceful phrases have something stirring underneath.

    4. Ten days at home

      In Vienna on 27 June 1788, Mozart writes to Michael Puchberg from his new lodgings: “In the ten days I have lived here I have worked more than in two months at the other place.” He asks his friend to visit. He is always at home.

    5. The small figures bite

      The development takes up the repeated notes and the quick string figures. Sharper harmonies and strong wind chords make them sound newly insistent. Mozart keeps the Andante pace; the agitation comes from what the parts do inside it.

    6. E♭ and the opening phrase

      The repeated-note phrase returns in E♭ major, rebuilding the melody through the strings. The quick interjections return too. Mozart brings home both the tune and the things that have been worrying it.

    7. A second wind section

      Mozart revisits the symphony and adds two clarinets, rewriting the oboe parts around them. Both versions survive: the same string-led Andante with two different wind ensembles.

    8. Musopen’s Kickstarter

      Musopen, a nonprofit founded by Aaron Dunn in 2006, raised more than $68,000 on Kickstarter in 2010 to record public-domain classics that anyone may use freely. This movement is among the results; Commons credits the players only as “Musopen Symphony”.

    How it is joined

    1. Repeated notes after the B♭ cadence

      The exposition closes in B♭ major. The strings restart the repeated-note figure, then quick string runs meet stronger wind chords. The tempo stays Andante while chromatic notes pull the phrase away from the settled cadence.

    2. The string phrase finds E♭

      The development subsides into short woodwind replies above the strings. The repeated-note opening then returns in E♭ major, with the string entries overlapping again. Its familiar rhythm survives the change from the middle’s crowded exchanges to the quieter opening phrase.

  3. III. Menuetto: AllegrettoMusopen Symphony (Commons credit; ensemble attribution disputed)
    4:33

    Mozart’s G minor minuet stamps across its three-beat bars, with the winds reinforcing the strings. In the Trio, G turns major and the orchestra breaks into smaller, gentler conversations.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A heavy-footed dance

      The strings begin with a rising, strongly accented phrase, reinforced by winds and horns. The minuet’s three beats are there, but the melody pulls across them. This is a splendidly awkward dance.

    2. Strings answer strings

      In the minuet’s second half, the parts pursue one another with fragments of the tune. The lower strings have their own say, pressing against the violins above them. The dance becomes an argument among several voices.

    3. G major opens the room

      The Trio changes G minor to G major. Strings offer a gentler phrase and winds answer it; the horns warm the exchanges. After the minuet’s overlapping arguments, these groups give one another room to finish.

    4. Why the Trio is called a Trio

      The middle dance is called the Trio because the seventeenth-century practice was to score it for three instruments. The name outlived the practice, and Mozart gives his Trio to strings and winds in changing groups.

    5. An entry in July

      On 25 July 1788 Mozart enters this G minor symphony in his catalogue. The E♭ symphony is already entered, on 26 June; the C major Jupiter follows on 10 August. Three very different symphonies come out of one Viennese summer.

    6. Back to the minuet

      “Menuetto da capo”: Mozart sends the orchestra back to the first dance. G minor and the heavy accents return at once. The Trio’s sunny exchanges are over.

    7. A grand symphony in 1791

      In April 1791 the Vienna Tonkünstler-Societät gives its concerts, with Antonio Salieri conducting, and the programme announces “A Grand Symphony composed by Herr Mozart”. This G minor symphony later gains a second version with clarinets, and both versions survive.

    How it is joined

    1. B♭ becomes B♮

      The minuet closes in G minor; the Trio begins in G major. G remains the tonic, while B♭ becomes B♮. The full orchestra withdraws to let the strings begin a lighter phrase, with the wind answer following. There is no connecting passage.

    2. The orchestra stamps back in

      The Trio closes in G major and the da capo instruction brings back the minuet in G minor. Winds and horns reinforce the strings again. The change from B♮ to B♭ restores the opening key, and the rising phrase resumes its forceful accents.

  4. IV. Finale: Allegro assaiMusopen Symphony (Commons credit; ensemble attribution disputed)
    4:45

    A quiet string arpeggio shoots upwards and the orchestra answers fiercely. Even the finale’s lyrical second tune eventually comes back in G minor; Mozart holds to that key right to the end.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Upwards, then the answer

      The strings climb quietly through a G minor chord. The orchestra answers with a forceful rush. Mozart keeps setting the two against each other: the spare rising phrase and the crowded reply.

    2. Violins and winds soften

      The second theme sings in B♭ major, shared by violins and winds. Its longer notes make a welcome space in this fast movement. The accompanying pulse continues underneath.

    3. “Shockingly voluptuous”

      The pianist and critic Charles Rosen finds pleasure inside Mozart’s pain: “In all of Mozart’s supreme expressions of suffering and terror … there is something shockingly voluptuous.”

    4. The leap breaks loose

      At the development, the opening leap returns in a jagged unison, studded with chromatic notes. The strings then chase fragments of it through overlapping entries. The tidy arpeggio has become difficult to pin down.

    5. Horns without trumpets

      The whole symphony uses horns, woodwinds and strings. Mozart leaves out trumpets and timpani. Even the finale’s fiercest answers come from this comparatively small orchestra.

    6. The first leap again

      The quiet rising figure is back in G minor, with its fierce answer still attached. After the development’s chromatic scrambling, the notes of the opening chord are suddenly clear again.

    7. The tune turns minor

      The gentle second theme returns in G minor. Its familiar curves remain, but the earlier B♭ major warmth has gone. The symphony keeps this minor key through its final chords.

    8. The most discussed symphony

      Neal Zaslaw says of K. 550: “No symphony of Mozart’s, not even the ‘Jupiter’, has aroused so much comment as this one.” Otto Jahn, in the nineteenth century, hears in it “sorrow and complaining”, and in the finale “a wild merriment that seeks to drown sorrow”.

    How it is joined

    1. F prepares the gentler tune

      The transition gathers on F, the dominant of B♭ major. After the break, violins and woodwinds share the quieter second theme. Longer notes replace the opening’s rushing answer, while the Allegro assai pulse continues.

    2. A jagged answer to B♭

      The exposition closes in B♭ major. Its successor is the opening leap stretched into a chromatic unison: the orchestra briefly loses the comfort of a supporting chord. The strings then take up fragments in imitation, one entry pursuing another.

    3. D leads back to G

      The running string fragments lead to D, the dominant of G minor. The opening arpeggio returns quietly in the strings over its original G minor harmony. Then comes the familiar forceful answer.

    4. D prepares the changed melody

      The returning transition prepares G minor through its dominant, D. Violins and winds resume the quieter second theme in that home key. The orchestral contrast survives; the earlier change to B♭ major is replaced by a G minor melody.
