Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart / II. Classical

Piano Concerto No. 21, K. 467

1785 · Piano and orchestra

3 recordings · 29 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro maestosoArtur Schnabel, piano; London Symphony Orchestra, Malcolm Sargent · 12 January 1937, Abbey Road Studio 1, London
    13:32

    Mozart begins with a quiet string march, then lets the piano slip in, pause and trill above the returning tune. Artur Schnabel plays with Malcolm Sargent and the London Symphony Orchestra, and supplies his own first-movement cadenza.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A march on tiptoe

      The strings begin a quiet march in C major, with short, separated notes. Woodwind replies and fuller orchestral phrases follow. The piano waits while Mozart supplies it with tunes, rhythms and several ways of answering.

    2. Mozart at the Burgtheater

      Vienna, 10 March 1785: Mozart plays this concerto at the Burgtheater in a concert for his own benefit. The advertisement also promises improvisation on a fortepiano fitted with a large pedal keyboard. The evening puts both his hands and his feet to work.

    3. A trill above the march

      The piano slips into the woodwinds’ conversation, runs up to a pause, then holds a trill while the strings recover their march. The soloist has arrived without taking the tune away from everyone else.

    4. G minor before G major

      The piano’s solo exposition takes a detour into G minor. Chromatic scales lead out of it towards a flowing G major tune. Mozart saves this change of mood for the soloist; the orchestra’s opening has kept it in reserve.

    5. The orchestra answers in G

      The piano’s closing trill hands the phrase to the orchestra. Winds, brass and strings confirm G major, then the strings carry on more quietly. The soloist rests before returning with another new melody.

    6. Another tune for the piano

      The development begins with a new, melancholy piano phrase, lightly accompanied by strings. Soon the soloist is running through rapid figures against held orchestral notes. Mozart keeps finding fresh things for the keyboard to say.

    7. The march comes home

      The strings recover the opening march in C major. When the piano rejoins, its entrance overlaps other statements of the march’s first few notes. The returning tune passes from player to player before the soloist moves on.

    8. Pleased without knowing why

      Writing to his father on 28 December 1782, Mozart describes his earlier concertos as a middle way between too hard and too easy. There are pleasures for connoisseurs, yet everyone else must be satisfied too, “without knowing why”.

    9. Schnabel writes his own

      The orchestra stops on an unfinished cadence and leaves Schnabel alone. This cadenza is his own composition. Mozart’s full score supplies the opening in which it belongs, then waits for the soloist to lead back to the orchestra.

    10. The orchestra tiptoes out

      The orchestra resumes the written close in C major. Its firm opening phrases gradually shrink to a quiet ending, recalling the restraint of the first string march.

    How it is joined

    1. A trill meets the march

      Quiet wind phrases lead into the piano’s first solo. The keyboard runs up to a fermata, then sustains a trill above the strings’ returning C major march. The strings have the melody while the soloist keeps the trill spinning overhead.

    2. A G major orchestral answer

      The piano’s trill leads into the G major tutti. Winds, brass and strings take over the cadence while the soloist rests. The full orchestra then subsides into a quieter string continuation that leads towards the next solo.

    3. A new melody in E minor

      The strings and woodwinds grow quiet after the orchestral bridge. The piano begins a new melody in E minor, with only light string support. The accompaniment soon drops out, leaving the soloist to extend the phrase into running notes.

    4. G holds beneath the piano

      The piano’s rapid figures continue over sustained strings and wind chords. G in the bass prepares C major. The soloist then falls silent as the strings recover the opening march, with the woodwinds answering again.

    5. C major above a waiting G

      The orchestra reaches a C major chord with G in the bass and holds it under a fermata. This cadential six-four leaves the resolution waiting. Schnabel takes over alone for his own cadenza before the orchestra can finish the phrase.

    6. The orchestra completes the cadence

      The piano’s cadenza leads back to the written orchestral close in C major. Winds and strings resume together, with brass and timpani reinforcing the fuller phrases. The final page reduces the sound to a quiet cadence.

  2. II. AndanteArtur Schnabel, piano; London Symphony Orchestra, Malcolm Sargent · 12 January 1937, Abbey Road Studio 1, London
    8:18

    Muted violins sing over repeated triplets and a plucked bass in this F major Andante. Artur Schnabel takes up the melody, which returns later in the surprising key of A♭ before finding its way home.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Muted strings, plucked bass

      Second violins and violas repeat soft triplets above plucked cellos and basses. The muted first violins enter with the melody, taking long breaths over that steady movement. The whole opening seems suspended between a song and a heartbeat.

    2. The melody leans hard

      The violin melody rises into wide leaps and accented dissonances, with winds supporting the clashes. Quiet playing leaves every sharp edge exposed. The triplet accompaniment continues beneath the changing chords.

    3. Two hands, two jobs

      The piano’s left hand takes over the repeated triplets, while the right hand sings the melody. All the strings are plucked at the solo entry. The tune now rests on the pianist’s own quiet accompaniment.

    4. A Bechstein at Abbey Road

      Schnabel records this concerto on a Bechstein at Abbey Road’s Studio 1 on 12 January 1937. Malcolm Sargent conducts the London Symphony Orchestra. The Andante occupies two sides of the original HMV issue.

    5. The winds answer back

      Winds and strings answer the piano, then the soloist resumes above the repeating accompaniment. The replies grow sharper as the middle passage unfolds. Even this hushed Andante has moments when the orchestra insists on being heard.

    6. A♭ catches the returning tune

      After a brief interruption of the repeated accompaniment, the opening melody returns on the piano in A♭ major. The tune is familiar; its key is unexpectedly far from F. Plucked strings and left-hand triplets settle beneath it again.

    7. F major comes into reach

      The piano’s embellished line works back towards F major, with sustained woodwind notes beside it. The triplets keep moving through the final phrases. Only the last cadence releases their quiet persistence.

    How it is joined

    1. The triplets move to the keyboard

      The orchestra closes its opening statement in F major. The piano’s left hand begins the triplets, and all the strings become pizzicato. The right-hand melody follows above this new accompaniment, still in F major.

    2. The winds take the phrase

      The solo passage reaches C major. Winds and strings answer while the piano rests, then the keyboard resumes with triplets beneath a held right-hand note. The plucked bass continues under the exchange.

    3. A pause before A♭

      The repeated accompaniment briefly stops. A connecting piano phrase crosses held string notes, then the opening melody resumes in A♭ major. The left hand restores its triplets and the strings return to pizzicato beneath the tune.

    4. The piano works back towards F

      The returning melody moves out of A♭ through further modulations towards F major. Piano triplets keep the accompaniment moving, while winds hold notes across the soloist’s ornaments. The closing phrases recover F and bring the repeated motion to rest.

  3. III. Allegro vivace assaiArtur Schnabel, piano; London Symphony Orchestra, Malcolm Sargent · 12 January 1937, Abbey Road Studio 1, London
    6:44

    Mozart’s C major finale lets a neat little refrain escape into piano runs and quick woodwind replies. Schnabel and Sargent bring it back repeatedly, with one last cadenza between the tune and its final dash home.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The tune, then a pause

      The strings introduce a quick C major refrain in two beats to the bar. Mozart interrupts the opening exchange with a pause for the piano, which takes up the tune after its flourish. The soloist makes an entrance of it.

    2. Piano runs, horns hold

      The piano begins a connected phrase, marked legato, above held horn notes. Its runs stretch beyond the refrain’s clipped little statements. New ideas follow as the first episode moves away from C major.

    3. A dozen new concertos

      Between February 1784 and December 1786, Mozart writes twelve piano concertos. Vienna’s audiences usually have the composer himself at the keyboard. K. 467 is one of the new scores he brings to the platform in 1785.

    4. C major after the pause

      A pause clears the way for the piano to recover the refrain in C major. The orchestra takes it up in turn. The same short tune can sound like a solo remark or a roomful of people agreeing.

    5. The refrain comes to pieces

      The middle episode breaks the refrain into smaller figures. Piano and woodwinds trade them in quick replies, and the keyboard spins them into longer runs. A few notes from the tune are enough to start each exchange.

    6. The tune launches another run

      The refrain returns in C major, now leading into more elaborate piano writing. The strings keep the short phrases moving beneath the soloist’s excursions. Mozart still has a cadenza in store.

    7. Schnabel has the floor

      A short orchestral tutti stops at a fermata, leaving the piano alone. The cadenza postpones the final cadence and gives Schnabel a longer solo turn after the rondo’s quick exchanges.

    8. The refrain gets the last word

      Piano and strings recover the refrain after the cadenza. Fuller orchestral replies and rising piano scales carry the closing passage towards C major. Trumpets and timpani are back for the final chords.

    How it is joined

    1. Held horns beneath the piano

      The orchestral refrain closes in C major. The piano resumes with a legato phrase over sustained horn notes; the strings initially rest. This lighter scoring lets the soloist extend the tune into a more continuous run of notes.

    2. A fermata before the refrain

      Piano runs and held string notes lead to a fermata on the dominant of C. After the pause, the piano recovers the refrain in C major over strings. The orchestra then repeats the tune.

    3. The orchestra carries the tune onward

      The orchestral continuation carries the refrain away from C major. Short piano phrases and woodwind replies take up pieces of the tune, repeating them at different pitches. The two-beat Allegro continues through these changing keys.

    4. G prepares the piano’s return

      A short orchestral link gathers on G, the dominant of C major. The piano resumes the refrain over the strings. The familiar two-beat phrase returns intact after the middle’s broken exchanges.

    5. The cadence waits on G

      The orchestra plays a short tutti and stops on a C major chord over a bass G. The fermata holds this cadential six-four unresolved. Schnabel continues alone in the cadenza before piano and strings can resume the refrain.

    6. Piano and strings restore the refrain

      The cadenza leads back to the refrain in C major, with the piano above the strings’ regular accompaniment. Winds, brass and timpani join the closing passage. Rising keyboard scales carry the music to the final cadence.
