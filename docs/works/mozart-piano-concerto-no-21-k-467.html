Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart / II. Classical

Piano Concerto No. 21, K. 467

1785 · Piano and orchestra

3 recordings · 29 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro maestosoArtur Schnabel, piano; London Symphony Orchestra, Malcolm Sargent · 12 January 1937, Abbey Road Studio 1, London
    13:32

    The first movement of Mozart’s Piano Concerto No. 21 in C major, K. 467, Allegro maestoso, opens with a quiet march in the strings; the piano enters and, after a pause, trills above the returning theme. Artur Schnabel is the soloist with Malcolm Sargent and the London Symphony Orchestra, and plays his own cadenza.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening march, strings

      The strings begin a march in C major, piano, in short, separated notes. The woodwind reply, and fuller orchestral phrases follow. The piano does not play during this opening.

    2. First performance, Burgtheater, 1785

      Mozart played this concerto at the Burgtheater in Vienna on 10 March 1785, in a concert for his own benefit. The advertisement also announced that he would improvise on a fortepiano fitted with a large pedal keyboard.

    3. Entry of the piano, bars 70–⁠83

      The piano enters during the woodwind passage and runs up to a fermata. It then holds a trill while the strings play the march theme again.

    4. G minor episode, solo exposition

      In the solo exposition the piano moves into G minor. Chromatic scales lead out of it to a flowing tune in G major. Neither the G minor passage nor this tune appears in the orchestral exposition.

    5. Recording, Abbey Road, 12 January 1937

      Artur Schnabel, Malcolm Sargent and the London Symphony Orchestra recorded the concerto on 12 January 1937 in Abbey Road’s Studio 1, with Lawrance Collingwood producing. The same booking, which began on 9 January, also covered the F major Concerto, K. 459.

    6. Orchestral tutti in G major

      After the piano’s closing trill the orchestra takes over. Winds, brass and strings confirm G major, then the strings continue more quietly. The soloist rests, then returns with another new melody.

    7. New piano theme, development

      The development begins at bar 222 with a new phrase in the piano, lightly accompanied by strings. The soloist then plays rapid figures against held notes in the orchestra.

    8. Recapitulation, from bar 274

      The strings play the opening march again in C major. The piano re-enters while other instruments are stating the first few notes of the march, so the entries overlap. The tune passes between the players before the soloist goes on to new material.

    9. HMV issue on four discs, 1937

      HMV issued the concerto in November 1937 on four discs, DB 3099–⁠3102, eight sides in all. The first movement took four sides, and the last of them carried Schnabel’s cadenza. The Andante and the finale took two each.

    10. Letter to Leopold Mozart, 1782

      Writing to his father on 28 December 1782, Mozart described his earlier concertos as a middle way between too hard and too easy. They held passages for connoisseurs, and other listeners were also to be satisfied, “without knowing why”.

    11. Cadenza by Schnabel

      The orchestra stops on an unfinished cadence and Schnabel plays alone. The cadenza is his own composition. Mozart’s full score marks the place for it, and the orchestra re-enters after the soloist leads back.

    12. Written close in C major

      The orchestra resumes the written close in C major. Its firm opening phrases gradually shrink to a quiet ending, recalling the restraint of the first string march.

    Joins

    1. Piano trill over the returning march

      Quiet wind phrases lead into the piano’s first solo. The piano runs up to a fermata, then holds a trill above the strings, which return with the C major march. The strings have the melody and the soloist continues the trill above them.

    2. G major tutti after the piano’s trill

      The piano’s trill leads into the G major tutti. Winds, brass and strings take over the cadence while the soloist rests. The full orchestra then drops to a quieter continuation for strings, which leads towards the next solo.

    3. Piano melody in E minor

      After the orchestral bridge the strings and woodwinds play more softly. The piano begins a new melody in E minor, with light accompaniment from the strings. The accompaniment soon stops, and the soloist continues the phrase alone in running notes.

    4. G in the bass beneath the piano

      In bars 264–⁠280, the piano’s rapid figures continue over sustained chords in the strings and wind. G in the bass prepares C major. The soloist then stops, the strings return to the opening march, and the woodwinds again alternate with them.

    5. Cadential six-four into Schnabel’s cadenza

      The orchestra reaches a C major chord with G in the bass and holds it under a fermata. The chord is a cadential six-four, and the resolution is deferred. Schnabel enters alone with his own cadenza before the orchestra completes the phrase.

    6. The orchestra completes the cadence

      The piano’s cadenza leads back to the written orchestral close in C major. Winds and strings resume together, and brass and timpani reinforce the fuller phrases. On the final page the sound is reduced to a quiet cadence.

  2. II. AndanteArtur Schnabel, piano; London Symphony Orchestra, Malcolm Sargent · 12 January 1937, Abbey Road Studio 1, London
    8:18

    In this F major Andante from the Piano Concerto No. 21 in C major, K. 467, muted violins play the melody over repeated triplets and a plucked bass. Artur Schnabel takes up the melody, which later returns in A♭ before coming back to F major.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, muted strings and pizzicato bass

      Second violins and violas repeat soft triplets above plucked cellos and basses. The muted first violins enter with the melody in long phrases over this accompaniment.

    2. Violin melody, leaps and dissonances

      The violin melody rises into wide leaps and accented dissonances, and the winds support the clashes. The dynamic stays soft. The triplet accompaniment continues beneath the changing chords.

    3. Solo entry, piano

      At the solo entry the piano’s left hand takes over the repeated triplets and the right hand plays the melody. All the strings play pizzicato.

    4. The film Elvira Madigan, 1967

      The Swedish film Elvira Madigan (1967) used this Andante, in a recording by Géza Anda, and the concerto took its nickname from the film. Schnabel’s performance, heard here, was made thirty years earlier.

    5. Recording, Abbey Road, 1937

      Schnabel recorded this concerto on a Bechstein in Abbey Road’s Studio 1 on 12 January 1937, with Malcolm Sargent conducting the London Symphony Orchestra. The Andante took up two sides of the original HMV issue.

    6. Middle section, winds and strings

      Winds and strings alternate with the piano, then the soloist resumes above the repeated accompaniment. The orchestral phrases become sharper as the middle passage goes on.

    7. Return of the opening melody, A♭ major

      After a brief break in the repeated accompaniment, the piano plays the opening melody again in A♭ major, a key far from F. Plucked strings and left-hand triplets return beneath it.

    8. LP reissue, COLH 67, 1959

      This stream is Bob Varney’s transfer of the Angel LP COLH 67, issued in the series “Great Recordings of the Century”. The London Symphony Orchestra’s discography dates that reissue to November 1959, twenty-two years after the Abbey Road session.

    9. Return to F major

      The piano’s embellished line modulates back towards F major, with sustained woodwind notes beside it. The triplets continue through the final phrases and stop at the last cadence.

    Joins

    1. Triplets pass to the piano

      The orchestra ends its opening statement in F major. The piano’s left hand takes up the triplets, and all the strings play pizzicato. The right-hand melody enters above this accompaniment, still in F major.

    2. Winds and strings during the piano’s rest

      The solo passage reaches C major. Winds and strings play while the piano rests, then the piano resumes with triplets beneath a held right-hand note. The pizzicato bass continues throughout.

    3. Link to the melody in A♭ major

      The repeated accompaniment stops for a short time. Over held notes in the strings, the piano plays a connecting phrase, and the opening melody then returns in A♭ major. Under the tune the left hand takes up its triplets again and the strings go back to pizzicato.

    4. The return from A♭ to F major

      The returning melody modulates out of A♭ by further steps towards F major. Piano triplets continue the accompaniment, and the winds hold notes under the soloist’s ornaments. The closing phrases return to F, and the repeated triplet motion stops.

  3. III. Allegro vivace assaiArtur Schnabel, piano; London Symphony Orchestra, Malcolm Sargent · 12 January 1937, Abbey Road Studio 1, London
    6:44

    Schnabel plays the finale, Allegro vivace assai, of Mozart’s Piano Concerto No. 21 in C major, K. 467, with Sargent conducting. The refrain returns several times between piano runs and short woodwind replies, and a last cadenza comes between the final statement of the tune and the closing bars.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Refrain and first solo entry

      The strings introduce a quick C major refrain in two beats to the bar. Mozart breaks off the opening exchange with a pause for the piano, which plays a flourish and then takes up the tune.

    2. First episode, piano legato

      The piano plays a connected phrase, marked legato, above held horn notes. Its runs extend beyond the short statements of the refrain. New ideas follow as the first episode modulates away from C major.

    3. Twelve piano concertos, 1784–⁠1786

      Between February 1784 and December 1786, Mozart wrote twelve piano concertos. Vienna’s audiences usually heard them with the composer himself at the keyboard. K. 467 was one of the new scores he brought to the platform in 1785.

    4. Return of the refrain in C major

      After a pause the piano plays the refrain in C major. The orchestra then takes it up.

    5. Middle episode, refrain motifs

      The middle episode breaks the refrain into smaller figures. Piano and woodwinds exchange them in quick alternation, and the piano extends them into longer runs. Each exchange begins with a few notes from the refrain.

    6. Return of the refrain, C major

      The refrain returns in C major and leads into more elaborate piano writing. The strings continue the short phrases beneath the solo part. A cadenza follows later in the movement.

    7. Cadenza

      A short orchestral tutti stops at a fermata, and the piano plays alone. The cadenza delays the final cadence and gives Schnabel a longer solo passage after the quick exchanges of the rondo.

    8. Original discs and LP transfer

      On the original discs the finale fills both sides of a single HMV record, DB 3102, matrices 2EA 4610 and 4611. This stream comes from a later LP transfer, so the movement plays without a break.

    9. Return of the refrain after the cadenza

      After the cadenza, piano and strings play the refrain again. Fuller orchestral replies and rising piano scales carry the closing passage towards C major, and trumpets and timpani return for the final chords.

    Joins

    1. Held horns beneath the piano

      The orchestral refrain closes in C major. The piano resumes with a legato phrase over sustained horn notes, and the strings rest at first. Over this lighter scoring the soloist extends the tune into a more continuous run of notes.

    2. A fermata before the refrain

      Piano runs over held string notes lead to a fermata on the dominant of C. After the pause, the piano plays the refrain in C major over the strings, and the orchestra then repeats the tune.

    3. Orchestral continuation leaves C major

      The orchestral continuation takes the refrain away from C major. Short piano phrases alternate with woodwind phrases, each taking up fragments of the tune and repeating them at different pitches. The two-beat Allegro continues through these changing keys.

    4. Dominant link to the piano’s return

      A short orchestral link holds G, the dominant of C major. The piano then takes up the refrain again over the strings. The two-beat phrase returns unchanged, following the broken exchanges of the middle section.

    5. Fermata on the cadential six-four

      The orchestra plays a short tutti and stops on a C major chord over a bass G (bars 418–⁠423). The fermata holds this cadential six-four unresolved. Schnabel played the cadenza alone, and then piano and strings resume the refrain.

    6. Piano and strings restore the refrain

      The cadenza leads back to the refrain in C major, with the piano above the strings’ regular accompaniment. Winds, brass and timpani join the closing passage. Rising keyboard scales carry the music to the final cadence.
