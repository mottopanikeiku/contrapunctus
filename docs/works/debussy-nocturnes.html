Claude Debussy / V. The French Line

Nocturnes

1899 · Orchestra; women’s chorus in Sirènes

Clouds, a festival, sirens. Nuages drifts on a phrase for cor anglais; in Fêtes a procession of muted trumpets approaches from far off; Sirènes adds women’s voices without words.

2 recordings · 13 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Nuages — ModéréOrchestre de la Suisse Romande; Ernest Ansermet, conductor · October 1951, Victoria Hall, Geneva
    7:11

    Clarinets and bassoons rock slowly beneath a recurring cor anglais phrase in Nuages, the first of Debussy’s three Nocturnes. A flute and harp briefly let in a different light.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Clarinets and bassoons

      Clarinets and bassoons begin alone, rocking slowly between chords. The strings have nothing to play yet. An oboe adds a held note, then a small falling phrase, while the chords continue underneath.

    2. The cor anglais phrase

      The cor anglais has a phrase that keeps coming back with almost the same shape. Its dry, slightly nasal voice sits apart from the soft chords around it. Debussy lets it return without growing into a long song.

    3. Whistler’s borrowed word

      Whistler calls his paintings of night and water Nocturnes, borrowing a musical title. Debussy takes the word back for his orchestra: his programme describes clouds fading through shades of grey and white.

    4. Two movements in Paris

      Camille Chevillard conducts Nuages and Fêtes in Paris on 9 December 1900. The third movement, Sirènes, waits nearly another year: the complete triptych reaches the stage on 27 October 1901.

    5. Flute and harp together

      The middle passage moves to F♯ major. Flute and harp share a melody made from five notes, above strings held softly in many parts. Un peu animé — a little more animated — gives the tune a gentler forward swing.

    6. The old phrase returns

      The cor anglais recovers its opening phrase. Debussy restores the first tempo, and the flute-and-harp tune falls away. The lower strings play near the fingerboard, softening the edge of their held notes.

    7. A few plucked notes

      Plus lent, then Encore plus lent: slower, then slower still. Bassoon and flute pick up scraps of the earlier phrases. Muted horns linger above the final plucked strings, with a soft timpani roll underneath.

    How it is joined

    1. Flute and harp let in light

      At Un peu animé, the score changes to six sharps. Flute and harp share the five-note melody in F♯ major; the divided strings hold a quiet chord underneath. The bass sustains while the tune moves freely above it, replacing the opening’s rocking wind chords.

    2. The cor anglais comes back

      The flute’s tune sinks and softens. At 1er tempo, the cor anglais restores its old phrase over held clarinet and bassoon notes. The key signature returns to two sharps. Violas, cellos and basses join quietly, bowing near the fingerboard.

    3. The phrases break apart

      The cor anglais phrase shortens, and bassoons take up the rocking chords. The score slows twice, through Plus lent and Encore plus lent. Timpani hold the low B while the string bows stop; the final wind fragments sound above quiet pizzicato notes.

  2. II. Fêtes — Animé et très rythméOrchestre de la Suisse Romande; Ernest Ansermet, conductor · October 1951, Victoria Hall, Geneva
    6:16

    A procession of muted trumpets approaches through the quick violin figures of Fêtes. For a few bars the two fill the orchestra together; at the end, only a distant drum and the low strings keep moving.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Violins under high flutes

      The violins begin with quick repeated triplets, sharply accented. High flutes hold above them. Cor anglais and clarinets soon join the dance, sending an upward phrase across the string rhythm.

    2. A procession through the festival

      Debussy’s programme puts a fantastic procession inside the festivities. It approaches, passes through the crowd and merges with the dance. His orchestra has to keep the marching band and the dancers going at once.

    3. A distant tread

      A♭ and E♭ repeat in the bass, with very soft timpani and harp. The metre changes to 2/4. Muted trumpets bring the procession’s broad tune over this steady tread, still far away.

    4. The two crowds meet

      The trumpet tune now has trombones beneath it. Fast violin figures and little woodwind bursts surround the march, with snare drum and cymbals joining in. Debussy asks the brass for a full tone without harshness.

    5. Flutes after the march

      The first tempo returns in 6/8. Flutes run above short reed phrases and a quick cello pulse. The heavy brass falls silent, leaving the dance on its feet again.

    6. Chevillard’s first two nocturnes

      At the first performance in Paris on 9 December 1900, Camille Chevillard conducts just Nuages and Fêtes. Sirènes, with its wordless women’s chorus, joins them at the complete première the following October.

    7. The drum keeps going

      The wind players finish their last fragments. A snare drum continues very softly, with cellos and basses playing at the tip of the bow. Debussy asks the low strings to move ever farther away. The festival disappears while its feet are still moving.

    How it is joined

    1. A♭ and E♭ begin the march

      Modéré mais toujours très rythmé: moderate, still strongly rhythmic. The dance contracts to quiet harp, timpani and plucked basses, with A♭ and E♭ repeating in 2/4. Cellos join the tread, then muted trumpets unfold the procession’s tune above it.

    2. The dance surrounds the march

      The marching bass continues as trumpets and trombones broaden the tune. Above them, quick violin figures and woodwind triplets bring back the dance. Snare drum and cymbals join the crescendo. The brass marking asks for a full, ringing tone without harshness.

    3. The flutes take over

      At 1er tempo, 6/8 replaces the procession’s 2/4. The brass and percussion stop after the first chord, and flutes carry a running line above short reed figures. Repeated cello notes keep the bass moving beneath this lighter dance.

    4. The procession moves away

      The late dance breaks into short wind phrases and quieter brass replies. Low-string figures keep running as the other players fall silent. In the final bars the snare drum returns at ppp, beside cellos and basses playing lightly at the tip of the bow.
