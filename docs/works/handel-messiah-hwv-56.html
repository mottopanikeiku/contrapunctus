George Frideric Handel / I. The Baroque

Messiah, HWV 56

1741; first performed 1742 · HWV 56 · Soloists, chorus, orchestra and continuo

Jennens compiled the words from scripture; Dublin heard the first performance on 13 April 1742. Handel’s later changes for different singers left several versions of individual numbers.

3 min

The recording

  1. HallelujahLondon Symphony Orchestra; London Philharmonic Choir; Hermann Scherchen, conductor; Frederick Jackson, chorus master · 1953
    3:21

    Handel closes Part II of Messiah with a chorus that can shout together, chase a tune through four voices, or hold one note above the commotion. Hermann Scherchen conducts the London Symphony Orchestra and London Philharmonic Choir.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The strings say it first

      The first violins already have the rhythm of “Hallelujah”. Three bars later the choir takes up the word, all four parts together. D major, short cries, and little answering runs in the strings: the celebration begins without ceremony.

    2. The kingdom of this world

      The quick cries stop. “The kingdom of this world” moves in measured crotchets, the choir speaking together over a descending bass. Handel leaves room around the words before “the kingdom of our Lord” carries the phrase upwards.

    3. The basses start a fugue

      The basses begin “And he shall reign” with a tune that leaps up and down through the chord. Tenors, altos and sopranos take it in turn. Short “Hallelujah” cries keep returning around the tune.

    4. Long notes above the choir

      “King of kings” begins on a held A in the sopranos and altos. The sopranos then climb through D, E, F♯ and G, stretching each proclamation over the busy answers below.

    5. A pause before the end

      The repeated “Hallelujah” cries break off. After the rest, the choir has one last, broad statement of the word. The bass moves from G to D beneath it, and Handel holds the final D major chord under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The basses take “For the Lord”

      The basses take “For the Lord God omnipotent reigneth” on D, rising through E and F♯. The first violins double the tune low in their range, while the sopranos rest. The opening’s four-part shouts separate into a melody and its shorter “Hallelujah” answers.

    2. An A held above the answers

      The fugue reaches A major. Sopranos and altos stay on A for “King of kings”, holding across the barline; tenors and basses answer with short “Hallelujah” figures. The strings join those quick replies, leaving the long vocal note hanging above them.

    3. The basses reopen the fugue

      The sopranos release their high G and descend towards E as the choir settles on A major. The basses bring back “And he shall reign”, and the other voices move into the leaping tune again. The first violins join the sopranos’ entry.

    4. A rest before the final word

      Choir and orchestra interrupt their repeated “Hallelujah” figures with a rest. The last statement begins in longer notes, and the bass moves from G to D for the plagal cadence. All four voices finish together on the D major chord, held under a fermata.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. HallelujahOratorio Chorus; conductor and accompanying players uncredited · 1916
    3:43

    The Oratorio Chorus brings Handel’s Hallelujah to an Edison Diamond Disc in 1916. Four choral parts turn a handful of biblical phrases into shouts, long proclamations and pursuing tunes.

    Notes as it plays
    1. One word, four voices

      The choir begins with short, shared cries of “Hallelujah”. Soon Handel has the parts pursuing one another through longer lines. The same four voices can sound like a crowd speaking together or four people all eager to speak.

    2. Handel on an Edison disc

      In May 1916 Edison issues this performance on Diamond Disc 80292. The singers appear on the label as the Oratorio Chorus. Handel’s great public celebration now fits on one side of a record.

    3. Room for more in Dublin

      For the first Messiah, in Dublin in April 1742, demand is so great that the organisers ask men to leave their swords at home and women to come without hooped skirts. They need every inch of the Music Hall in Fishamble Street.

    4. Two lengths of note

      Handel gives “King of kings” long notes and “Hallelujah” quick ones. The choir can proclaim both at once: one part holds its place while another dances around it.

    5. The final broad Hallelujah

      At the close Handel brings the choir together for one last, spacious “Hallelujah”. The bass moves from G to D, and the final D major chord carries a fermata: everyone holds the same ending.

  2. HallelujahRoyal Choral Society; Royal Albert Hall Orchestra; Malcolm Sargent, conductor · 2 April 1926, Royal Albert Hall, London
    4:25

    Malcolm Sargent conducts the Royal Choral Society and Royal Albert Hall Orchestra at the Albert Hall on 2 April 1926. Handel’s short cries of praise grow into long notes that hang above the racing choral lines.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Three bars before the choir

      The strings introduce the rhythm of “Hallelujah” before anyone sings. When the choir enters, all four parts speak together; the violins slip little answering phrases between the cries.

    2. A sentence in crotchets

      “The kingdom of this world” slows the choir’s syllables to measured crotchets. The four parts move together, and the bass descends beneath them. After so many short cries, Handel lets a whole sentence unfold.

    3. A tune passed upwards

      The basses start “And he shall reign”; tenors, altos and sopranos follow in turn. The tune leaps through the chord, then runs down in smaller steps.

    4. Inside the Albert Hall

      This is a concert at the Royal Albert Hall, preserved on disc on 2 April 1926. The Royal Choral Society sings with the hall’s orchestra; Malcolm Sargent conducts.

    5. The sopranos keep climbing

      The sopranos take “King of kings” up to D, then climb through E and F♯ to G. Each note lasts across barlines. The lower voices keep answering, briskly, beneath that steadily rising line.

    6. The word after the rest

      The quick repetitions stop, leaving a rest before the last “Hallelujah”. This time the word fills long notes. G in the bass settles on D, and the choir holds the final chord under a fermata.
