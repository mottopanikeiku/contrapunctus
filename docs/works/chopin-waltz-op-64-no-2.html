Frédéric Chopin / III. Romanticism

Waltz, Op. 64 No. 2

1847 · Piano

Recordings

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. Waltz in C♯ minor, Op. 64 No. 2Olga Gurevich, piano · c. 2014
    3:50

    Olga Gurevich plays a waltz with three distinct voices: a sighing C♯ minor melody, quick descending quavers and a slower song in D♭ major. The running tune keeps coming back between the others.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Held notes, then a curl

      Two notes sound together in the right hand and hang across the bar line. A small, quick curl answers them. Below, the left hand keeps leaping from a bass note to two chords. Chopin marks this opening Tempo giusto.

    2. Quavers take over

      Più mosso: more movement. The right hand runs down in chains of quavers while the left keeps the bass-and-two-chords pattern. This is the waltz’s recurring tune; it will return after each of the broader melodies.

    3. Five flats on the page

      Four sharps become five flats: D♭ major, the bright twin of C♯ minor on the keyboard. The marking is Più lento, slower. Long melody notes open out above chords that carry their own little answering lines.

    4. After school in Baku

      As a girl in Baku, Gurevich goes from ordinary school to music school: piano lessons, solfège, music history and choir. At seventeen she moves to Moscow to study at the Gnesin Academy. New York becomes her home in 1998, where she performs and teaches.

    5. One last descent

      The running refrain gets the last word. Its quavers fall, climb back up and fall again; the left hand continues its three-beat tread beneath them. The waltz closes in C♯ minor.

  2. Waltz in C♯ minor, Op. 64 No. 2Ignacy Jan Paderewski, piano (Steinway) · 1917
    3:18

    Paderewski plays Chopin’s C♯ minor Waltz in a 1917 recording. He is a passionate defender of rubato, the freedom to stretch and quicken a phrase; Chopin’s score already asks the pianist to move between three different paces.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A melody in pairs

      The right hand begins in two voices. Their long notes hang over the bar line before a quick flourish answers. The left hand supplies a bass note and two chords in each bar. Tempo giusto is Chopin’s opening instruction.

    2. The enemy of the metronome

      In his 1909 essay, Paderewski calls rubato “this irreconcilable foe of the metronome”. He rejects the idea that every moment borrowed by slowing must be paid back by speeding up. For a solo pianist, he writes, “What is lost is lost.”

    3. The refrain between the songs

      Chopin builds the waltz around the running Più mosso tune. It appears three times: after the opening melody, after the D♭ major song and once more at the end. The quick descent is the familiar face between the longer conversations.

    4. Six quavers against three beats

      The final Più mosso puts six running quavers above the left hand’s three beats. The melody falls in slurred arcs, then climbs high to begin again. Its last descent brings the piano home to C♯ minor.

  3. Waltz in C♯ minor, Op. 64 No. 2Walter Morse Rummel, piano · 18 January 1943
    4:26

    Walter Morse Rummel, a pianist closely associated with Debussy, records Chopin’s C♯ minor Waltz in January 1943. The piece moves between a chordal melody, a running refrain and a slower D♭ major song.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Two voices in one hand

      Chopin writes the opening melody in paired notes, with one voice sustained beneath the other. Little flourishes loosen the line; the bass keeps its waltz pattern. The first change of pace, Più mosso, will send the upper voice running down in quavers.

    2. An invitation from Paderewski

      On his way to Paris in 1908, Rummel meets Paderewski in Switzerland. Paderewski likes his compositions and invites him to stay for a year as a pupil. Rummel turns him down and continues to Paris, where he meets Debussy.

    3. Bach on Rummel’s piano

      Rummel’s piano also carries music written for other instruments and for voices. He arranges organ works by Bach and Vivaldi, and fills four books with piano versions of movements from Bach’s cantatas. Several singing parts must live under ten fingers.

    4. The refrain comes home

      The quicker tune closes the waltz in C♯ minor. Its slurred quavers keep descending across the bass’s three beats, with upward runs resetting the phrase. The final right-hand leap and left-hand chord bring both hands to rest.
