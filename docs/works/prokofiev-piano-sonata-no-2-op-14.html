Sergei Prokofiev / VI. Modernism

Piano Sonata No. 2, Op. 14

1912 · Piano

16 min

The recording

  1. Complete · Allegro ma non troppo — Scherzo — Andante — VivaceE.M. Zinger · 8 October 1962
    16:19

    Prokofiev’s Second Sonata lets the piano sing, then makes it bite. A tune from the first movement returns inside the rushing tarantella finale, like someone trying to finish a sentence in a crowded room.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Two against three

      The right hand groups its melody in pairs while triplet arpeggios run underneath. The hands share a beat but divide it differently. Soon the piano turns sharply percussive; then the opening song comes back.

    2. A quiet song after the bridge

      A faster bridge in 2/4 leads into 3/4 and the second idea: quiet, sweetly lyrical, the opposite of the percussive writing before it. The development will linger on this tune.

    3. A student’s earlier pieces

      Prokofiev builds the sonata partly from music already in his drawer. An earlier sonatina grows into the first movement; a piece for Lyadov’s composition class becomes the scherzo. In 1912 he adds the Andante and finale.

    4. The opening returns, almost whispered

      The development closes quietly and the recapitulation rounds off the opening bars only slightly. The faster bridge returns marked pp, then the 3/4 second idea, pp dolce. A later phrase carries the direction tristemente: sadly.

    5. Sabaneyev on the Concerto

      In the summer of 1912 Prokofiev plays his First Piano Concerto in Moscow, to a warm public welcome. The critic Leonid Sabaneyev calls it harsh and coarse, primitive and cacophonous. The Second Sonata, dated 1912 in the printed score, comes from the same year.

    6. The scherzo snaps back

      The scherzo is marked Allegro marcato: fast, with the notes clearly struck. Brief phrases jab against repeated chords. Its middle section loosens the pace before the clipped opening returns.

    7. A song over rocking bass

      The Andante begins with a rocking bass and a melody that has time to stretch. The piano’s singing voice grows more urgent over that repeated motion, before a gentler middle section opens up.

    8. Dedicated to a friend

      Prokofiev dedicates the sonata to Maximilian Schmidthof, a friend and fellow student at the St Petersburg Conservatory. Schmidthof takes his own life in 1913, before the sonata is first played in 1914.

    9. Between the song and bass

      In the Andante’s middle section, a chromatic line winds between the melody and the bass. It moves in semitones while the upper voice sings more broadly. For a few bars, the accompaniment shifts into an uneven seven-quaver measure.

    10. The melodies matter

      Writing his autobiography in 1941, Prokofiev puts the lyrical side of his music ahead of its toccata-like drive. He remembers how little attention critics paid to the melodies of his youth. This slow movement gives the piano plenty of room to sing.

    11. The tarantella takes off

      The finale dances in quick groups of three. Leaps and sharp accents interrupt the running notes, and the first movement’s singing second theme returns for a brief visit. Then the tarantella carries on to the end.

    12. Perpetual motion, high theme

      The finale is a perpetual motion: a tightly pulsing rhythm under a sharp, ironic theme high on the keyboard. Arrigo Quattrocchi calls it the high point of the sonata and a prototype, perhaps unequalled, of many similar pages by Prokofiev.

    13. Prokofiev at the piano

      On 5 February 1914, Prokofiev gives the sonata its first public performance in Moscow. Jurgenson has already published it the previous year. The composer is still a student at the St Petersburg Conservatory.

    How it is joined

    1. The piano changes its stride

      The first movement ends its long D-minor argument. The separate Scherzo starts with short accents and repeating piano chords, marked Allegro marcato. The hands now strike out compact rhythmic phrases after the first movement’s singing line and triplet arpeggios.

    2. A rocking bass replaces the scherzo

      The Scherzo finishes before the Andante begins. The piano settles into a slower rocking bass, and a long melody rises above it. Repeated chords have carried the short movement; now the upper voice can hold its notes across several changes beneath it.

    3. The piano begins a tarantella

      The slow movement closes; the new marking is Vivace. Fast groups of three replace the Andante’s longer notes. The pianist’s hands drive the tarantella together, with leaps and accents cutting across its running figures.
