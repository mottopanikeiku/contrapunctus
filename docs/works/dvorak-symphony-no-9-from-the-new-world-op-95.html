Antonín Dvořák / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 9 “From the New World”, Op. 95

1893 · Op. 95 · B 178 · Orchestra, including cor anglais

Anton Seidl conducted the première in New York on 16 December 1893. The last movement brings back themes from all three earlier movements, including the cor anglais melody of the Largo.

4 recordings · 42 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Adagio — Allegro moltoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    10:05

    The first movement of the New World Symphony, Dvořák’s Symphony No. 9 in E minor, Op. 95, opens with an Adagio in the lower strings before a horn call enters. The horn call returns in all four movements, including passages far from E minor.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, lower strings

      Violas and cellos begin softly, with basses underneath. The violins rest while clarinets, bassoons and horns play short phrases. These phrases already contain the outline of the horn call that is the main theme of the Allegro.

    2. Introduction and its later recurrences

      Dvořák asked for the whole introduction to be “drawn out, where possible”. Its opening melodic outline recurs in other works from his American years, in the third movement of the E♭ major String Quintet and in the Humoresque No. 1.

    3. First theme of the Allegro, horns

      From bar 24 the horns play the Allegro’s theme in E minor above quiet string chords and pizzicato basses. It opens with leaps in the manner of a fanfare, and the answering phrase falls. Dvořák brought this theme back in every later movement.

    4. Letter to Emil Kozánek, 1893

      On 12 April 1893 Dvořák wrote from New York to Emil Kozánek about his new symphony: “It pleases me very much and will differ considerably from my early compositions.” He finished the score on 24 May.

    5. Second theme, flutes and oboes

      From bar 91 the second theme begins in G minor, shared by flutes and oboes above a repeated horn note. It stays within a small compass and uses F♮, the lowered seventh. Later the same theme returns in the major as a dance.

    6. Copying the score, Spillville, 1893

      In summer 1893, on his first afternoon in Spillville, Iowa, Dvořák gave his assistant Josef Kovařík the 128-page score to copy at four pages a day. Kovařík did so, and Dvořák complained that they were “quite empty ones”. When Kovařík copied sixteen, Dvořák said angrily: “That’s dreadful work, I should know!”

    7. Third theme, solo flute

      A solo flute plays the third theme in G major. Its opening has the rhythm of the horn call. The strings hold sustained chords beneath it.

    8. Flute theme and <i>Swing Low, Sweet Chariot</i>

      The flute’s tune is often compared with the spiritual Swing Low, Sweet Chariot, and most scholars consider its outline and rhythm too close to be chance. Dvořák told the New York Herald he had “not actually used any of the melodies”, only the spirit of Black and Native American songs.

    9. Development

      The development takes up the horn call and the opening of the flute melody. The music modulates quickly from A major to A minor, then to F major and F♯ minor. The phrases return over moving bass notes.

    10. New York Herald, 21 May 1893

      On 21 May 1893, three days before he finished the score, the New York Herald printed Dvořák’s views under the headline “Real Value of Negro Melodies”. “These are the folk songs of America,” he said, “and your composers must turn to them.”

    11. Recapitulation

      The opening call returns in E minor. The other themes follow a semitone higher. The second begins in G♯ minor, and the flute melody returns in A♭ major.

    12. Harry T. Burleigh and Dvořák, New York

      In New York, Harry T. Burleigh sang spirituals for Dvořák, and the two met repeatedly before Dvořák began the symphony. The five-note scales and displaced accents of these songs informed the melodies he wrote for it.

    13. Jeannette Thurber’s National Conservatory

      Jeannette Thurber founded New York’s National Conservatory in 1885 and admitted students whatever their race or sex. In 1892 she offered Dvořák its directorship. On 23 January 1894 he conducted its student orchestra and an all-Black choir of 130 at Madison Square Garden Concert Hall, for the Herald’s clothing fund.

    14. Coda in E minor

      In the coda the horn call passes up through the brass, with timpani prominent beneath the full orchestra. The flute melody earlier moved to A♭ major. The movement ends in E minor.

    Joins

    1. Adagio into Allegro molto

      The Adagio’s last loud passage ends on B, the dominant of E minor. The strings hold as the metre changes to 2/4. At Allegro molto, the horns play the main tune over string chords, with the cellos and basses plucked.

    2. Flutes and oboes begin the G-minor theme

      The running string phrases grow quieter. From bar 91, flutes and oboes play the G-minor tune above sustained strings, and a solo horn repeats one note underneath. The melody uses F♮ in place of the leading note F♯.

    3. Solo flute enters in G major

      The preceding wind phrases come to rest, and the moving strings drop in volume to held chords. At bar 149 a solo flute plays the closing tune in G major. Its dotted opening recalls the rhythm of the main call. The brass and double basses are silent.

    4. From the G-major close to the development

      The exposition closes in G major, and the development begins in A major. Dvořák moves the main call and the first phrase of the flute song through a sequence of keys. A major turns to A minor, and the music then moves to F major and F♯ minor.

    5. Return to E minor and the main theme

      The modulating passage leads back to E minor and the main theme. The rising first phrase and falling second phrase return in their opening pattern. The recapitulation then follows the order of themes in the exposition, and the later wind themes are transposed up a semitone.

    6. The flute tune returns a semitone higher

      The returning fanfare drops in volume and leads into the second tune. At bar 316, after the strings have prepared the entry, the solo flute plays its narrow melody again over held strings, now in G♯ minor. Plucked bass notes sound beneath the line, and the other winds join as the phrase continues.

    7. The flute returns in A♭

      At rehearsal 12 the moving string accompaniment changes to long held chords. A solo flute plays the closing theme in A♭ major, a semitone above its first appearance. The double basses rest, and the cellos hold the bass line under the theme.

    8. Coda returns to E minor

      The coda moves from the A♭ major of the flute song back to E minor. The main call returns in the brass above the full orchestra, and the timpani reinforce the approach to the final chords.

  2. II. LargoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    11:34

    In the Largo of Dvořák’s New World Symphony, a cor anglais plays the melody over muted strings. Wind chords surround it, and after a section in C♯ minor the string sections reduce to solo players at the quiet return.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening chords, wind and brass

      Clarinets, bassoons and brass begin with a chain of chords that moves from E major to D♭ major. The violins enter muted, and the wind choir drops out, leaving a single cor anglais.

    2. Cor anglais solo, bars 7–10

      The cor anglais solo in bars 7–10 uses four pitches, F, A♭, E♭ and D♭. Each phrase is in dotted rhythm, played over muted strings.

    3. Clarinet sketch and cor anglais

      In the sketches Dvořák gave the melody to a clarinet, in a more European shape. In the score it is played by the cor anglais, with a stronger pentatonic outline. The instrument’s tone is said to have reminded him of the voice of the singer Harry T. Burleigh.

    4. Sketchbook inscriptions

      Dvořák wrote two phrases into his sketches for this movement: “Legend” and “The Legend Begins”. Michael Beckerman heard the opening chords as a musical “once upon a time”. The sketchbooks contain earlier versions of these chords, one of which begins and ends in C major.

    5. William Arms Fisher, <i>Goin’ Home</i>, 1922

      In the summer of 1922 William Arms Fisher, a former pupil of the composer, played a piano arrangement of the Largo and began to write words to it. He made the cor anglais melody into the song Goin’ Home, nearly thirty years after the symphony.

    6. Letter to Oskar Nedbal, 1900

      In February 1900 Dvořák sent Oskar Nedbal, who was preparing to conduct the symphony in Berlin, a published analysis of it. He asked him to leave out “that nonsense about my using Indian and American motifs – it is a lie!” He had meant only to write in the spirit of American national melodies.

    7. Middle section in C♯ minor

      The key changes from D♭ major to C♯ minor, on the same sounding tonic. Flutes and oboes share a new tune over repeated string chords. Later, pizzicato bass notes give this middle section the tread of a funeral march.

    8. Indian melodies, New York Herald, 1893

      Dvořák told the New York Herald in December 1893 that he had “carefully studied a certain number of Indian melodies which a friend gave me”. Iroquois performers had sung and danced in Prague for ten days in August 1879. He saw Buffalo Bill’s Wild West show in spring 1894, after the symphony was finished.

    9. Public rehearsal, Carnegie Hall, 1893

      On 15 December 1893 people queued in heavy rain for the public rehearsal at Carnegie Hall, and every seat was sold. Anton Seidl conducted the New York Philharmonic. The official première followed the next evening.

    10. Oboe solo, C♯-major episode

      A quick figure for solo oboe divides the long phrases into short groups of notes. Flutes and clarinets join with trills and runs. The harmony moves to C♯ major, and the strings play a crescendo underneath.

    11. Climax with first-movement themes

      The dance builds to a full orchestral climax. The first movement’s main theme and closing theme return alongside the Largo theme, with the brass above the strings.

    12. Return of the cor anglais theme

      At Meno mosso, Tempo I the opening tempo returns. The cor anglais plays the theme again in D♭ major, accompanied by four players in each violin, viola and cello part.

    13. Strings reduced to soloists

      The string parts are reduced again, to two players each and then to solo violin, viola, cello and bass. Fermatas separate the phrases.

    14. Applause after the Largo, 1893

      At the première on 16 December 1893, the audience would not allow the concert to continue after the Largo until Dvořák appeared and bowed. His son Otakar, who was in the hall, recorded this in his memoirs.

    15. Return of the wind chords

      Bassoons, horns, trombones and tuba bring back the introductory chords, which move slowly to D♭ major once more. After the last wind chord, divided double basses descend and the movement ends.

    Joins

    1. Wind chords to cor anglais

      The wind chords move from E major to D♭. Their final G♭-minor chord resolves onto D♭ major, a minor-subdominant plagal cadence. Muted strings take over the held harmony, and the cor anglais begins its tune above them. The double basses hold D♭.

    2. D♭ major to C♯ minor

      The song ends, and D♭ is respelt as C♯. E♮ replaces F as the third above it, so the harmony turns to the minor. At Un poco più mosso (bar 46), flutes and oboes play the new tune together over repeated chords in the second violins and violas.

    3. Solo oboe introduces the quicker figure

      The strings, in the minor, play more softly and settle on a held C♯. A solo oboe then plays a quick figure above them, and flutes and clarinets follow with trills and running notes. The tonic remains C♯, now with the major third E♯, and the episode is a quasi-scherzo in C♯ major (Šourek full score, printed p. 84, rehearsal 4).

    4. Brass brings back first-movement themes

      The strings make a crescendo beneath the quick wind figures. Brass and timpani join, and the main and closing themes of the first movement sound against the Largo melody, so that three melodies are heard together.

    5. Four strings to a part

      The orchestra slows and softens into Meno mosso, Tempo I, the original tempo, at rehearsal 5. The cor anglais returns with the D♭-major melody over four players in each upper string part. The brass and timpani play their last quiet punctuation and then stop, leaving the melody to the cor anglais and strings.

    6. Return of the introductory wind chords

      The solo violin’s last phrase falls to ppp. At bar 120 bassoons, horns, trombones and tuba play the opening chord sequence again, which moves once more towards D♭ major. The bass changes with each chord, and the divided double basses play the final bars.

  3. III. Scherzo — Molto vivaceMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    8:26

    The third movement of Dvořák’s Symphony No. 9 in E minor “From the New World”, Op. 95, is a scherzo marked Molto vivace, in which the strings, timpani and triangle play accents off the beat. Two contrasting dance sections interrupt it, and themes from the first movement return in the coda.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening of the Scherzo

      The orchestra begins with sharp chords, timpani and triangle. Running strings follow, with accents displaced against the three-beat bar.

    2. Interview with the New York Herald, 1893

      Dvořák told the New York Herald in December 1893 that the scherzo came from “the scene of the feast where the Indians dance” in Longfellow’s The Song of Hiawatha. In that passage of the poem, a dancer whirls faster and faster and throws sand into the air.

    3. Poco sostenuto, flutes and oboes

      At bar 61, marked Poco sostenuto (a little sustained), flutes and oboes play a new tune in E major. Soft repeated string chords keep the three-beat pulse underneath. The bassoons play short phrases in reply.

    4. Descending string entries over B pedal

      The repeated-note figure passes from first violins to second violins, then to violas and cellos. Horns and timpani hold B, and each new entry is softer than the one before.

    5. Trio in C major

      The central trio, from bar 176, is a broader dance in C major. The woodwinds play the melody over a running accompaniment in the first and second violins. Timpani and triangle return with short, widely spaced entries.

    6. Kovařík’s copying, Spillville, 1893

      In Spillville in the summer of 1893, Josef Kovařík copied the new symphony at four pages a day. When he once produced sixteen, Dvořák scolded him: “Are you trying to dig yourself an early grave? Why do you do it? This is your holiday, man!”

    7. Trio, second dance

      The trio has a second dance, made of short skipping figures followed by trills. The violins play them first and the flutes, oboes and clarinets take them over. The bass continues in steps underneath.

    8. Return of the scherzo

      Soft string figures lead back to the scherzo in E minor. Its sharp accents and abrupt shifts of weight return, and so does the wind tune in E major set within it. The trio in C major is not repeated.

    9. Coda, first-movement themes

      In the coda the horns bring back the main theme of the first movement over repeated string chords. Later the theme first played by solo flute in that movement passes through winds and horns over running cellos and basses.

    Joins

    1. E minor to E major

      The E-minor dance gives way to E major at Poco sostenuto. Flutes and oboes play a melody, with the bassoon below. Second violins, violas and cellos repeat quiet chords and keep the three-beat bar steady beneath the tune.

    2. From Poco sostenuto back to scherzo

      The E-major wind tune of the Poco sostenuto ends, and the scherzo resumes in E minor. Short notes with forceful accents follow the sustained phrases. The strings take up their running figures again, and the winds reinforce the dance’s abrupt changes of direction.

    3. Repeated-note figure descends through the strings

      The wind chords fall away onto a held B in horns and timpani. A short repeated-note figure descends through the string sections, each entry quieter than the last. Clarinets and bassoons sustain the harmony as the bass instruments stop.

    4. Into the trio in C major

      Short chromatic phrases pass between winds and strings. The violins move into a flowing accompaniment, and a new wind tune establishes C major at the start of the trio (bar 176). Cellos, basses and timpani play a few light notes under the dance, and the triangle enters.

    5. Strings lead back to the scherzo

      The C-major trio closes with winds, horns and percussion. The violins then play quiet triplets that begin the transition, and the strings build in force before the E-minor scherzo returns. The timpani retune from C and G to E and B for the return.

    6. Return of the wind tune

      The music returns to E major for the Poco sostenuto tune. Flutes and oboes play the melody above repeated string chords, and the bassoons play between the phrases. The three-beat pulse continues under the longer melody. In the Šourek full score this passage is printed on pp. 101–103 and is heard again under the da capo instruction.

    7. Return of the E-minor scherzo

      The sustained wind melody ends and the clipped E-minor dance returns, with running strings and strong orchestral accents as at the start of the scherzo. In the Šourek full score the return comes under the da capo instruction. On printed p. 110 the direction “al Coda” sends the music from the end of this section into the coda and the earlier horn call.

    8. First-movement horn call in the coda

      The strings repeat a dark chord in long notes, stepping down from ff to pp. Above them the horns play the main call of the first movement. The coda stays in E minor, and the scherzo’s running figures give way to broad, sustained chords.

  4. IV. Allegro con fuocoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    11:43

    The finale of the New World Symphony opens with fast string figures and a theme in the brass. Before the end, melodies from all four movements return, and the final E-major chord fades in the winds after the strings have stopped.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening and main theme

      The strings begin alone and drive a short figure upwards. Winds and brass enter at the top of the climb. The brass then play the main theme, which has a broad opening phrase and a sharply accented reply.

    2. Second theme, solo clarinet

      A solo clarinet plays the second theme in G major. It holds long notes over quick figures in the cellos. The brass rests, and the flutes later join the clarinet.

    3. Completion of the score, 24 May 1893

      Dvořák finished the score at nine in the morning on 24 May 1893. That afternoon a telegram told him that his children had reached Southampton on their way to America, and he added the news beside the time of completion.

    4. Themes from earlier movements

      The development combines themes from the second, third and fourth movements. The slow movement’s melody and the scherzo’s quick figures sound against the finale’s main theme. Dvořák changes their pairings and accompaniment, so that melodies from different movements occupy the same bars.

    5. Recall of earlier movements

      In the finale of Beethoven’s Ninth, themes from the earlier movements are recalled at the start and then dropped. Dvořák brings them back in the development and adds more of them as the movement goes on. By the coda, every key idea of the symphony has returned.

    6. Public rehearsal, New York, 1893

      At the public rehearsal on 15 December 1893 the Herald asked musicians what the symphony meant for America. Seidl hoped it would lead younger composers towards “a truly national school”. Victor Herbert was asked whether it would shape the country’s music and replied: “Yes, if the composers are Dr. Dvořák.”

    7. Return of the main theme

      The finale’s main theme returns after the combination of earlier melodies. Its broad statement leads into a shortened recapitulation. Dvořák kept the longest treatment of material from the earlier movements for the expanded coda.

    8. First performances in Europe, 1894

      London heard the symphony first in Europe, on 21 June 1894. Czech audiences first heard it at Karlovy Vary on 20 July. In Prague, on 13 October, Dvořák conducted it himself at the National Theatre.

    9. Title page, November 1893

      In November 1893 Anton Seidl asked for the score so that he could prepare its première. Shortly before Kovařík took it to him, Dvořák added From the New World to the title page. Until that evening the title page had read Symphony in E minor.

    10. Coda, return of the Largo chords

      The coda brings back themes from the earlier movements alongside the finale’s own. The chords that opened the Largo return, scored for full orchestra. The brass hold them in long notes while the strings rise against them.

    11. Letter to Simrock, December 1893

      After the première Dvořák wrote to his publisher Simrock in Berlin that “the papers are saying that no composer has ever achieved a triumph such as this”. The applause was so long, he added, that he had to bow from his box “like a king (don’t laugh!)”.

    12. Clarinets over held bass, bar 310

      From bar 310 the orchestra makes a diminuendo after its large chords. The repeated string figures soften and the bass holds a long note. The clarinets enter above it with another quiet recollection before the final climb.

    13. Final chord in E major

      The closing pages move towards E major. In the final bar the strings, trombones and timpani play their last chord short, while the woodwind, horns and trumpets hold theirs. The held chord makes a diminuendo to ppp under a long fermata.

    Joins

    1. Brass to solo clarinet

      The brass stop and the strings play more softly. A solo clarinet enters with the second theme in G major, in long phrases above quick figures in the cellos. The upper strings hold long chords beneath the melody.

    2. From exposition into development

      The exposition’s closing figures diminish over a held bass. The first violins then play a rising phrase that begins the development. Above the moving strings, horns, trumpets and winds take up themes from the other movements.

    3. Return of the main tune

      Repeated wind chords and rising violin figures build towards the return of the main tune. Horns, bassoons and low strings give it weight below the high winds. The recapitulation is shorter than the exposition, and the largest build-up comes in the coda.

    4. Tempo I into the coda

      At Tempo I, the quieter return gives way to long orchestral chords and a forceful line in bassoons, cellos and basses. The coda brings back earlier themes, including the Largo’s opening chord sequence. Brass sustains the harmony while the strings climb repeatedly beneath it.

    5. Clarinets enter over the held bass

      The wind chords and repeated string figures diminish together. Cellos and basses stop their running notes and hold a quiet foundation. The clarinets begin a new phrase above it. The brass rests and the timpani roll fades.

    6. Into the final E major chord

      The quiet wind recollection leads into the final orchestral build. The strings rise, and brass and timpani play emphatically as the music moves to E major. At the last chord the orchestra divides. Strings, trombones and timpani stop short, and winds, horns and trumpets hold the chord and diminish to ppp.
