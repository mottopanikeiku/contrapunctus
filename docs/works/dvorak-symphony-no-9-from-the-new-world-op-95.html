Antonín Dvořák / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 9 “From the New World”, Op. 95

1893 · Op. 95 · B 178 · Orchestra, including cor anglais

Anton Seidl conducted the première in New York on 16 December 1893. The last movement gathers up tunes from all three earlier movements, including the Largo’s cor anglais melody.

4 recordings · 42 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Adagio — Allegro moltoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    10:05

    The New World Symphony begins quietly in the lower strings, then finds its voice in a horn call. That call will keep returning through all four movements, even when Dvořák seems to have wandered far from E minor.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The lower strings begin

      Violas and cellos start softly, with basses underneath. The violins wait while clarinets, bassoons and horns add short replies. These hesitant phrases already contain the shape of the horn call that will drive the Allegro.

    2. Drawn out, where possible

      Dvořák asks for the whole introduction to be “drawn out, where possible”. Its opening outline becomes a signature of his American years: it returns in the third movement of the E♭ major String Quintet and in the Humoresque No. 1.

    3. The horns announce it

      The horns launch the Allegro’s E-minor tune above quiet string chords and plucked basses. Its opening leaps like a fanfare; the answer falls back. Dvořák will bring this call into every later movement.

    4. A letter from New York

      On 12 April 1893 Dvořák writes from New York to Emil Kozánek about his new symphony: “It pleases me very much and will differ considerably from my early compositions.” He finishes the score on 24 May.

    5. Flutes and oboes answer

      The second tune begins in G minor, shared by flutes and oboes above a repeated horn note. It stays within a small compass and uses F♮, the lowered seventh. Later, the same tune opens out into a dance in major.

    6. Four pages a day

      In summer 1893, on his first afternoon in Spillville, Iowa, Dvořák gives his assistant Josef Kovařík the 128-page score to copy: four pages a day. Kovařík obeys, and Dvořák grumbles that they are “quite empty ones”. When he copies sixteen, Dvořák rages: “That’s dreadful work, I should know!”

    7. The flute’s third tune

      A solo flute sings the third tune in G major. Its opening has the rhythm of the horn call, now softened into a song. The strings hold their chords quietly, leaving the flute plenty of air.

    8. A tune close to a spiritual

      The flute’s tune is often compared with the spiritual Swing Low, Sweet Chariot: most scholars find its outline and rhythm too close to be chance. Dvořák told the New York Herald he had “not actually used any of the melodies”, only the spirit of Black and Native American songs.

    9. The tunes leave home

      The development takes up the horn call and the opening of the flute song. The keys turn quickly: A major becomes A minor, then F major and F♯ minor. Familiar phrases acquire sharper edges as their bass notes move.

    10. “The folk songs of America”

      Three days before he finishes the score, on 21 May 1893, the New York Herald prints Dvořák’s views under the headline “Real Value of Negro Melodies”. “These are the folk songs of America,” he says, “and your composers must turn to them.”

    11. E minor, then a surprise

      The opening call returns in E minor. The other tunes follow, but both have moved up a semitone: the second begins in G♯ minor, and the flute song returns in A♭ major. Homecoming has a detour built into it.

    12. Burleigh sings for Dvořák

      In New York, Harry T. Burleigh sings spirituals for Dvořák. They meet repeatedly before he begins the symphony. The songs’ five-note scales and displaced accents feed his thinking as he writes his own melodies.

    13. Mrs Thurber’s conservatory

      Jeannette Thurber founded New York’s National Conservatory in 1885 and admitted students whatever their race or sex. In 1892 she offered Dvořák its directorship. On 23 January 1894 he conducted its student orchestra and an all-Black choir of 130 at Madison Square Garden Concert Hall, for the Herald’s clothing fund.

    14. The brass claims E minor

      The coda brings the horn call up through the brass, with timpani driving underneath the full orchestra. After the flute song’s visit to A♭ major, the ending plants both feet in E minor.

    How it is joined

    1. The horns release the Allegro

      The Adagio’s last outburst subsides onto B, the dominant of E minor. The strings hold quietly as the metre changes to 2/4. At Allegro molto, horns announce the main tune above string chords and plucked cellos and basses.

    2. The winds settle into G minor

      The running string phrases quieten. Flutes and oboes begin the G-minor tune above sustained strings, with a solo horn repeating one note underneath. Its F♮ keeps the melody clear of the leading note F♯.

    3. A solo flute sings in G

      The preceding wind phrases settle and the moving strings soften into held chords. A solo flute introduces the closing tune in G major. Its dotted opening recalls the main call’s rhythm, while the brass and double basses remain silent.

    4. G major loosens its hold

      The G-major close leads into development beginning in A major. Dvořák takes the main call and the first phrase of the flute song through a sequence of new keys, turning A major to A minor before moving on to F major and F♯ minor.

    5. The call finds E minor

      The changing keys lead back to E minor and the main call. Its rising announcement and falling answer restore the opening pattern. The recapitulation then follows the earlier order of themes, with the later wind tunes shifted up a semitone.

    6. The wind tune rises a semitone

      The returning fanfare quietens into the second tune. A solo flute recovers its narrow melody over held strings, now in G♯ minor. Plucked bass notes leave plenty of space beneath the line; the other winds join as the phrase unfolds.

    7. The flute returns in A♭

      The busy string accompaniment settles into long chords. A solo flute sings the closing theme in A♭ major, a semitone above its first appearance. The double basses rest while cellos hold the foundation beneath the tune.

    8. E minor takes the ending

      The flute song’s A♭ major is left behind as the coda turns back to E minor. The main call grows into a brass statement above the full orchestra, with timpani reinforcing the drive to the final chords.

  2. II. LargoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    11:34

    A cor anglais sings over muted strings in the New World Symphony’s Largo. Dvořák surrounds that simple tune with wandering wind chords, a C♯-minor lament and a return so quiet that the string sections shrink to solo players.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Chords before the song

      Clarinets, bassoons and brass begin with a solemn chain of chords. E major is their starting point; D♭ major is where they land. The violins enter muted, and the wind choir leaves the stage to a single cor anglais.

    2. Four notes make a song

      The cor anglais begins with just four pitches: F, A♭, E♭ and D♭. Its dotted rhythm gently lifts each phrase above the muted strings. The tune has scarcely any notes, and all the time it needs.

    3. A clarinet tune becomes cor anglais

      In the sketches Dvořák gave the melody to a clarinet, in a more European shape. In the score it passes to the cor anglais, with a stronger pentatonic outline. The instrument’s tone is said to have reminded him of the singer Harry T. Burleigh’s voice.

    4. “The Legend Begins”

      Two phrases are written into Dvořák’s sketches for this movement: “Legend” and “The Legend Begins”. Michael Beckerman hears the opening chords as a musical “once upon a time”. The sketchbooks hold earlier versions of them, one beginning and ending in C major.

    5. Fisher writes “Goin’ Home”

      In the summer of 1922, Dvořák’s former pupil William Arms Fisher plays a piano arrangement of the Largo. Words begin coming to him. He turns the cor anglais melody into the song Goin’ Home, nearly thirty years after the symphony.

    6. “It is a lie!”

      In February 1900 Dvořák sends Oskar Nedbal, who is preparing to conduct the symphony in Berlin, a published analysis of it. He asks him to leave out “that nonsense about my using Indian and American motifs – it is a lie!” He had meant only to write in the spirit of American national melodies.

    7. The same note, in minor

      D♭ becomes C♯: the same sounding tonic, now in minor. Flutes and oboes share a more restless tune over repeated string chords. Later, plucked bass notes give this middle section the tread of a funeral march.

    8. Indian melodies from a friend

      Dvořák told the New York Herald in December 1893 that he had “carefully studied a certain number of Indian melodies which a friend gave me”. Iroquois performers sang and danced in Prague for ten days in August 1879. He saw Buffalo Bill’s Wild West show only in spring 1894, after the symphony was done.

    9. A queue in the rain

      On 15 December 1893, people queue in heavy rain for the public rehearsal at Carnegie Hall. Every seat sells. Anton Seidl conducts the New York Philharmonic; the official première follows the next evening.

    10. The oboe starts dancing

      A quick solo oboe figure breaks the long phrases into little groups of notes. Flutes and clarinets join it with trills and runs. C♯ major brightens the harmony, and the strings begin to swell beneath the dance.

    11. The whole symphony interrupts

      The little dance grows into an orchestral outburst. Themes from the first movement return beside the Largo’s own melody, with the brass projecting above the strings. The cor anglais song has acquired some formidable company.

    12. The cor anglais comes home

      Meno mosso, Tempo I: the first pace returns. The cor anglais sings again in D♭ major, over just four players in each violin, viola and cello part. The tune is surrounded by much less sound than before.

    13. Two players, then one

      The string parts shrink again: two players in each, then solo violin, viola, cello and bass. Pauses separate the phrases. A whole symphony orchestra has become a handful of people playing very softly.

    14. Applause after the Largo

      At the première on 16 December 1893, Dvořák’s son Otakar remembered in his memoirs, the audience would not let the concert go on after the Largo until his father appeared to bow. Otakar was in the hall.

    15. The wind chords return

      Bassoons, horns, trombones and tuba bring back the introductory chords. Their slow steps lead to D♭ major once more. After the last wind chord, divided double basses carry the music down into silence.

    How it is joined

    1. The winds leave one voice

      The wind chords travel from E major to D♭. Their final G♭-minor chord resolves onto D♭ major, a minor-subdominant plagal cadence. Muted strings take over the held harmony, and the cor anglais begins its tune above them. The double basses settle on D♭.

    2. D♭ turns into C♯ minor

      The song subsides, and D♭ is respelt C♯. E♮ replaces F as the third above it, turning the harmony to minor. At Un poco più mosso, flutes and oboes take the new tune together over repeated second-violin and viola chords.

    3. The oboe quickens the phrase

      The minor-key strings soften onto a held C♯. A solo oboe starts a quick figure above them; flutes and clarinets answer with trills and running notes. The tonic stays C♯, but the major third E♯ brightens this small dance.

    4. The brass brings back earlier tunes

      The strings swell beneath the quick wind figures. Brass and timpani join the growing orchestra, and themes from the first movement sound against the Largo tune. The short dance has expanded into a meeting of three melodies.

    5. Four strings to a part

      The orchestra slows and softens into Meno mosso, Tempo I. The cor anglais recovers the D♭-major song over four players in each upper string part. Brass and timpani make their last quiet punctuation, then leave the melody to the cor anglais and strings.

    6. The wind choir returns

      The solo violin’s last phrase dwindles to ppp. Bassoons, horns, trombones and tuba recover the opening chord sequence, moving once more towards D♭ major. The bass gives each chord its own footing; the final withdrawal belongs to divided double basses.

  3. III. Scherzo — Molto vivaceMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    8:26

    Dvořák’s scherzo springs off the beat, with timpani and triangle helping the strings kick up their heels. Two gentler dances interrupt it before the first movement’s tunes return in a surprisingly solemn coda.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Three beats, uneven feet

      The orchestra begins with sharp chords, timpani and triangle. Running strings and displaced accents soon make the three-beat bar feel unstable. The music keeps springing away from the place where a dance step ought to land.

    2. A dance from Longfellow

      Dvořák tells the New York Herald in December 1893 that the scherzo comes from “the scene of the feast where the Indians dance” in Longfellow’s The Song of Hiawatha. In the poem, Pau-Puk-Keewis whirls faster and faster, throwing sand into the air.

    3. The winds smooth the dance

      Flutes and oboes sing a gentler tune in E major. The marking is Poco sostenuto, a little sustained. Softly repeated string chords keep the three-beat pulse underneath, while the bassoons supply their own small answers.

    4. A figure travels downwards

      The repeated-note figure passes from first violins to second violins, then to violas and cellos. Horns and timpani hold B while each new entry speaks more softly. The dance seems to be descending a staircase.

    5. The C-major trio

      The central trio opens a broader dance in C major. Woodwinds carry the tune while the two violin sections weave a flowing accompaniment beneath it. Timpani and triangle return with small, well-spaced touches.

    6. Four pages a day

      In Spillville in summer 1893, Josef Kovařík copies the new symphony at four pages a day. When he produces sixteen, Dvořák scolds him: “Are you trying to dig yourself an early grave? Why do you do it? This is your holiday, man!”

    7. Little hops and trills

      The trio has a second kind of dance: short, skipping figures followed by trills. Violins begin the exchange, then flutes, oboes and clarinets answer. The bass keeps stepping underneath their quick turns.

    8. The scherzo starts again

      Quiet string figures prepare the return of the E-minor scherzo. Its sharp accents and sudden changes of weight come back, along with the gentler E-major wind tune enclosed inside it. The C-major trio has gone.

    9. Older tunes cross the dance

      The coda turns solemn. Horns bring back the first movement’s main call above repeated string chords. Later, the tune first sung by a solo flute in that movement rises through winds and horns, over running cellos and basses.

    How it is joined

    1. E minor softens into E major

      The E-minor dance gives way to E major at Poco sostenuto. Flutes and oboes stretch out a gentler melody, with bassoon replies below. Second violins, violas and cellos repeat quiet chords, keeping the three-beat bar steady while the tune relaxes.

    2. The clipped dance returns

      The E-major wind tune closes, and the scherzo resumes in E minor. Short notes and forceful accents replace the sustained phrases. The strings recover their running figures, with the winds reinforcing the dance’s abrupt changes of direction.

    3. The strings pass the rhythm down

      The wind chords subside onto a held B in horns and timpani. A small repeated-note figure descends through the string sections, each entry quieter than the last. Clarinets and bassoons sustain the harmony as the bass instruments fall silent.

    4. The trio settles in C

      Short chromatic phrases move between winds and strings. The violins settle into a flowing accompaniment, and a new wind tune establishes C major. Cellos, basses and timpani give the dance a few light footfalls; the triangle adds its quiet ring.

    5. Quiet strings reopen the scherzo

      The C-major trio closes with winds, horns and percussion. Quiet violin triplets begin the route back; the strings gather force before the E-minor scherzo returns. The timpani retune from C and G to E and B for the homecoming.

    6. The gentler wind tune returns

      E major returns with the Poco sostenuto tune. Flutes and oboes sing above the softly repeated string chords, and bassoons answer between phrases. The three-beat pulse continues underneath the longer melody.

    7. E minor cuts back in

      The sustained wind tune gives way again to the clipped E-minor dance. Running strings and strong orchestral accents recover the opening movement of the scherzo. This time, its close leads towards the coda and the older horn call.

    8. The horn call crosses the ending

      The strings repeat a dark chord in long notes, stepping down from ff to pp. Horns then sound the first movement’s main call above them. The coda remains rooted in E minor, but the scherzo’s running feet give way to these broad, weighted chords.

  4. IV. Allegro con fuocoMusopen recording; performers uncredited
    11:43

    The finale of the New World Symphony opens with rushing strings and a brass tune built to fill a hall. By the end, melodies from all four movements have returned, and the last E-major chord fades in the winds after the strings have stopped.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Strings before the brass

      The strings begin alone, driving a short figure upwards. Winds and brass enter as the climb reaches its summit. The main tune then strides out in the brass, with a broad opening and a sharply accented answer.

    2. The clarinet takes its time

      A solo clarinet introduces the second tune in G major. Its long notes float above the cellos’ quick, curling figures. The brass falls silent, and the flutes later join the clarinet’s song.

    3. A telegram after the ending

      On 24 May 1893, Dvořák finishes the score at nine in the morning. That afternoon a telegram tells him his children have reached Southampton on their way to America. He adds the news beside the time of completion.

    4. The other movements return

      The development draws on the whole symphony. The Largo’s song and the scherzo’s quick figures meet the finale’s main tune. Dvořák changes their companions and their accompaniment, letting melodies from different movements occupy the same bars.

    5. Memory saved for later

      Beethoven’s Ninth recalls the earlier movements at the start of its finale and then drops them. Dvořák waits for the development to bring them back, and keeps adding to them until the coda has gathered every key idea of the symphony.

    6. “If the composers are Dr. Dvořák”

      At the public rehearsal on 15 December 1893 the Herald asks musicians what the symphony means for America. Seidl hopes it will lead younger composers towards “a truly national school”. Victor Herbert, asked whether it will shape the country’s music, answers: “Yes, if the composers are Dr. Dvořák.”

    7. The main tune comes back

      The finale’s main tune returns after the tangle of earlier melodies. Its broad statement now leads into a shortened recapitulation. Dvořák saves the longest reckoning with the symphony’s past for the coda.

    8. London, Karlovy Vary, Prague

      London hears the symphony first in Europe, on 21 June 1894. Czech audiences first hear it at Karlovy Vary on 20 July. In Prague, on 13 October, Dvořák conducts it himself at the National Theatre.

    9. A title added in November

      In November 1893, Anton Seidl asks for the score to prepare its première. Just before Kovařík carries it away, Dvořák adds From the New World to the title page. Until that evening, it has simply said Symphony in E minor.

    10. The Largo’s chords grow immense

      The coda gathers the symphony’s old tunes around the finale’s own. Even the quiet chords that opened the Largo return, enlarged for the full orchestra. Rising strings press against their long brass notes.

    11. “A triumph such as this”

      After the première Dvořák writes to his publisher Simrock in Berlin that “the papers are saying that no composer has ever achieved a triumph such as this”. The applause was so long, he adds, that he had to bow from his box “like a king (don’t laugh!)”.

    12. Clarinets above the quiet bass

      The orchestra draws back from its huge chords. Repeated string figures soften, and the bass settles into a long note. Clarinets emerge into the space, beginning another quiet recollection before the final climb.

    13. The winds keep the ending

      The closing pages drive towards E major. At the very end, strings, trombones and timpani cut their last chord short, but the other winds and brass hold on. Their sound diminishes to ppp under a long fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The brass yields to a clarinet

      The brass withdraws and the strings soften. A solo clarinet opens the G-major second tune, stretching its phrases above the cellos’ quick figures. The upper strings hold long chords while the melody finds room to breathe.

    2. The strings reopen the argument

      The exposition’s closing figures diminish over a held bass. The first violins turn upwards from this quiet ending, setting the development in motion. Horns, trumpets and winds take up memories of the other movements above the moving strings.

    3. The main tune gathers the orchestra

      Repeated wind chords and rising violin figures build towards the main tune’s return. Horns, bassoons and low strings give it weight below the high winds. The returning music follows a shorter course than the exposition, leaving the great accumulation for the coda.

    4. The orchestra enlarges the Largo chords

      At Tempo I, the quieter return gives way to long orchestral chords and a forceful line in bassoons, cellos and basses. The coda gathers earlier themes, including the Largo’s opening chord sequence. Brass sustains the harmony while the strings repeatedly climb beneath it.

    5. The clarinets emerge from the chords

      The wind chords and repeated string figures diminish together. Cellos and basses stop their running notes and hold a quiet foundation. Clarinets begin a new phrase above it, while the brass rests and the timpani roll fades.

    6. E major fills the closing pages

      The quiet wind recollection grows into the final orchestral drive. Rising strings, emphatic brass and timpani push towards E major. The last chord separates the orchestra: strings, trombones and timpani stop short; winds, horns and trumpets sustain and diminish to ppp.
