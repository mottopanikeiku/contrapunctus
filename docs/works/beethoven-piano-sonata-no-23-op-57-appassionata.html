Ludwig van Beethoven / II. Classical

Piano Sonata No. 23, Op. 57 “Appassionata”

1804–06 · Piano

3 recordings · 22 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro assaiArtur Schnabel, piano · 11 April 1933
    8:59

    The Appassionata begins almost inaudibly, with both hands tracing a bare F-minor chord. Artur Schnabel plays a movement in which the gentlest tune can turn savage within a few bars.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A bare broken chord

      The hands move together in octaves, quietly tracing F minor. Then the same phrase shifts up a semitone. A small repeated-note figure answers it: three short notes and a long one, knocking from low on the keyboard.

    2. A publisher names the sonata

      Hamburg, 1838: Cranz publishes a four-hand arrangement as Sonata appassionata. Beethoven has been dead for eleven years. The title sticks, although he never writes appassionato anywhere in the sonata.

    3. The gentle tune darkens

      A new melody rises through A♭ major, marked dolce — sweetly. It shares the opening’s broken-chord shape, now with an accompaniment. Within the same passage A♭ minor takes over, and the hands drive towards a much fiercer ending.

    4. E major after A♭ minor

      The exposition sinks into A♭ minor. The bare opening phrase starts again in E major, a startlingly distant place. Beethoven carries it through new keys, then works on fragments of the gentler tune.

    5. The bass will not leave

      The opening returns in F minor, but a repeated C keeps sounding beneath it. The first phrase once stood alone. Now the bass follows it like a second thought that will not be silenced.

    6. Schnabel leaves Berlin

      This is Schnabel’s 1933 recording. That year, with the Nazis in power, he leaves Germany with his family and settles in Italy. He has made Berlin his home for more than thirty years.

    7. F major, briefly

      The dolce melody comes home in F major. For a while the sonata seems able to end in sunlight. Then F minor returns, repeating the major-to-minor change that overtook the tune in the exposition.

    8. Another turn through D♭

      The coda brings back the opening’s broken chord and takes the second tune into D♭ major. Long runs cross the keyboard. Eventually Beethoven slows them with ritardando, then Adagio, holding the pianist at a fermata before the final rush.

    9. A storm that whispers out

      Più allegro: faster. The second tune returns in F minor above the left hand’s repeated chords. The final rush spends itself, and the music sinks into a quiet F-minor ending.

    How it is joined

    1. The broken chord begins to sing

      The fast triplet figures settle on E♭, the dominant of A♭ major, and diminish. The right hand begins a connected melody, marked dolce, above a separate accompaniment. Its rising broken chord recalls the shape of the opening.

    2. The opening finds E major

      The exposition closes quietly in A♭ minor. A short link leads to the opening broken chord in E major, with the hands once again exposed in octaves. There is no exposition repeat: this familiar phrase starts the development.

    3. C stays beneath the returning tune

      The development lingers on the dominant of F minor, with diminished harmony and the repeated-note figure pressing against C. The opening phrase returns, but the C pedal carries on underneath. The pianist must fit the familiar octaves around that persistent bass.

    4. The sweet tune comes home

      The bridge now prepares C, the dominant of F. Its quick figures subside, leaving the dolce melody in F major over a gently moving accompaniment. The major third will soon flatten as the continuation turns to F minor.

    5. The quiet bass starts again

      The F-minor closing figures diminish, but their running notes continue into the coda. The opening broken chord returns low on the keyboard; the second tune then appears in D♭ major. Beethoven begins another stretch of developing these two phrases.

    6. A held C releases the rush

      The runs break into short chords as Beethoven writes ritardando, then Adagio. A fermata holds the dominant harmony over C. Più allegro begins with forceful chords and sends the second tune back into F minor above the left hand’s repeated accompaniment.

  2. II. Andante con motoArtur Schnabel, piano · 11 April 1933
    6:00

    Artur Schnabel plays the Appassionata’s quiet centre: a D♭-major tune whose plain chords blossom into ever quicker notes. At the end, one diminished seventh opens the door to the finale.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A tune close to earth

      Both hands begin low on the keyboard. D♭-major chords move in a measured two-beat tread, with small dotted figures at the ends of phrases. Each eight-bar half comes round twice.

    2. Never called Appassionata

      Beethoven dedicates the sonata to his friend Count Franz von Brunsvik. He never gives it the name Appassionata: that first appears in 1838, on a four-hand arrangement published by Cranz of Hamburg.

    3. The hands part company

      The first variation pulls the hands apart. The upper chord sounds on the beat; the bass answers afterwards and hangs across the next beat. The same tune has acquired a gentle, persistent tug.

    4. Marie Bigot’s wet manuscript

      In Paul Bigot’s account, Beethoven arrives at their Vienna home carrying the sonata’s rain-soaked manuscript. Marie Bigot sits down and plays through its erasures and corrections. Beethoven promises her the manuscript and brings it back after the engraver has finished with it.

    5. Chords become a stream

      The right hand unfolds the tune into semiquavers. Underneath, the left hand keeps the slower steps of the D♭-major theme. The pianist now plays four notes in the space once occupied by one.

    6. Eight notes to the beat

      Demisemiquavers pour through the left hand while the tune hangs above them in longer notes. Later the hands exchange jobs. The quick notes climb high on the keyboard, then tumble back towards the opening chords.

    7. All thirty-two, twice

      In January and February 1927 Schnabel plays all thirty-two Beethoven sonatas at Berlin’s Volksbühne, which no other pianist had done. He gives the cycle again at the Philharmonie from January to April 1933; the Nazis take power that year and he leaves Germany.

    8. The theme comes home

      The plain chords return in D♭ major. Beethoven leaves out the repeats and lets phrases answer one another across the keyboard: low chords, then a reply an octave higher.

    9. The wrong last chord

      The expected D♭-major chord never comes. E–G–B♭–D♭ sounds instead: a diminished seventh, rolled and held under a fermata. Beethoven repeats it an octave higher. The finale begins on that same chord.

    How it is joined

    1. The hands strike separately

      The theme closes on D♭. The first variation begins with the same upper chords, but the left hand enters a quaver after the right and ties its notes across the beat. Both hands keep the tune’s harmonic steps while their attacks pull apart.

    2. The right hand begins to flow

      The first variation reaches D♭ major and leaves a brief rest. The right hand begins again high on the keyboard, breaking the chords into semiquavers. The left hand returns to the theme’s slower bass, beginning on D♭.

    3. The quick notes change hands

      The second variation closes in D♭ major. A demisemiquaver pickup starts in the left hand, and the right hand stretches the melody into longer, syncopated notes. The smallest written note-value has halved again; the theme’s bass and cadences remain in place.

    4. The run lands on the theme

      The demisemiquavers descend through the keyboard. At the bottom, the run meets the D♭ bass of the returning theme. Plain right-hand chords restore the opening’s measured tread, and the melody begins its last statement without repeats.

    5. D♭ becomes a dissonance

      The last cadence turns away from D♭ major into E–G–B♭–D♭, with B♭ at the bottom. The pianist rolls the chord and holds it under a fermata, then repeats it an octave higher. The first edition’s attacca sends this unresolved diminished seventh directly into the finale.

  3. III. Allegro ma non troppo — PrestoArtur Schnabel, piano · 11 April 1933
    7:11

    The Appassionata’s finale runs on semiquavers, with barely a place for the pianist to breathe. Artur Schnabel takes Beethoven’s long repeat through the development and return before the final, faster Presto.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Thirteen blows on one chord

      The diminished seventh left hanging at the end of the Andante comes back thirteen times. Then the right hand breaks free in semiquavers, sweeping down the keyboard towards F minor.

    2. The running tune

      The main tune starts pianissimo over a low F. It rises through the minor chord and runs down again, eight semiquavers to a bar. The left hand soon takes over the running while the right sings above it.

    3. C minor keeps running

      The second tune arrives in C minor with the semiquavers still racing. Little rests break the right hand’s line; the left hand strikes chords beneath it. Even the contrasting tune has trouble sitting still.

    4. A low B♭

      The development begins quietly over a low B♭. The opening tune takes on the flats of B♭ minor and starts moving through other keys. A new, chordal phrase joins it further on.

    5. A theme on the way to Döbling

      On a walk that ended at Döbling at eight in the evening, Beethoven hummed and howled up and down the whole way. “A theme for the last Allegro of the sonata has come to me,” he told his pupil Ries. Indoors, hat still on, he raged at the piano for at least an hour.

    6. F under the fingers

      After the bass has lingered on C, a low F brings back the opening tune. It returns pianissimo, still running. The second tune will return in F minor too.

    7. Thirty-two sonatas in Berlin

      In January and February 1927, Schnabel plays all thirty-two Beethoven sonatas at Berlin’s Volksbühne. He presents the complete cycle again at the Philharmonie in 1933, the year of this recording.

    8. The long repeat

      Back to the low B♭. Beethoven repeats the whole development and recapitulation, sending the pianist through the changing keys and the F-minor return a second time. The exposition has no repeat.

    9. A name from 1838

      The first edition, dedicated to Count Franz von Brunsvik, came out in Vienna in 1807 from the Bureau des Arts et d’Industrie. “Appassionata” is not in it. The nickname first appears in 1838, on a four-hand arrangement published by Cranz of Hamburg.

    10. A new dance, faster

      Presto. A short chordal tune arrives in F minor, with a springing bass and clipped replies. Beethoven repeats its two halves. There is still room for a new dance before the semiquavers rush back.

    11. Down to F minor

      The opening tune returns at the faster speed. Its semiquavers drive downwards until the hands land on the final F-minor chords. The sonata ends in the minor key of its first whispered phrase.

    How it is joined

    1. A low F catches the run

      The opening diminished seventh breaks into a descending run. Repeated figures settle over C, the dominant of F minor, and diminish. At the main tune, marked pianissimo, the bass drops to F while the right hand rises through the minor chord.

    2. The bass turns towards B♭

      The C-minor close runs into a descending link. Its final figures turn around A♮, the leading note of B♭, and diminish. The development opens pianissimo on a low B♭, with the first tune recast in B♭ minor above it. A repeat sign marks the beginning of this passage.

    3. The bass returns to F

      The development ends over a six-bar pedal on C. The last dominant chord leaves a rest before the first tune returns pianissimo over low F. The hands resume their exchanges, and the later second tune comes back in F minor.

    4. The first ending turns back

      The first ending sends a run down through both hands. The bass circles A♮ again as Beethoven writes diminuendo. The repeat returns to the low B♭ and pianissimo opening of the development, with the entire recapitulation still to come again.

    5. C resolves to F again

      The repeated development reaches the same six-bar C pedal. Once again, low F releases the pianissimo main tune. At the end of this return, the pianist will take the second ending and continue into the coda.

    6. The second ending finds a dance

      The second ending continues in F minor and accelerates: sempre più allegro. At Presto, a held upper chord sounds over low F before short chordal phrases begin. The left hand supplies a springing accompaniment, and each half of the new tune is repeated.
