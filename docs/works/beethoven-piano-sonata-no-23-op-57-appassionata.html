Ludwig van Beethoven / II. Classical

Piano Sonata No. 23, Op. 57 “Appassionata”

1804–06 · Piano

3 recordings · 22 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro assaiArtur Schnabel, piano · 11 April 1933
    8:59

    Artur Schnabel plays the first movement, Allegro assai, of the Piano Sonata No. 23, Op. 57, the Appassionata. It begins very softly, with both hands tracing a bare F-minor chord, and within a few bars its quietest theme gives way to violent writing.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening in octaves, F minor

      The hands play together in octaves, quietly outlining F minor. The same phrase is then repeated a semitone higher. A short repeated-note figure follows, low on the keyboard, made of three short notes and a long one.

    2. Cranz edition, Hamburg, 1838

      In 1838, eleven years after Beethoven’s death, the Hamburg publisher Cranz issued a four-hand arrangement under the title Sonata appassionata. The name stuck. Beethoven never wrote appassionato anywhere in the sonata.

    3. Second subject in A♭ major

      A new melody rises through A♭ major, marked dolce (sweetly), in bars 36–62. It has the broken-chord shape of the opening, now with an accompaniment. A♭ minor takes over within the same passage, and the hands drive towards a much fiercer ending.

    4. Development, E major restatement

      The exposition ends in A♭ minor. The development opens with the bare opening phrase restated in E major, a distant key. Beethoven takes it through further keys, then develops fragments of the second theme.

    5. Recapitulation over dominant pedal

      The opening theme returns in F minor at bar 136, over a repeated C in the bass. This dominant pedal moves to D♭ and then back to C.

    6. Schnabel leaves Berlin, 1933

      This recording by Schnabel dates from 1933. That year, with the Nazis in power, he left Germany with his family and settled in Italy. He had made Berlin his home for more than thirty years.

    7. Second subject in F major

      The dolce melody returns in F major. F minor then follows, repeating the change from major to minor that the tune underwent in the exposition.

    8. Coda, second subject in D♭ major

      The coda brings back the broken chord of the opening and takes the second theme into D♭ major. Long runs cross the keyboard. Beethoven then marks ritardando and Adagio, with a fermata before the final fast passage.

    9. Più allegro and coda in F minor

      Più allegro: faster. The second subject returns in F minor above repeated chords in the left hand. The movement ends quietly in F minor.

    Joins

    1. Triplets give way to a dolce melody

      The fast triplet figures settle on E♭, the dominant of A♭ major, and diminish. The right hand then plays a connected melody, marked dolce, above a separate accompaniment. Its rising broken chord recalls the shape of the opening.

    2. From A♭ minor to E major

      The exposition closes in A♭ minor. A short link leads to the opening broken chord, now in E major, with the hands again in octaves. The exposition is not repeated, and this phrase begins the development (bars 62–70).

    3. C pedal beneath the returning opening

      The development stays on the dominant of F minor in bars 124–140, with diminished harmony and the repeated-note figure sounding against C. The opening phrase returns above the C pedal, which continues underneath it. The pianist plays the octaves of the opening around that sustained bass.

    4. Second subject returns in F major

      In the recapitulation (bars 152–201 in the first edition) the bridge is rewritten to prepare C, the dominant of F. Its quick figures stop, and the dolce melody enters in F major over a moving accompaniment. In the continuation the major third is flattened and the music turns to F minor.

    5. The bass starts the coda again

      The F-minor closing figures diminish, but their running notes continue into the coda, which begins at bar 201. The opening broken chord returns low on the keyboard, and the coda is extended at bar 205. At bar 211 the second tune appears in D♭ major, and both phrases are developed again.

    6. Fermata on C and the Più allegro

      The runs break into short chords, marked ritardando, then Adagio. A fermata holds the dominant harmony over C. The Più allegro opens with chords, and the second theme returns in F minor above a repeated accompaniment in the left hand.

  2. II. Andante con motoArtur Schnabel, piano · 11 April 1933
    6:00

    Artur Schnabel plays the Andante con moto from the Appassionata Sonata, Op. 57, a theme in D♭ major set in plain chords and then varied in faster note values. At the end a diminished seventh chord leads straight into the finale.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, bars 1–16

      Both hands begin low on the keyboard. D♭-major chords move in a measured two-beat tread, with small dotted figures at the ends of phrases. Each eight-bar half is repeated.

    2. Dedication and nickname

      Beethoven dedicated the sonata to his friend Count Franz von Brunsvik. He never called it Appassionata. The name first appeared in 1838, on a four-hand arrangement published by Cranz of Hamburg.

    3. First variation, syncopated bass

      In the first variation (bars 17–35) the hands are separated. The upper chord sounds on the beat, and the bass enters after it and is tied across the next beat.

    4. Marie Bigot and the autograph

      According to Paul Bigot, Beethoven arrived at the Bigots’ home in Vienna with the manuscript of the sonata soaked by rain. Marie Bigot played through it, erasures and corrections included. Beethoven promised her the manuscript and brought it back once the engraver had finished with it.

    5. Semiquaver arpeggios, bars 36–53

      The right hand breaks the tune into semiquaver arpeggios. The left hand keeps the slower steps of the D♭-major theme underneath, and the pianist now plays four notes where there was once one.

    6. Demisemiquaver variation, bars 54–85

      Demisemiquavers run through the left hand below the melody, which moves in longer notes. Later the hands exchange roles, and the demisemiquavers rise high on the keyboard above the melody. The variation ends with a descending run back towards the opening chords.

    7. Beethoven sonata cycles, 1927 and 1933

      In January and February 1927 Schnabel played all thirty-two Beethoven sonatas at Berlin’s Volksbühne, which no other pianist had done. He gave the cycle again at the Philharmonie from January to April 1933. The Nazis took power that year, and he left Germany.

    8. Return of the theme in D♭ major

      The plain chords return in D♭ major, bars 86–100. Beethoven leaves out the repeats, and the phrases move between registers of the keyboard. Low chords are followed by the same material an octave higher.

    9. Diminished seventh, bars 101–102

      In place of a D♭-major chord, the movement ends on E–G–B♭–D♭, a diminished seventh, rolled and held under a fermata. Beethoven repeats it an octave higher. The finale begins on the same chord.

    Joins

    1. Left hand offset in the first variation

      The theme closes on D♭. The first variation begins with the same upper chords. The left hand enters a quaver after the right and ties its notes across the beat. Both hands keep the harmonic steps of the theme, with their attacks offset by a quaver.

    2. From first to second variation

      The first variation closes in D♭ major, followed by a brief rest. The right hand starts again high on the keyboard and breaks the chords into semiquavers. The left hand goes back to the slower bass of the theme, starting on D♭.

    3. Demisemiquavers move to the left hand

      The second variation closes in D♭ major. At bars 53–56 the left hand begins with a demisemiquaver pickup, and the right hand plays the melody in longer, syncopated notes. The smallest written note-value has halved again. The bass and cadences of the theme stay as they were.

    4. Descending run into the theme’s return

      The demisemiquavers descend through the keyboard. At the bottom the run reaches the D♭ bass of the returning theme. The right hand returns to plain chords at the pace of the opening, and the melody begins its last statement without repeats.

    5. Diminished seventh leads into the finale

      The last cadence moves away from D♭ major to E–G–B♭–D♭, with B♭ at the bottom. The pianist rolls the chord and holds it under a fermata, then repeats it an octave higher. The diminished seventh is left unresolved, and the first edition marks attacca, so the finale follows directly.

  3. III. Allegro ma non troppo — PrestoArtur Schnabel, piano · 11 April 1933
    7:11

    The finale of Beethoven’s Appassionata Sonata, Op. 57, moves in almost continuous semiquavers. Artur Schnabel plays Beethoven’s long repeat of the development and recapitulation before the faster closing Presto.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening diminished seventh chords

      The diminished seventh left unresolved at the end of the Andante returns, struck thirteen times. The right hand then begins a semiquaver figure that descends the keyboard towards F minor.

    2. First subject in F minor

      The main theme starts pianissimo over a low F. It rises through the minor chord and runs down again, eight semiquavers to a bar. The left hand soon takes over the semiquavers, and the right hand plays a melody above them.

    3. Second subject in C minor

      The second subject enters in C minor at bar 76, over continuing semiquavers. Rests break the right-hand phrases, and the left hand plays chords beneath them.

    4. Development, bars 118–158

      The development begins over a low B♭. The opening theme appears in B♭ minor and then modulates. A new chordal phrase enters at bar 142.

    5. Walk to Döbling, Ries’s account

      On a walk to Döbling, ending at eight in the evening, Beethoven hummed and howled the whole way. “A theme for the last Allegro of the sonata has come to me,” he told his pupil Ries. Indoors, hat still on, he raged at the piano for at least an hour.

    6. Return of the first subject

      After a dominant pedal on C in the bass, a low F brings back the opening tune, pianissimo and still running. The second subject also returns in F minor.

    7. Schnabel’s Beethoven cycles, Berlin, 1927 and 1933

      In January and February 1927, Schnabel played all thirty-two Beethoven sonatas at Berlin’s Volksbühne. He gave the complete cycle again at the Philharmonie in 1933, the year of this recording.

    8. Repeat of development and recapitulation

      The music returns to the low B♭. Beethoven repeats the whole development and recapitulation, so the changing keys and the return to F minor are played a second time. The exposition has no repeat.

    9. First edition and nickname

      The first edition, dedicated to Count Franz von Brunsvik, came out in Vienna in 1807 from the Bureau des Arts et d’Industrie. It does not use the name “Appassionata”. The nickname first appeared in 1838, on a four-hand arrangement published by Cranz of Hamburg.

    10. Presto, new theme in F minor

      Presto. A short chordal theme enters in F minor over a leaping bass, with clipped replies. Beethoven repeats each of its two halves before the semiquavers return.

    11. Closing bars in F minor

      The opening theme returns at the faster tempo. Its semiquavers move downwards to the final chords in F minor, the key of the sonata’s first phrase.

    Joins

    1. From opening run to main theme

      The opening diminished seventh breaks into a descending run. Repeated figures continue over C, the dominant of F minor, and diminish. At the main tune, marked pianissimo, the bass drops to F and the right hand rises through the minor chord.

    2. The bass turns towards B♭

      The C-minor close runs into a descending link. Its final figures turn around A♮, the leading note of B♭, and diminish. The development opens pianissimo on a low B♭, with the first tune recast in B♭ minor above it. A repeat sign marks the beginning of this passage.

    3. The bass returns to F

      The development ends over a six-bar pedal on C. A rest follows the last dominant chord, and the first theme returns pianissimo over low F. The hands resume their exchanges. Later the second theme returns in F minor (bars 268–288).

    4. First ending and return to development

      The first ending (bars 300–307) sends a run down through both hands. The bass circles A♮ again under a diminuendo. The repeat goes back to bar 118, the low B♭ and pianissimo opening of the development, and the whole recapitulation is then played a second time.

    5. C pedal resolves to F again

      The development is repeated, and bars 206–228 are played a second time. The same six-bar pedal on C returns, and low F again brings back the main theme pianissimo. At the end of this return the pianist takes the second ending at bar 308 and continues into the coda.

    6. Second ending into the Presto

      The second ending stays in F minor and gets faster, marked sempre più allegro. At Presto an upper chord is held over low F, and then short chordal phrases begin. The left hand plays a springing accompaniment, and each half of the new tune is repeated.
