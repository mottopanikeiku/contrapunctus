Maurice Ravel / V. The French Line

Pavane pour une infante défunte (Pavane for a Dead Princess)

1899; orch. 1909 · Piano; later orchestra

An imagined Spanish court, with a G major melody that returns between episodes. Ravel gave the opening of the orchestral version to a solo horn.

5 min

Recording

  1. Pavane pour une infante défunteThérèse Dussaut, piano · 1976
    5:08

    Thérèse Dussaut plays the piano original of Ravel’s Pavane, a stately dance imagined for a princess at the old Spanish court. The melody returns several times, each time with more movement beneath its long notes.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening melody on G

      The melody holds its first note, G, while the accompaniment plays detached quavers beneath it. Ravel marked the passage “Assez doux, mais d’une sonorité large”.

    2. Commission and dedication

      The Princesse de Polignac commissioned the piece and received its dedication. Fauré, Ravel’s teacher, had introduced him to her Paris salon. Ricardo Viñes gave the public première on 5 April 1902 at the Société nationale de musique.

    3. Très lointain, over low B octaves

      Très lointain — very distant. Low B octaves are held beneath a melody in closely spaced chords. The walking bass of the opening has stopped, and the chords move together above the held notes.

    4. The title and the Spanish court

      Ravel described the piece as a pavane that a young princess might have danced at the Spanish court centuries earlier. He orchestrated it in 1909, ten years after the piano original.

    5. Sudden soft legato passage

      A broad fortissimo chord gives way to a line marked subitement très doux et très lié. B♭ colours the melody, and short connecting notes fill its gaps. Later the piano plays forcefully again.

    6. Last refrain, new accompaniment

      The melody returns above a new accompaniment in flowing semiquavers. Ravel marks it marquez le chant, so the tune stands clear of the moving notes. Its long phrases stay recognisable while the accompaniment grows busier around them.

    7. Final chord

      The ending broadens greatly. The last G-major chord is marked fortissimo and held under a fermata.

    Joins

    1. Walking bass stops on low B

      The refrain broadens, then the tempo is restored and the next phrase is marked Très lointain. Low B octaves hold beneath a B-minor melody in chords. The piano’s walking bass stops, and the upper voices move together above it.

    2. Fermata before the G-major return

      Chromatic chords move to a close marked un peu plus lent, and a fermata holds the last chord. At Reprenez le mouvement the original tempo resumes and the G-major refrain returns, now accompanied by fuller chords in the bass.

    3. Fortissimo chord to a soft, legato line

      The G-major refrain reaches a chord marked Large and fortissimo. A line marked subitement très doux et très lié follows. B♭ enters the melody, and the bass leaves more space beneath the upper notes.

    4. A 2/4 bar before the refrain

      The piano descends through chromatic chords. Ravel writes Très grave, shortens the bar to 2/4 and puts a fermata on the last chord. The G-major refrain then resumes in four beats, with its melody above a new semiquaver accompaniment.
