Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Symphony No. 5

1901–02 · Orchestra

Opens with a solo trumpet’s funeral fanfare in C♯ minor and ends in D major with a contrapuntal rondo. The fourth movement, the Adagietto for strings and harp, is his best-known music.

5 recordings · 1 h 11 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Trauermarsch — In gemessenem Schritt. Streng. Wie ein KonduktPeabody Symphony Orchestra
    12:44

    A trumpet stands alone at the head of Mahler’s Fifth, calling a funeral procession into being. Twice the march breaks open; at the end, a flute is left to answer the trumpet.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A trumpet alone

      A solo trumpet calls into an empty hall: repeated-note triplets, then a dotted rhythm reaching higher. Mahler marks the march strict and measured, like a funeral procession. When the orchestra answers, the call is still there, hammered out by many players.

    2. Violins beside cellos

      The first violins play low; the cellos play high. Their voices almost meet in the funeral tune, with a darker sound than violins alone. Plucked strings mark the steps beneath it. The procession has begun to sing.

    3. A house by the lake

      In June 1901 Mahler moves into his new villa at Maiernigg, on the Wörthersee. He writes the Fifth over this summer and the next. During the rest of the year, the Vienna Court Opera has first claim on his working day.

    4. The march breaks open

      B♭ minor, and the strings race beneath a long trumpet cry. Wide upward leaps fall back by a semitone. The bass keeps dropping through fourths, the funeral tread now moving at twice its old speed.

    5. The signal comes back

      The trumpet’s opening rhythm brings C♯ minor back with it. Shivering string tremolos surround the call. When the funeral tune resumes in the woodwinds, Mahler writes Schwer — heavy — above its quiet tread.

    6. Violins inherit the cry

      The earlier trumpet melody returns as a slower song for violins. Its great upward leaps now end in sighs. Underneath, rhythms from the funeral march accompany the tune that once tore the march apart.

    7. The procession recedes

      The trumpet call survives the climax in broken pieces. Brass and strings withdraw around it; towards the end, a flute carries the triplets above a distant bass-drum roll. A sharp plucked note from the low strings puts the last full stop on the page.

    How it is joined

    1. The trumpet call is cut short

      A quiet trumpet begins the familiar triplets. Before it can finish, the strings launch a faster B♭-minor passage. The bass drops through repeated fourths while the first trumpet stretches into a long melody above racing strings.

    2. C♯ minor and the old signal

      The trumpet fanfare cuts back into the faster music. C♯ minor returns with chromatically shifting string tremolos, then the introductory chords and triplets. The woodwinds resume the funeral tune over its clipped accompaniment, marked Schwer.

    3. Timpani leave room for violins

      The opening triplets subside in the timpani. Violins take the first episode’s trumpet theme at a slower pace, its rising leaps followed by falling seconds. The clipped march rhythms continue underneath, joining the two kinds of music.

    4. The fanfare outlasts the climax

      The strings descend from the climax in shivering tremolos. Trombones climb chromatically in slow triplets, and the trumpet fanfare reappears. Cellos and basses take its falling dotted figure as the procession slows towards its final C♯-minor calls.

  2. II. Stürmisch bewegt, mit größter VehemenzPeabody Symphony Orchestra
    13:58

    Mahler follows the funeral march with an A-minor storm, torn open by a cello lament. Near the end, the brass find a magnificent D-major chorale — and lose it again.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Five notes strike upwards

      Low strings fling up a five-note figure. The orchestra answers with slashing diminished-seventh chords, and the strings try again. Mahler asks for the greatest possible vehemence. This is the funeral march’s anger let loose.

    2. Cellos remember the march

      The rapid woodwind notes run out, leaving a timpani roll. In F minor, the cellos begin a lament. Mahler explicitly restores the tempo of the first movement’s funeral march; its tune has followed us into the storm.

    3. Two summers at Maiernigg

      Mahler begins the Fifth in the summer of 1901. By the time he returns to Maiernigg the next summer, he has met and married Alma Schindler, and they are expecting their first child. The symphony takes shape across those two summers.

    4. Twenty bars for cellos

      The cellos stretch a small sigh into a long E♭-minor song. For twenty bars, almost nothing accompanies them: a timpani roll, then a held G♭ in the violas. The orchestra has left them an enormous empty room.

    5. The brass find D major

      Trumpets and trombones open out into D major. The brass sing a chorale in long notes while strings and harp keep sweeping beneath them. Mahler crowns it with a chord marked as the high point of the movement.

    6. The storm has survived

      As the chorale fades, scraps of the opening creep into strings and brass. The woodwinds release another shower of descending triplets. The D-major hymn will have to wait until the finale to close the symphony.

    7. Harp notes in the wreckage

      The last pages scatter little wind and harp notes among pale string figures. The opening’s furious phrases have dwindled to scraps. Three low notes descend towards the close; the last belongs to the timpani.

    How it is joined

    1. A timpani roll, then the cellos

      The woodwinds’ racing notes dwindle to a stop over a quiet timpani roll. Mahler slows to the first movement’s funeral-march tempo and turns to F minor. Cellos sing the march-derived melody, with short wind figures still flickering around it.

    2. The woodwinds bring back the storm

      A rising cello phrase reaches a strained dominant-seventh chord in the brass. Rapid chromatic woodwind figures break in, and the violins recover the fanfare-like theme. Timpani hammer its rising and falling thirds beneath them.

    3. The timpani roll stays behind

      The woodwinds race to a halt again. A timpani roll continues beneath the cellos’ slower E♭-minor line; later the violas add a held G♭. A leap and falling second from the storm become the beginning of a long song.

    4. D major opens above the strings

      Rising figures drive towards a broad D-major statement in trumpets and trombones. The strings turn their rushing notes into scales and arpeggios, reinforced by harp, while the brass extend the melody into a chorale.

    5. Short figures invade the chorale

      The D-major brass chords subside. The opening five-note figure returns in fragments among strings and brass, then woodwind triplets restore the falling chromatic lines. Another short outburst follows before the harp and scattered strings are left to finish.

  3. III. Scherzo — Kräftig, nicht zu schnellPeabody Symphony Orchestra
    16:42

    A horn player takes the floor in the enormous central dance of Mahler’s Fifth. Country Ländler and Viennese waltz trip over one another, then stop for a horn call that seems to come from far outside the ballroom.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A horn takes the floor

      Three notes from the horns set the dance going. Mahler gives one player a separate corno obbligato part: a soloist inside the orchestra. Its jaunty tune meets the violins’ country dance, both in three, their accents constantly nudging one another off balance.

    2. Violins with a lilt

      The first violins start a softer waltz in B♭ major. Much of the accompaniment is plucked, leaving the tune room for its little hesitations. The marking is Etwas ruhiger — a little calmer.

    3. The call cuts in

      The opening call interrupts the waltz. Soon the strings chase one another through a fugato, each entry diving in while the earlier line is still running. Mahler marks this writing wild.

    4. A horn beyond the ballroom

      The solo horn stretches the waltz into a slow, spacious song. Its phrases linger and stop short; smaller groups of instruments answer. After so much elbowing on the dance floor, this one player seems to have walked outdoors.

    5. Bach on the shelves

      Mahler subscribes to the collected edition of Bach’s works. In the Fifth, tunes begin to run alongside one another with a new persistence: the scherzo’s strings can keep their own dance going underneath the horn’s.

    6. The dance is plucked

      In D minor, the strings pluck fragments of the dance. Without the bows sustaining them, the phrases seem to tiptoe from note to note. A tune that filled the orchestra now fits into the gaps between a few players.

    7. D major, crowded again

      The opening horn call brings back D major and the scherzo’s tunes. The strings keep racing around them. A country dance, a waltz and scraps of fanfare all share the same floor.

    8. One last turn

      Running strings set the coda spinning. The waltz’s dotted rhythms and the opening horn call pile up around them. At the end, the horns reclaim the tune, and two sharp versions of the first call stop the dance.

    How it is joined

    1. B♭ major, with plucked strings

      A rising version of the horn call interrupts the busy dance. At Etwas ruhiger, first violins begin the B♭-major waltz above plucked string accompaniment and a separate cello line. The three-beat bar stays in place; the tune takes more time over it.

    2. The trumpet interrupts the waltz

      The trumpet abruptly takes the opening horn signal. D major and the scherzo’s quicker dance return. Soon the running string figure enters successively in lower parts, turning the accompaniment into a fugato.

    3. Repeated horn notes become a song

      The horns overlap on repeated F♮s. The solo horn takes that same note and extends it into a slower version of the waltz, above dwindling accompaniment. Its phrase hesitates, then stops before finishing.

    4. The first call restores D major

      The brass dance themes and string runs crowd together in a quickening passage. The opening horn call cuts through them, restoring the first tune and D major. The string runs continue into the reprise, with the melodies moving between different desks.

    5. The bass drum starts the dance

      The horn’s waltz sinks into the bass and comes to a cadence. The bass drum quietly taps a familiar tattoo. Running strings enter softly, then burst out with the waltz’s dotted rhythms above them; the horn call joins the rush towards the ending.

  4. IV. Adagietto — Sehr langsamPeabody Symphony Orchestra, Baltimore
    10:21

    Mahler sets aside the Fifth Symphony’s winds and brass for this song without a singer. Strings lean into a melody in F major while harp notes spread beneath them; the quiet ending leads straight into the finale.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A melody gathers its breath

      The violas hold a chord while the harp begins to move. Cellos enter, then the first violins find their long F-major melody. Mahler writes seelenvoll, soulful: the notes lean against the harmony before settling into place.

    2. Mengelberg’s note about Alma

      Willem Mengelberg writes in his score that the Adagietto is Mahler’s declaration of love to Alma. He says both Mahlers tell him so. In his account, Mahler sends her the manuscript without a covering letter, and she understands.

    3. Warmth on the G string

      The middle moves more freely. The first violins play on the G string, with warmth and a full tone, while the harp falls silent. The string melody presses towards G♭ major; a brief return of harp arpeggios helps it swell along the way.

    4. The harp finds its way back

      The strings slow and die away. Harp triplets return under held chords, and the opening melody begins again at Tempo I. The harp’s small, separate notes make the long string phrases feel suspended in the air.

    5. A funeral and a film

      In June 1968 Leonard Bernstein conducts the Adagietto at Robert Kennedy’s funeral in New York. Three years later Luchino Visconti makes it the musical centre of Death in Venice. A movement associated with love enters public life as music of farewell.

    6. The high phrase opens out

      The violins climb and hold their high notes while the cellos rise beneath them. Mahler asks for broad bow strokes and a full sound. At the crest, the phrase slows further and begins its long descent.

    7. F major, then straight on

      The harp has stopped, leaving the strings to ease down into F major. Mahler holds the last chord for a long time, then asks for the Rondo-Finale to follow without a break. This stillness belongs inside a symphony that is about to wake up.

    How it is joined

    1. The harp leaves the strings alone

      The opening phrases settle and the harp stops. At Fliessender, the first violins begin a warmer, more mobile line on the G string. The harmony travels towards G♭ major, with a brief harp return before the new key settles.

    2. Harp triplets restore the opening song

      The middle’s string figures slow through zurückhaltend and morendo. The harp re-enters in triplets under sustained string chords. At Tempo I (Molto Adagio) the opening melody returns, with plucked bass notes underneath.

    3. Broad bows carry the high notes

      Over harp arpeggios, the cello line climbs into the violins’ high phrase. Mahler asks for broad bow strokes as the strings crescendo. The basses change from plucking to bowing, strengthening the harmony beneath the final expansive statement.

    4. The strings finish without the harp

      The harp strikes its last broad chord and falls silent. Under Sehr zurückhaltend, the strings descend and diminish through the closing harmony into F major. The final fermata is marked long, with attacca Rondo-Finale beneath the basses.

  5. V. Rondo-Finale — AllegroPeabody Symphony Orchestra
    17:09

    Mahler’s Fifth ends in D major with a horn tune, chasing string parts and a brass chorale big enough to fill the hall. Even the preceding Adagietto gets up and dances.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One A becomes a conversation

      The horn holds an A, the note left behind by the Adagietto. Bassoon, oboe and clarinet try out little phrases around it. The finale begins as a conversation among a few players, each putting a tune on the table.

    2. The horn finds a tune

      The horn gathers those small phrases into the rondo’s D-major tune. A second horn follows close behind. The melody has the sturdy gait of a country dance, with little falling figures that will keep turning up throughout the movement.

    3. Cellos start the chase

      The cellos begin a running line before the previous phrase has quite finished. Other strings enter behind them and keep going. This is a fugato: one player’s arrival adds a new voice to the conversation without silencing the others.

    4. The Adagietto learns to dance

      The finale borrows a melody from the Adagietto and sets it among quick string figures. Its long, yearning phrases acquire a spring in their step. Mahler’s direction for the transformed tune is schwungvoll — with swing.

    5. Bach beside the lake

      While writing the Fifth, Mahler is also living with Bach’s collected works, to which he subscribes. The finale sends its string parts after one another in fugues and canons, then lets a horn stroll through with a tune.

    6. The bass gets the dance

      The big climb subsides and the dance tune drops into the bass. Scraps pass between low strings and woodwinds before the horns and violins take them up again. Mahler starts another ascent from this quieter floor.

    7. The chorale comes home

      The brass chorale from the second movement returns in D major. Quick strings run beneath its broad chords. Earlier, the hymn dissolved back into the storm; here it carries straight on towards the symphony’s finish.

    8. A sharp last stroke

      The closing pages hurry into Presto. Horn calls and rushing strings seem ready to start another round, but Mahler cuts them short with the little figure from the introduction. A sharp stroke ends the symphony.

    How it is joined

    1. The oboe hands over to the horn

      The oboe lingers on B♮. The horn takes that note into the main tune at Allegro commodo, with a second horn following in imitation. Open fifths in the accompaniment establish D major beneath the tune’s falling phrases.

    2. The brass open into D major

      A long descending trombone line and horn calls usher in the D-major chorale. Mahler writes Pesante above its broad brass chords. The strings keep their quick figures moving underneath, carrying the hymn into the faster coda.

  1. IV. Adagietto — Sehr langsamConcertgebouw Orchestra, Willem Mengelberg · May 1926, Concertgebouw, Amsterdam
    7:17

    Willem Mengelberg’s Concertgebouw strings slide between notes together and let Mahler’s Adagietto move with a singer’s flexible breath. Made in Amsterdam in 1926, this is a performance by a conductor who knew Mahler and championed his music.

    Notes as it plays
    1. The melody leans forward

      Harp and low strings prepare the first violin entry. Mengelberg brings its pickup forward within the bar, giving the phrase a gentle push before it stretches out. The melody breathes unevenly, with small changes of speed inside its long line.

    2. The whole section slides

      The strings slide audibly between some notes, moving together as a section. This portamento gives the tune the connected vowels of a singing voice. Mengelberg makes the slides part of the phrase, alongside its pauses and sudden surges.

    3. Mengelberg meets Mahler’s music

      At Krefeld in 1902, Mengelberg hears Mahler conduct the première of his Third Symphony. He invites him to Amsterdam the following year. The Concertgebouw becomes one of Mahler’s strongest homes, and Mengelberg keeps conducting the music after the composer’s death.

    4. The strings move more freely

      At Fliessender, the harp withdraws and the violins begin a warm melody on the G string. The harmony moves towards G♭ major. The strings carry most of this middle alone, with one brief return of harp arpeggios.

    5. The return takes more time

      Harp triplets return beneath the held strings, and the first melody begins again. Mengelberg gives this reprise more time than the opening. Its long notes stretch across the bar while the harp keeps placing smaller notes beneath them.

    6. The high phrase broadens

      The violins climb over rising cellos and harp arpeggios. Mahler asks the strings for broad bow strokes and a full sound; the basses begin to bow beneath them. The phrase opens out before its final descent.

    7. Strings finish the song

      The harp falls silent. Strings descend into the last F-major chord and hold it under a long fermata. In the complete symphony, Mahler asks the Rondo-Finale to begin without a break.
