Anton Bruckner / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 7, WAB 107

1883 · WAB 107 · Orchestra including four Wagner tubas

The Leipzig première in 1884 made his name internationally. The closing funeral music of the Adagio is a lament for Wagner, who died while Bruckner was composing the symphony.

4 recordings · 57 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro moderatoHochschulsymphonieorchester, University of Music and Theatre Munich; Marcus Bosch, conductor · Munich, 2020 or 8 November 2021
    17:55

    The first movement, Allegro moderato, of Bruckner’s Symphony No. 7 in E major, WAB 107, in a live performance in Munich conducted by Marcus Bosch. A long cello melody rises over tremolo violins and passes through the orchestra, and the timpani play only in the closing pages, with one sustained roll under the final E major.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening theme, cellos and horn

      Cellos and a solo horn rise through E major above tremolo violins. Violas join the melody, then a clarinet. The single melodic line takes up most of the opening page.

    2. Bruckner’s account to Hans Richter

      Bruckner told Hans Richter that the melody had come to him in a dream, in which his old mentor Ignaz Dorn played it on a viola and promised him success. Bruckner woke, lit a candle and wrote the tune down.

    3. Second theme, oboe and clarinet

      Oboe and clarinet play the second theme together, marked Ruhig (calm), while the strings play separate lines beneath them. Later in the movement the strings play the melody in inversion.

    4. Autograph first page, September 1881

      The first page of Bruckner’s autograph is dated 23 September 1881, from his summer holiday at St Florian. He did not complete this movement until 29 December 1882, and in the meantime he wrote the whole of the Scherzo and Trio.

    5. Third theme, violins in octaves

      The violins play in octaves over repeated short notes in the basses. Bruckner marks the violin line immer hervortretend (always prominent). The third theme is built from compact phrases and moves more briskly than the long melodies before it.

    6. Cohrs on the third theme

      Benjamin-Gunnar Cohrs, who edited the symphony in 2015, heard in this third theme an echo of the parallel passage in Beethoven’s Eroica. He also found traces of Bizet’s Carmen, a Vienna favourite at the time.

    7. Opening figure in the cellos

      At letter I the cellos resume the opening figure beneath sustained flute and bassoon notes. From letter K clarinet, bassoon and horn exchange shorter fragments of it.

    8. Last meeting with Wagner, 1882

      In July 1882 Bruckner was at Bayreuth for the first season of Wagner’s Parsifal, and met Wagner for the last time. He later recalled Wagner’s parting words: “Be calm, Bruckner. Goodnight!” Wagner died in Venice on 13 February 1883, six weeks after Bruckner completed this movement.

    9. Inversion of the opening theme

      The opening melody returns inverted, in C minor. Woodwind entries follow one another downwards in canon, and brass and strings play the chords beneath them. Bruckner restores Tempo I, and the quiet development is interrupted abruptly at full strength.

    10. Opening theme with its inversion

      At letter O the opening cello theme returns in horn and trumpet. Above it, flute and first violins play the theme in inversion, so the two forms sound together, one rising and the other falling.

    11. First performance, Leipzig, 1884

      Arthur Nikisch conducted the premiere with the Gewandhaus Orchestra in Leipzig’s Neues Theater on 30 December 1884. Works by Liszt and excerpts from Wagner’s Götterdämmerung were also on the programme. Bruckner, then sixty, had his first great public success with a symphony.

    12. Munich performance, March 1885

      Little more than two months after Leipzig, Hermann Levi conducted the Seventh in Munich on 10 March 1885. The two performances marked Bruckner’s international breakthrough. He dedicated the symphony to King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Wagner’s great patron.

    13. First timpani entry

      The timpani, silent until this point in the movement, begin a soft roll on E. Double basses hold the same note while clarinets, oboe and flute climb above them. Bruckner marks the passage sehr feierlich (very solemn).

    14. Rheingold prelude and the ending

      Wagner’s Rheingold prelude opens on a long low E♭ in the double basses. The Berlin Philharmonic’s note heard it again at the end of this movement, where basses and timpani hold a long E. The same note identified other Wagnerian echoes across the symphony.

    15. E pedal, letter X

      E major is held beneath flowing violin figures and broad wind phrases. Trombones, tuba and timpani sustain the bass while the other instruments join the ascent. The marking begins “very calm”, then asks for gradually a little more speed.

    Joins

    1. Oboe and clarinet take over

      The first great crescendo subsides into quiet strings. At B, marked Ruhig, oboe and clarinet begin the new tune together. Horn and trumpet hold soft sustained notes, and the violins and violas play shorter phrases beneath the wind melody.

    2. Woodwind figures lead to the third theme

      Woodwind figures lead to the third theme. The violins play it in octaves, marked immer hervortretend, above repeated bass notes and sustained chords in the lower strings. A soft horn note bridges the entry. The winds return as the string phrase extends.

    3. Strings drop out at letter H

      The orchestra’s forceful closing phrases die away until only the strings are left, playing softly. At H, marked Ruhig, the strings stop altogether, and the double basses are silent too. Flute, oboe and clarinet exchange phrases above soft notes in the horn and trombone.

    4. Cellos return beneath held winds

      The woodwind passage slows under a ritardando. At I, a tempo, the cellos begin a rising figure beneath sustained flute and bassoon notes. Violas and basses return with held notes, followed by quiet violin chords and an oboe phrase.

    5. Flute over cellos and basses at L

      The brass crescendo gives way to exchanges among flute and strings. At L, marked Ruhig, the flute plays a short, detached phrase while cellos and basses begin a continuous line below it. The violins enter next, and then the clarinet joins the exchange.

    6. Inverted opening theme in C minor

      Soft flute phrases over held strings lead directly into letter M, Tempo I. The woodwinds play the opening theme inverted, in C minor, and the bassoons follow in canon. Horns, trumpets and low brass add rhythmic figures against chords in the strings.

    7. Woodwind after the C-minor tutti

      The C-minor tutti ends. At N, oboe, clarinet and bassoon enter softly again with horn and strings, and trumpets and low brass stop. The woodwind phrases overlap as the harmony moves through D and E♭ towards the return of the E-major melody.

    8. Cello theme returns beneath its inversion

      At O, the cellos play the E-major opening tune again, joined first by horn and then by trumpet. Above it, flute and first violins descend with the tune’s inversion. Second violins and violas play quiet tremolo chords, and the basses rest.

    9. Short wind phrases replace the broad chords

      The broad orchestral phrases stop at letter T, marked Ruhig. Flutes and oboes play short phrases above a moving clarinet line. The strings follow each phrase and overlap it, and a soft horn enters between the entries. The same calm marking appeared earlier at the wind-led close of the exposition (letter H in the Gutmann first edition).

    10. Timpani roll on E at letter W

      W follows the closing climax of the recapitulation and is marked sehr feierlich. The timpani enter for the first time with a soft roll on E, above a held E in the double basses. The clarinet begins the rising wind line, with cellos and violas moving beneath it and the violins holding quiet chords.

  2. II. Adagio — Sehr feierlich und sehr langsamHochschulsymphonieorchester, University of Music and Theatre Munich; Marcus Bosch, conductor · Munich, 2020 or 8 November 2021
    17:56

    The Adagio of Bruckner’s Symphony No. 7 in E major, WAB 107, marked Sehr feierlich und sehr langsam, opens with a C♯-minor melody for four Wagner tubas. Bruckner built this melody to a climax in C major, and the movement has a quiet ending for violins and low brass.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, Wagner tubas and contrabass tuba

      Four Wagner tubas and a contrabass tuba begin in C♯ minor, doubled by violas, cellos and basses. The melody rises, pauses and falls back. The violins rest through its four-bar phrase and enter after it.

    2. Violins on the G string

      The violins enter on their lowest string. Bruckner keeps them on the G string as the melody climbs, marked “G Saite immer fort” in the Gutmann first edition (bars 5–9). Their five-bar phrase follows the four-bar opening.

    3. Remark to Felix Mottl

      Bruckner later told his former pupil Felix Mottl that he had come home one day very sad, thinking that “before long the Master would die”, and that the C♯-minor theme of the Adagio had then come to him. Wagner died in Venice on 13 February 1883.

    4. Quotation from the Te Deum

      Twenty-two bars in, Bruckner quotes the melody he set to “Non confundar in aeternum” (“let me never be put to shame”) in his Te Deum, finished not long before. It appears once only, at a slower tempo than in the Te Deum.

    5. Wagner tubas

      Wagner devised the tenor and bass tubas for his Ring. Bruckner was the first to write for them in a symphony. In this movement four of his eight horn players switch to them.

    6. Moderato in F♯ major, strings

      The strings begin a new tune in F♯ major. The marking is Moderato, three beats to a bar, and the violins play running notes. Low strings play the bass pizzicato while clarinet and bassoon add soft phrases.

    7. Model in Beethoven’s Ninth

      Bruckner modelled this Adagio on the slow movement of Beethoven’s Ninth, in which a slow theme in 4/4 alternates with a faster one in 3/4. Benjamin-Gunnar Cohrs identified the singing theme that follows the first tuba chorale as a quotation of the parallel passage in the Ninth.

    8. Return of the opening theme

      The Wagner tubas bring back the C♯-minor opening. This time the first violins play a separate line above it, and violas and cellos double the brass melody.

    9. Cohrs on the Adagio as funeral march

      Benjamin-Gunnar Cohrs heard a funeral march in the line of those in Beethoven’s Third and Seventh Symphonies. He suggested that Bruckner may first have mourned the dead of the 1881 Ringtheater fire, and only later connected the coda with Wagner’s death.

    10. Return of the tuba theme

      The tuba theme returns varied and expanded, with new countersubjects around it. The music builds to a bright outburst in G major.

    11. Return of the three-beat tune, A♭ major

      The three-beat tune returns in A♭ major, a whole tone above its first appearance. Flutes and oboes double the violins’ melody, and bassoons and inner strings play other moving lines. The plucked bass returns.

    12. Third return of the tuba theme

      The tuba theme returns for the third time. Above it, the first violins play a continuous chain of arpeggios, marked to keep an even strength, without swelling. The tubas and lower strings carry the melody beneath them with crescendo markings.

    13. Sketch and score of the Adagio, 1883

      Bruckner finished the sketch of the Adagio on 11 January 1883. Wagner died in Venice on 13 February. By 21 April Bruckner had completed the orchestral score, and the movement, begun while Wagner was alive, ends as a memorial to him.

    14. Climax in C major, letter W

      The long ascent arrives at C major. Winds and brass hold their melody across the bar, and both violin sections continue in rising and falling figures beneath it.

    15. Cymbal crash at the climax

      The climax carries the symphony’s only cymbal crash, at its exact midpoint. Bruckner first wrote the movement without percussion and added the cymbals later. In the autograph the part is crossed out and marked “gilt nicht”, “not valid”, though the words are thought not to be his; the crash is usually played.

    16. Funeral music for Wagner, 1883

      The last thirty bars are funeral music for Wagner, who died in Venice on 13 February 1883, written “in memory of the highly blessed, dearly beloved immortal master”. Wagner tubas and horns play the closing passage.

    17. Private performance, Munich, 1885

      On the day after the Munich premiere in March 1885, Hermann Levi took Bruckner to Die Walküre. The orchestra stayed after the audience had left. Around midnight Levi had the musicians play the Adagio’s funeral music for Bruckner alone in the empty theatre.

    18. Closing chorale in C♯ major

      At letter Z the low brass hold a C♯-major chorale. Above it the horns play a fragment of the opening melody. The strings sustain the harmony, then play the last chords pizzicato.

    19. Hamburg radio, 1 May 1945

      On 1 May 1945 Reichssender Hamburg broadcast the Seventh’s Adagio after excerpts from Wagner’s Rheingold and Götterdämmerung. At 10.25 that night the music stopped, drums rolled three times, and the announcer reported Hitler’s death.

    Joins

    1. Clarinets descend to quiet brass chords

      The first orchestral crescendo ends and the strings stop. At C, two clarinets descend together above held notes in the horn and trumpet. The Wagner tubas and contrabass tuba then take over in quiet chords before the string melody that follows.

    2. Strings begin in F♯ major, 3/4

      The low brass diminish and stop. At D, Moderato, the metre changes from 4/4 to 3/4 and the strings introduce the F♯-major melody. Violins and violas play flowing figures over a plucked bass, and clarinet and bassoon enter as the phrase continues.

    3. The slow tuba melody returns

      The string tune slows and diminishes. At G, Tempo I restores four beats and the opening C♯-minor melody in Wagner tubas, violas and cellos. First violins play a new line above it, and horns and trumpets enter during the theme.

    4. The three-beat tune returns in A♭

      The orchestral passage in G major slows, grows softer and ends in a short phrase for the wind. At letter P the Moderato tempo and 3/4 time return. The tune first heard in F♯ major now begins in A♭. Flute and oboe join the violins, with figures in the bassoon below, moving lines in the inner strings and pizzicato basses.

    5. Violin arpeggios under the Wagner tubas

      The string phrases grow quieter and slower. At letter S, marked Tempo I, sehr langsam, the Wagner tubas bring back the C♯-minor theme over held bass notes. The first violins begin even arpeggios with an instruction against swelling. The tubas and low strings have a crescendo of their own.

    6. Violin figures in the climb to C

      The violin figures rise through the long crescendo while winds and brass broaden the melody. At W the orchestra reaches C major, with low C in the bass. Both violin sections continue their fast rising and falling figures beneath the sustained upper voices.

    7. Violins turn to pizzicato before tubas

      After the broad C-major climax the dynamic falls. The violins change their running figures to pizzicato and the brass stop. A short woodwind phrase follows, then the strings stop as well. At letter X the Wagner tubas and contrabass tuba play their quiet, close-knit phrases alone. The strings rest throughout these opening tuba phrases.

    8. First violins enter after the horn phrase

      The horns play an expressive phrase above the tuba chords, then diminish to pianissimo. At Y the first violins enter quietly. Violas and plucked low strings join them, followed by the flute, and the broad brass chords give way to separate string and wind lines.

  3. III. Scherzo — Sehr schnell; Trio — Etwas langsamerHochschulsymphonieorchester, University of Music and Theatre Munich; Marcus Bosch, conductor · Munich, 2020 or 8 November 2021
    8:55

    The third movement of Bruckner’s Symphony No. 7 is a scherzo in A minor, in which a trumpet call sounds over a figure in the strings. In the trio between the scherzo’s two fast sections, the violins play a broad tune in F major, and the trumpet’s rhythm is still heard.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, trumpet and strings

      The strings repeat a compact figure. After four bars a solo trumpet enters with an octave leap and a sharply pointed rhythm, and the clarinet follows. The string figure continues underneath.

    2. Inversion of the scherzo figures

      The strings resume quietly and the winds follow in longer phrases. Bruckner inverts the short figures and passes them between high and low instruments.

    3. Composition of the Scherzo, 1882

      Bruckner wrote this Scherzo before he had finished the first movement. He completed the sketch by 14 July 1882 and scored it between 12 August and 16 October. He composed the Adagio after the Scherzo.

    4. Trio in F major

      Soft timpani introduce the trio. First violins play a long F-major melody, marked gesangvoll (songful), over a held bass. The tempo is slower, and the trumpet’s pointed rhythm returns among the violin phrases.

    5. Ringtheater fire, 1881

      On 8 December 1881, Vienna’s Ringtheater burned across the street from Bruckner’s home. Afraid the fire would spread, he gathered his manuscripts. The following day he saw the bodies of victims being carried away, and he described the disaster in a letter to his brother-in-law.

    6. Cohrs on the Trio and the Ringtheater

      Benjamin-Gunnar Cohrs heard the Trio as a picture of the audience in the Ringtheater before the fire. He described it as a lyrical waltz in F major, innocent and intimate, introduced by ghostly timpani, with the people in the stalls in a happy mood and unaware that anything was wrong.

    7. Scherzo da capo

      The trio ends in F major. After the pause, the quick string figure in A minor returns, followed by the trumpet call. Bruckner asks for the whole scherzo again, including its quiet wind exchanges and brass entries.

    8. Cohrs on the returning Scherzo

      Cohrs read the returning Scherzo as a fire, with the strings’ figure as flames. The trumpet call, long heard as a cock-crow, may in his view be the “red cock”, a folk expression for flames on a burning roof.

    9. First performance, Leipzig, 1884

      Arthur Nikisch conducted the premiere of the Seventh with the Gewandhaus Orchestra in Leipzig on 30 December 1884, when Bruckner was sixty. His symphonies had met hostile receptions for years. This performance was a public triumph.

    10. End of the scherzo, A minor

      Woodwind and brass join the strings’ repeated figure. Trumpets carry the opening rhythm forward, and trombones, tuba and timpani reinforce the final climb. The scherzo ends in A minor with the whole orchestra playing together.

    Joins

    1. Timpani open the trio

      The scherzo ends with the full orchestra in A minor. After the rests the timpani play alone, softly, at the slower Etwas langsamer. The first violins then enter with the F-major trio melody, marked gesangvoll. Beneath it the inner strings hold sustained notes, the cellos move and the basses hold an F.

    2. Trio’s F major close to A-minor scherzo

      Flute, oboe and clarinet finish the trio above held F-major chords in the strings, and the horn plays the scherzo’s rhythm. The phrase drops to pianissimo and stops. At Scherzo Da Capo the fast A-minor string figure returns, followed by the solo trumpet’s octave call.

  4. IV. Finale — Bewegt, doch nicht schnellHochschulsymphonieorchester, University of Music and Theatre Munich; Marcus Bosch, conductor · Munich, 2020 or 8 November 2021
    12:34

    The finale of the Symphony No. 7 in E major, WAB 107, marked Bewegt, doch nicht schnell, turns the symphony’s long opening melody into a quick violin tune in sharply dotted rhythms. Three contrasting themes return in reverse order before the horns restate the first movement’s broad E-major melody.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening theme, first violins

      The first violins play the notes that open the symphony in a quick, double-dotted rhythm, over tremolo in the second violins. The cellos and basses then take up the theme. In the first movement the same notes begin a long melody.

    2. Chorale in A♭ major, letter C

      The violins play long, quiet notes in A♭ major. The violas add a second line, and the cellos and basses play a pizzicato bass that moves by semitones. The chords of the chorale change as these lower parts move.

    3. Discarded first page of the finale

      A discarded first page of the finale has no staves for Wagner tubas. According to Cohrs, who edited the score, Bruckner apparently planned the movement without them. The finished finale uses them.

    4. Completion of the score, 1883

      Bruckner finished the sketch of the finale on 10 August 1883 and its orchestral score on 5 September. The first page of the symphony is dated 23 September 1881 at St Florian, nearly two years earlier.

    5. Third theme in A minor, tutti

      Strings, woodwinds and brass play the third theme in A minor together, in octave doublings. The strings are marked to bow strongly throughout, and schwer asks for weight on the falling phrase.

    6. Cohrs on the third theme

      Cohrs heard an organ toccata in the dotted rhythm of the finale’s opening tune and of this third theme. He described the powerful third theme and its restatement as the centre of the movement.

    7. Woodwind and Wagner tubas

      The strings stop. Flute, oboe and clarinet pass a quiet phrase down through the woodwind, and the Wagner tubas follow. A few soft timpani notes on C carry over from the preceding passage, then the drum also stops.

    8. Key plan of the finale

      Bruckner set the finale’s main ideas in E major, A♭ major and C major. These keys lie a major third apart and divide the octave into three equal parts. Relations by fifths play only a minor part.

    9. Hugo Wolf and Hermann Levi

      The composer Hugo Wolf and the conductor Hermann Levi both found certain parts of this finale “incomprehensible”. Both later came to appreciate it.

    10. Georg Tintner on the finale

      The conductor Georg Tintner called this finale, next to the rest of the symphony, “more like the finale in a Haydn symphony”. It runs about thirteen minutes, against twenty-two for the first movement. The Fifth’s finale runs twenty-three minutes against nineteen, the Eighth’s twenty-one against fourteen.

    11. Return of the chorale

      The orchestra stops on a held note. Violins begin the chorale again over plucked cellos and basses, and oboe, clarinet and horn join its long phrases. The third theme has returned before the second; in this recapitulation Bruckner brings the themes back in reverse order.

    12. Two-piano reading, Leipzig premiere, 1884

      Arthur Nikisch played through the new symphony on two pianos with Bruckner’s pupil Josef Schalk, and decided to conduct it with the Gewandhaus Orchestra. The premiere took place in Leipzig on 30 December 1884.

    13. Hermann Levi on the Seventh

      Bruckner told a friend in Linz that Hermann Levi had called the Seventh “the most important symphonic work since 1827”, the year Beethoven died. Levi later called it a Wunderwerk and the “crowning event” of his career as a conductor.

    14. Timpani roll on E, brass entries

      A quiet timpani roll on E begins beneath repeated string chords. Horns enter in pairs with the dotted tune and trumpets follow. The woodwinds then sustain the harmony above them, and each new voice adds to the crescendo over the held bass note.

    15. Return of the first movement’s theme

      The horns lengthen the dotted tune into the broad opening theme of the first movement. Trumpets and clarinets follow with its descending figure while the violins continue their quick figures. The harmony stays in E major, and the timpani roll through to the last chord.

    Joins

    1. From the dotted tune to long notes

      The horn chords and the last violin figures diminish. At rehearsal C the pace broadens and the violins begin the A♭-major chorale in long notes. The violas play below them, and the cellos and basses play a chromatically moving bass pizzicato. The Wagner tubas enter as the phrase continues.

    2. Crescendo into the third theme at F

      The quiet string phrases grow louder in a crescendo. At F, winds, brass and strings enter together with the third theme in A minor, doubled across several octaves. Its falling notes carry repeated accents and the marking schwer.

    3. Strings stop, timpani continue on C

      The string figures diminish and stop, leaving a few soft timpani notes on C. Flute begins a phrase, which passes to oboe and clarinet, and the Wagner tubas follow. The timpani then drop out as well. Winds and brass continue alone through Ruhig and the following a tempo.

    4. Dotted figures in brass and strings

      The short dotted figures pass through the orchestra and grow louder. Winds and brass play separately marked notes against running figures in the strings. At Q the passage broadens, marked Breit und wuchtig (broad and weighty), and then immer breiter, broader still.

    5. Held note and pause before letter S

      The full orchestra comes together on a held note with a pause. At S, Tempo I, the strings begin the chorale softly over pizzicato cellos and basses. The oboe enters first, then the clarinet and horn. The trumpets and low brass rest.

    6. Flutes and violins above the brass

      A crescendo in winds and strings reaches letter V, marked Breit. Flutes and first violins climb in fast figures. Horns, trumpets, trombones and tubas then play the dotted rhythm, and second violins and violas repeat the chords in steady notes.

    7. Strings resume the dotted figure softly

      The orchestral chords slow and diminish. At X the music returns to a tempo. First violins, violas and low strings exchange quiet dotted figures above tremolo in the second violins. The oboe plays a longer phrase, and horn and trumpet then join the gradual crescendo towards Y.

    8. Accented chords to timpani roll at Z

      The full orchestra broadens into accented chords. At Z the brass stop, leaving repeated string chords and a timpani roll on E. Tempo I is marked “anfangs noch sehr ruhig”. Horns enter in pairs, then trumpets, in a crescendo over a bass that does not change.
