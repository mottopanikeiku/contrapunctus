Hector Berlioz / III. Romanticism

Symphonie fantastique, Op. 14

1830 · Op. 14 · Orchestra with harps, bells and expanded percussion

The beloved’s melody returns in all five movements, from the first ardent flute-and-violin statement to the E♭ clarinet’s parody at the witches’ sabbath. Four timpanists supply the thunder at the end of the country scene.

5 recordings · 52 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Rêveries — PassionsIgor Markevitch, Orchestre Lamoureux · 11–17 January 1961
    14:15

    Berlioz’s Symphonie fantastique gives its beloved a melody that will haunt all five movements. Here she first appears in flute and violins, after a slow introduction full of silences and sudden stirrings.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A hesitant beginning

      Flute and clarinet begin quietly, repeating their notes as other wind voices join them. Muted violins take up a melody full of pauses, its phrases separated by fermatas. The strings repeatedly gather courage and fall silent again.

    2. A song survives the fire

      Berlioz burns his early compositions before leaving for Paris, but one melody stays in his memory. Years later it becomes the violins’ song in this introduction. He originally writes it to words from Florian’s Estelle, while grieving over his own adolescent love.

    3. Harriet on the Paris stage

      In September 1827 Berlioz sees Harriet Smithson acting Shakespeare in Paris. He falls in love before they have met. By 1830 he is writing a symphony about an artist haunted by his beloved; Smithson will hear the revised work in December 1832.

    4. Flute and violins fall in love

      The flute and first violins sing the idée fixe — the fixed idea, or obsession. Its opening G hangs across the barline before the melody reaches upwards. Long notes and fresh starts carry the phrase far beyond a neat four-bar tune.

    5. Two rehearsals in Paris

      On 5 December 1830, Habeneck conducts the première at the Paris Conservatoire. The players give their services free. They have only two rehearsals for the new symphony and Berlioz’s cantata Sardanapale. Three movements cause a sensation; the countryside scene barely registers.

    6. The melody in pieces

      In the development, the long tune breaks into shorter phrases. Strings begin a figure and woodwinds answer; a held chord can leave the lower strings moving beneath it. The beloved’s melody keeps returning in these scraps of conversation.

    7. The beloved’s earlier voice

      The idée fixe already exists in Berlioz’s 1828 cantata Herminie, written for the Prix de Rome. He takes a melody first heard in a vocal work and gives it to the orchestra. It will survive a waltz, an execution and a witches’ dance.

    8. Strings against brass chords

      The late Allegro drives quick string phrases against broad wind and brass chords. Trumpets and cornets join the horns, and the violins climb high above them. The score presses from forte to fortissimo.

    9. Chords like a quiet prayer

      Religiosamente. Woodwinds, horns and strings hold broad chords, marked pianississimo. Trumpets and cornets fall silent. In the programme, the artist finds religious consolation; in the score, the restless string figures have finally stopped.

    How it is joined

    1. A held G begins the obsession

      The score changes to C major and Allegro agitato e appassionato assai. After emphatic chords, the strings soften and the bass moves downwards. Flute and first violins begin the idée fixe on G; that note hangs across a barline before the melody rises to C and E.

    2. The orchestra holds a quiet chord

      The strings’ moving phrases slow and settle. At Religiosamente, woodwinds, horns and strings hold a succession of quiet chords, marked pianississimo. The timpani punctuate the bass while trumpets and cornets remain silent. The closing progression settles in C major.

  2. II. Un bal — Valse. Allegro non troppoIgor Markevitch, Orchestre Lamoureux · 11–17 January 1961
    6:08

    Two harps glitter above the strings in Berlioz’s ballroom. The beloved’s melody joins the waltz, vanishes into the crowd, then returns for one last glimpse.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Harps over trembling strings

      The strings tremble quietly while two harp parts enter in turn, climbing through broken chords. The sound swells towards the ballroom. Then the first violins begin the waltz.

    2. The violins begin to dance

      The waltz opens softly in A major. First violins carry the tune; cellos and double basses pluck the first beat of the bar. The melody rises and turns above that steady dancing step.

    3. The beloved in the crowd

      Berlioz’s programme puts the artist in the middle of a brilliant party. He sees his beloved there, just as he sees her in the countryside. The ballroom has its own music, but she brings the melody that has haunted him since the symphony began.

    4. The idée fixe waltzes

      Flute and oboe take the beloved’s melody, now reshaped into three beats to the bar. The strings keep the dance beneath it, with soft chords above running cello and bass figures. A clarinet joins the recollection.

    5. A new ending in Florence

      In Florence in April 1831, Berlioz returns to the ballroom on paper. He reorchestrates the movement and writes a new coda, although the waltz has already pleased the audience at the Paris première.

    6. The dance fills the room

      The waltz returns in A major. Its tune moves between strings and woodwinds, while the harps add rippling figures and bright chords. The private recollection has passed; the dancers are still turning.

    7. A clarinet remembers her

      The strings fall silent. A solo clarinet begins the idée fixe again, with a brief flute phrase, quiet horns and harp chords around it. The melody has only a few bars before the dance sweeps it away.

    8. The ballroom catches fire

      Tempo primo con fuoco — the first tempo, with fire. The strings rush back, the woodwinds join them, and both harp parts throw in chords. A major carries the dance to its emphatic finish.

    How it is joined

    1. The violins step into the waltz

      The rising harps and trembling strings subside. First violins begin the waltz softly in A major, while cellos and basses pluck the first beat. Second violins and violas supply the lighter chords between those bass notes.

    2. The wind players recognise the beloved

      The A-major waltz dwindles to soft string chords. Flute and oboe begin the idée fixe in the same triple metre. Cellos and basses add running figures below it, and the clarinet takes up the melody as the phrase continues.

    3. The strings take the floor again

      The wind melody finishes and the strings begin to rise. A major returns with the waltz: plucked bass notes hold the first beat, bowed strings carry the quick figures, and winds and horns answer above them. The triple metre stays in place.

    4. The strings leave the clarinet alone

      The waltz slows and the strings stop. A solo clarinet recalls the idée fixe while quiet horns and harp chords support it; the flute adds a short answer. The string bass has disappeared with the dancers’ regular tread.

    5. The strings rush back with fire

      The clarinet’s recollection ends. At Tempo primo con fuoco, the strings return in A major with quick rising figures; the wind players and both harp parts join the rush. Bass notes and repeated chords drive the last bars home.

  3. III. Scène aux champs — AdagioIgor Markevitch, Orchestre Lamoureux · 11–17 January 1961
    15:58

    A cor anglais calls across the orchestra to a distant oboe: two shepherds at the end of the day. When the call returns, only thunder answers.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Two shepherds call

      A cor anglais and a distant oboe exchange slow phrases. The oboe is marked lontano — far away. There is no accompaniment at first; the shepherds have the orchestra to themselves.

    2. Three weeks in the countryside

      This Adagio costs Berlioz more than three weeks of work. He puts it aside and returns to it two or three times. In his Mémoires, he sets that struggle beside the march to the scaffold, which he writes in a single night.

    3. Flute and violins sing

      The flute and first violins begin a broad melody in F major, playing together in unison. Plucked strings join beneath them. The shepherds’ short calls have opened onto a song that can stretch across the hillside.

    4. Unease among the trees

      The middle episode brings shorter wind phrases and increasingly restless strings. The idée fixe enters this troubled countryside in the woodwinds and, reshaped, in the basses. Berlioz’s artist begins to fear that his beloved is deceiving him.

    5. A clarinet above plucked strings

      The woodwinds soften as the pastoral music returns. A solo clarinet takes the tune above softly plucked second violins and divided violas. The flute echoes it; the string players keep their bows off the strings.

    6. A melody from the Mass

      The pastoral tune begins life in the Gratias of Berlioz’s Messe solennelle, written in 1824–25. He moves it from E major to F major for the symphony. The Mass disappears for generations; its rediscovery in 1991 reveals the borrowing.

    7. Hiller helps rewrite the scene

      At the première in December 1830, this movement makes little impression. Berlioz decides to rewrite it. His friend Ferdinand Hiller offers advice, and the long countryside scene takes its place among the revisions Berlioz makes over several years.

    8. Thunder answers the shepherd

      The cor anglais calls again. The distant oboe stays silent. Four timpanists supply the thunder, their rolls rising and fading beneath the solitary voice. Berlioz asks for soft sponge-headed sticks.

    9. The sun goes down

      Berlioz ends this scene in his 1855 programme with a few broken phrases: “The sun goes down … distant thunder … solitude … silence.” The second shepherd never answers.

    How it is joined

    1. The flute opens a longer phrase

      The shepherds’ exchange ends. Flute and first violins begin the principal tune in F major, in unison and almost alone. Plucked second violins, violas, cellos and basses join beneath the melody. The broad Adagio continues.

    2. The pastoral tune breaks into fragments

      The F-major pastoral tune gives way to short wind phrases. Strings begin to climb, adding chromatic notes and repeated figures; bassoons, cellos and basses will take a distorted form of the idée fixe beneath trembling violin chords. The score marks the gathering string figures crescendo poco a poco.

    3. The strings put down their bows

      The agitated phrases slow and fade. At Tempo I, flute and oboes answer a sustained note in the solo cellos. The return of the pastoral tune brings a new accompaniment: plucked semiquavers in second violins and divided violas, marked pianississimo, beneath a solo clarinet.

    4. The oboe leaves the shepherd alone

      The F-major pastoral music thins to plucked strings and quiet wind chords. At un poco ritenuto, it yields to the cor anglais. The opening call returns without the distant oboe; timpani tuned to B♭, F, A♭ and C roll beneath it.

  4. IV. Marche au supplice — Allegretto non troppoIgor Markevitch, Orchestre Lamoureux · 11–17 January 1961
    4:48

    In Berlioz’s nightmare, the artist marches to his own execution. A clarinet remembers the beloved for a few bars before the whole orchestra cuts her off.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The drums gather

      Timpani begin softly, struck with sponge-headed sticks. Stopped horns answer above plucked cellos and basses. In Berlioz’s programme, an opium-dazed musician dreams that he has murdered his beloved and is being led to execution.

    2. A tune marches downwards

      The opening march gives its descending tune to cellos and double basses in unison. Plucked upper strings keep the tread beneath it. The tune sinks through G minor, stopping and starting under sharp orchestral replies.

    3. Brass for the procession

      The second march stands tall in B♭ major. Woodwinds and brass carry its square, emphatic tune over the strings and timpani. It has all the ceremony the condemned man could wish to be spared.

    4. The march wins Paris

      At the Paris Conservatoire on 5 December 1830, Habeneck conducts the new symphony after only two rehearsals. The march overwhelms the audience. Berlioz remembers its success in his Mémoires, alongside the failure of the countryside movement to make an impression.

    5. Back to G minor

      The descending march returns in G minor. Bassoons and low strings drive it onwards, and the percussion grows more brutal: wooden timpani sticks, bass drum and cymbals join the procession.

    6. The clarinet remembers her

      The march clears away for a solo clarinet, marked dolce assai ed appassionato — very sweetly and passionately. It begins the beloved’s melody in G major. The orchestra leaves it alone for these last few bars.

    7. The blade and the crowd

      A full-orchestra blow cuts off the clarinet. Two short plucked notes follow, then the drums roll and G-major chords fill the hall. In Berlioz’s dream, the execution has taken place.

    How it is joined

    1. The cellos take the march

      The soft timpani and stopped horns build towards an orchestral chord in G minor. Cellos and double basses then begin the descending march tune together. Violins and violas pluck the accompaniment, giving the bowed bass melody a dry tread underneath.

    2. The brass arrive in B♭

      The bassoon line and strings diminish. Low brass and timpani prepare the new march, which opens in B♭ major. Flutes, oboes, clarinets, horns and trumpets carry the tune above sharply marked strings. The two-beat tread continues.

    3. The bass tune returns in minor

      The B♭-major march withdraws into plucked strings and wind phrases. G minor returns with the descending tune in bassoons and low strings. Their crescendo brings back the brass and adds bass drum, cymbals and hard wooden timpani sticks.

    4. A clarinet alone on the scaffold

      A rising orchestral flourish breaks off. The bass instruments fall silent, leaving the clarinet to begin the idée fixe in G major, pianissimo and dolce assai ed appassionato. Its long held notes replace the clipped march rhythm.

    5. The orchestra cuts the melody off

      The clarinet’s phrase ends under a fortissimo orchestral blow. Two short pizzicato notes follow in the strings; then timpani rolls and wind-and-brass chords establish the G-major close. Berlioz’s programme names the interruption as the fatal stroke.

  5. V. Songe d’une nuit du SabbatIgor Markevitch, Orchestre Lamoureux · 11–17 January 1961
    11:13

    Berlioz brings his artist to his own funeral, surrounded by witches, monsters and the beloved, now laughing in an E♭ clarinet. Deep bells announce the Dies irae; the funeral chant ends up dancing with the witches.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Trembling strings at a funeral

      Muted violins and violas tremble high above the cellos and basses, whose quick figures climb out of the depths. Soft timpani strokes interrupt them. Berlioz’s artist dreams that witches and monsters have gathered for his funeral.

    2. The beloved joins the witches

      The idée fixe returns in the clarinets, covered in trills and grace notes. After the first glimpse on C clarinet, the small E♭ clarinet turns it into a quick, grotesque dance. Berlioz’s beloved has joined the sabbath.

    3. Bells behind the stage

      Bassoons, cellos and basses sink to a long, quiet note. Deep bells enter behind the stage, sounding C and G. Their tolling leaves room for scraps of the coming dance in the strings.

    4. The funeral chant arrives

      Four bassoons and two ophicleides intone the Dies irae, the chant from the Mass for the Dead. Its long notes move together above the bells. Later statements shorten those notes and hand the tune to brighter wind and brass voices.

    5. The witches dance in fugue

      Cellos and double basses start the witches’ round. Violas follow, then violins: each entry begins while the earlier players continue. A tune built from quick rising notes and a turn becomes a dance crowded with pursuing voices.

    6. Berlioz changes the printed story

      In 1845 Berlioz insists that the audience receive his programme before hearing the symphony. In 1855 he allows the symphony to stand alone, without the explanation. The story changes too: in the later version, the whole work takes place in an opium dream.

    7. Funeral chant against witches’ dance

      The wind and brass hold the Dies irae in broad notes while the strings race through the witches’ round beneath it. Berlioz writes both names above the score. The solemn chant and the quick dance now occupy the same bars.

    8. The wood of the bow

      For one late episode, the violins and violas strike their strings with the wood of their bows: col legno. The high repeated notes become a dry rattle. Woodwinds trill above plucked cello and bass notes.

    9. The bows bite again

      The woodwinds have a brief turn alone, then the strings resume bowing, fortissimo. Brass chords and timpani join them. The last pages drive towards C major, with the whole orchestra hammering out the close.

    How it is joined

    1. The clarinet begins a grotesque recollection

      The slow, chromatic introduction yields to a clarinet glimpse of the idée fixe over quiet drums. An orchestral outburst interrupts it. The E♭ clarinet then takes the tune in a quick dance, with oboe chords and running bassoon figures; the score has moved into E♭ major.

    2. The low instruments prepare the bells

      The brilliant E♭-major episode breaks off. Bassoons, cellos and double basses descend in long notes, fading to pianississimo. Bells marked “behind the stage” begin tolling C and G; short string figures stir between their strokes.

    3. Bassoons answer the funeral bells

      The C and G bells leave space for four bassoons and two ophicleides to begin the Dies irae. They play the chant together in long notes, marked forte. High strings and woodwinds remain silent for this first statement.

    4. The basses start the round

      An orchestral chord gives way to the Ronde du Sabbat in C major. Cellos and basses begin its quick rising figure, with a sharply accented turn at the top. Violas enter next, then violins, while the earlier parts keep dancing.

    5. Quiet horns recall the chant

      The round subsides into held string and bassoon notes. Solo horns recall the chant in short phrases; divided violas begin to move beneath them. Cellos and a quiet bass-drum roll join the gathering figures, preparing the return of the dance and chant together.

    6. The strings return to their bows

      After the col legno rattle, the strings pause for a quick woodwind phrase. They return arco, fortissimo, against brass chords and timpani. Cellos and basses bow the low notes again; the closing rush leads to the final C-major chords.
