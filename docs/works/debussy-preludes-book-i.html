Claude Debussy / V. The French Line

Préludes, Book I

1909–10 · Piano

Twelve pieces, each title printed at the end: Voiles, Des pas sur la neige, La cathédrale engloutie, Minstrels.

6 min

The recording

  1. X. La cathédrale engloutieMarcelle Meyer · 1956
    5:40

    Marcelle Meyer plays Debussy’s drowned cathedral: distant bells rise into a great C-major organ, then sink beneath the water. One piano supplies the whole building.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Bells in the mist

      G and D sound across the piano, with a great space between them. Open fifths climb slowly through the middle. Debussy marks the opening Profondément calme, deeply calm, and places it in a gently ringing mist. The bass descends as the bells keep rising.

    2. The drowned city of Ys

      In the Breton legend, the cathedral of Ys lies beneath the sea. On clear mornings it rises from the water; bells toll, priests chant and the organ sounds before the building sinks again. Debussy prints its name only after the last bar.

    3. Out of the mist

      The bass slips down from C to B. Beneath the rising chords, the left hand starts rolling in triplets, and B major shines through. Debussy writes Peu à peu sortant de la brume: little by little, emerging from the mist.

    4. An organ without pipes

      The piano gathers around a G dominant seventh, then plants a low C beneath a procession of full chords. The melody moves inside them, doubled through both hands. Debussy asks for Sonore sans dureté — sonorous without harshness — even at fortissimo.

    5. A chant in shadow

      The C-major chords fall quiet. An earlier chant returns in C♯ minor, low in the piano, above a held bass. Debussy asks for a little more movement and an expression that keeps growing; the small phrase climbs towards a second great chordal crest.

    6. The organ under water

      The broad C-major tune returns pianissimo, lower on the keyboard. Quavers rock beneath it like water moving around a submerged bell. Debussy calls the melody an echo of the phrase heard earlier, and marks the bass flottant et sourd — floating and muffled.

    7. The last bells

      The rocking bass stops. Rising fifths recall the opening, now resting on C. The last chord holds a D among the notes of C major: one small note keeps ringing against the settled harmony.

    How it is joined

    1. C slips down to B

      The rising fifths return over a low C. The bass then drops a semitone to B, and the left hand begins rolling triplets under the B-major chords. Debussy marks the passage as gradually emerging from the mist, while the right hand keeps the opening’s upward shape.

    2. G prepares the organ’s low C

      The rising figures pass from B major through E♭ major to a G dominant seventh. The left-hand triplets stop. After a broad preparation, a low C supports the fortissimo melody in parallel chords, with both hands moving like an organist’s.

    3. The chant returns in C♯ minor

      The organ-like chords soften into widely spaced bell notes. C♯ minor then replaces C major, and the chant first heard near the opening returns low in the piano. The bass is held beneath it; Un peu moins lent starts the next ascent.

    4. Seventh chords sink towards the echo

      The second crest subsides through parallel seventh chords. A low quaver figure begins to rock, and the earlier C-major melody returns above it, pianissimo. The tune now lies lower in the piano, explicitly marked as an echo.

    5. The rocking bass stops

      The quaver accompaniment stops on a low C. The piano returns to the opening’s rising fifths, marked Dans la sonorité du début. Slow bell chords remain, and the last C-major chord keeps D sounding inside it.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. X. La cathédrale engloutieIvan Ilić, piano · April 2006, Paris
    5:25

    Ivan Ilić plays Debussy’s La cathédrale engloutie, where bare fifths become bells and a great C-major melody becomes an organ. The tune returns at the end as though heard through water.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A fifth left open

      The first chord contains only G and D, spread across the keyboard. There is no third to make it major or minor. Rising fifths move through the middle of the piano while the outer notes hang in the air, like bells heard across water.

    2. Triplets stir below

      B major brings sharps into the rising chords. The left hand rolls in triplets beneath them, moving more continuously than the opening bells. Debussy’s marking describes a slow emergence from the mist; the piano grows towards an organ’s full voice.

    3. The piano becomes an organ

      G dominant-seventh harmony prepares a broad C-major statement. Both hands carry the tune in thick parallel chords above a low C. The marking asks for a sonorous sound without harshness: a full organ, with the bass held beneath its moving voices.

    4. The chant starts again

      The great chords retreat into isolated bell notes. A quieter chant returns in C♯ minor, low in the piano, carrying a phrase already heard near the beginning. It gradually climbs and thickens until the hands fill the keyboard again.

    5. Debussy at the piano

      On 25 May 1910, Debussy plays this prelude himself at a Société musicale indépendante concert in Paris. He presents it with three others from the first book: Danseuses de Delphes, Voiles and La danse de Puck.

    6. The organ becomes an echo

      The C-major melody comes back lower and much softer. Quavers rock underneath it, marked floating and muffled; above them the chords sing the old tune as an echo. At the close, the rocking stops and the opening’s fifths ring once more.

    How it is joined

    1. A semitone sets the bass moving

      The bass drops from C to B. The right hand keeps its rising chords, now in B major, while left-hand triplets fill the spaces beneath them. Debussy marks the piano as gradually coming out of the mist.

    2. G opens the door to C

      The ascent passes through E♭ major and reaches G dominant-seventh harmony. The triplets fall away. A low C then supports the broad melody in parallel chords, fortissimo, with the tune doubled through both hands.

    3. Bell notes uncover the chant

      The C-major chords soften into widely spaced bell notes. The bass settles beneath a low C♯-minor chant, marked Un peu moins lent. Its phrase recalls the opening pages and begins to climb again.

    4. The full chords return underwater

      Parallel seventh chords descend from the second crest. A low rocking figure begins beneath them, and the C-major tune returns pianissimo above it. Debussy lowers the chordal melody on the keyboard and calls it an echo.
