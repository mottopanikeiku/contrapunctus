Claude Debussy / V. The French Line

Children’s Corner

1906–08 · Piano

Six pieces for Chouchou, five with English titles. Golliwogg’s Cake-walk stops to quote the opening of Tristan, “with great emotion”, and laughs at it.

6 recordings · 14 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Doctor Gradus ad ParnassumRobert Casadesus, piano · 11 April 1950, Columbia 30th Street Studio, New York
    1:57

    A piano exercise starts dutifully in C major, wanders onto the black keys and ends at a gallop. Robert Casadesus plays the first of Debussy’s six pieces for his daughter Chouchou.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The fingers get busy

      The right hand runs up and down the white keys above a low C. Soon a slower tune peeps out between the semiquavers. Debussy marks the touch égal et sans sécheresse: even and without dryness. This pupil already has a mind of their own.

    2. A father’s tender apology

      On the dedication page of Children’s Corner in 1908, Debussy addresses his daughter: “To my dear little Chouchou, with her father’s tender apologies for what follows.” Two small elephants decorate the page.

    3. The exercise breaks loose

      The opening semiquavers return in C major, very softly. Then the hands spread farther apart and Debussy asks for more and more animation. By the final page the marking is Très animé; the last C-major chords land fortissimo.

    How it is joined

    1. The exercise pauses over held chords

      The running notes descend and slow under Retenu. A short phrase in the piano’s middle register ends on held chords. The key signature then opens out to five flats: D♭ major, with a low D♭ held beneath a gentler version of the exercise.

    2. D♭ slips back to C

      The black-key passage slows once more. Debussy cancels the flats and restores 1° Tempo: C returns in the bass and the right hand picks up its first semiquaver figure, pianissimo. The exercise soon spreads across both hands and begins to accelerate.

  2. II. Jimbo’s LullabyRobert Casadesus, piano · 11 April 1950, Columbia 30th Street Studio, New York
    2:58

    Debussy’s elephant sings from the bottom of the piano, gently and a little awkwardly. Robert Casadesus plays a lullaby whose small tune eventually reaches the treble, still rocking.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. An elephant’s low voice

      The left hand begins alone, low on the piano, with a tune marked doux et un peu gauche — gentle and a little awkward. Its short steps and long pauses suit the elephant in Debussy’s title. Small, soft replies arrive from above.

    2. For Chouchou, with apologies

      The suite’s dedication reads: “To my dear little Chouchou, with her father’s tender apologies for what follows.” Chouchou was his daughter. The contents leaf gives this second piece the French subtitle Berceuse des éléphants, “lullaby of the elephants”.

    3. Rocking beneath the song

      The piano fills the gaps with a descending accompaniment. Longer notes sing above it; little pairs of notes keep answering. Farther into the passage, Un peu plus mouvementé lets the lullaby stir, and the bass starts moving in quavers.

    4. A church for a studio

      Robert Casadesus recorded Children’s Corner on 11 April 1950 in Columbia’s 30th Street Studio in New York. Columbia had turned a disused church into the studio two years earlier, and its musicians nicknamed it “the Church”.

    5. The tune comes home

      The first tempo returns, and the familiar tune sits above softly repeated notes. The descending accompaniment resumes beneath it. Debussy keeps the piano very quiet, then asks the closing notes to die away; a low B♭ has the last word.

    How it is joined

    1. The bass begins to rock

      The first page ends with isolated low notes and a pause. The left hand then starts a descending pattern, repeated beneath the upper melody. Debussy marks it pianissimo and asks the tune to stand out slightly; the elephant has acquired a rocking chair.

    2. A pause before the lullaby returns

      Retenu holds back the closing phrase of the middle. After a fermata, 1° Tempo brings the tune back, with repeated quavers tucked underneath it in the right hand. The left hand holds its low notes before resuming the descending accompaniment.

  3. III. Serenade for the DollRobert Casadesus, piano · 11 April 1950, Columbia 30th Street Studio, New York
    2:13

    A little serenade passes between the pianist’s hands, its tune flicked into life by grace notes. Debussy keeps the soft pedal down throughout, even when the doll’s song becomes emphatic.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A tiny plucked serenade

      Detached chords make the piano sound plucked. The tune starts in the left hand, with little grace notes that flick upwards before each longer note; then the right hand takes it over. Debussy marks the whole exchange léger et gracieux — light and graceful.

    2. The soft pedal stays down

      Sharps and flats creep into the tune, and sudden accents interrupt its polite little phrases. The soft pedal stays down even at forte: Debussy puts that instruction in a footnote. The doll’s serenade can raise its voice without losing its veiled piano tone.

    3. The left hand flows

      The grace-note song returns in E major, now carried by continuous left-hand quavers. The hands trade short notes again as the piece ends. Finally the piano reaches up to a high E and leaves a whole bar of silence under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. Chromatic notes enter the serenade

      The piano keeps its short, detached notes while the bass moves through chromatic pitches. Debussy cancels the four-sharp signature and writes En animant un peu. The tune strays from E major; the grace notes survive, now interrupted by sharply accented chords.

    2. A running bass carries the tune

      Rising runs cross the piano and end in a sharp accent. The left hand then starts a quiet, even pattern of quavers. Above it the right hand recalls the grace-note tune in E major, with long notes where the opening accompaniment used to pluck.

  4. IV. The Snow Is DancingRobert Casadesus, piano · 11 April 1950, Columbia 30th Street Studio, New York
    2:02

    Snow falls in tiny notes passed between the pianist’s hands. Debussy slips a slow, sad tune through the flurry, then sends the last flakes towards the top of the keyboard.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Snow between the hands

      Each hand drops its notes into the gaps left by the other. Debussy marks the piano doux et estompé — soft and blurred — although the notes themselves are short. A slower tune emerges among them, marked doux et triste: gentle and sad.

    2. Three against the snow

      Repeated notes in the treble fall into groups of three, pulling across the piano’s running accompaniment. Debussy asks for a little hesitation, then the original pace. Sharply accented chords keep breaking into the flurry.

    3. The last high flakes

      The opening pattern returns quietly. Its little notes eventually climb into the highest octaves, where Debussy writes perdendo — dying away. Even here the score says Sans retenir: the snow keeps moving as it disappears.

    How it is joined

    1. Repeated triplets cross the running notes

      The two hands briefly run down and up the piano. At Cédez un peu, the right hand settles on repeated triplets while the left keeps its even semiquavers. The treble line then descends under Au Mouvement, and an accented chord cuts across the pattern.

    2. The bass returns beneath the snow

      The accented chords stop and a quiet line of semiquavers runs alone. A low D then anchors the returning pattern: the hands alternate again, with the original tune above. Debussy marks the return softer still, più pp.

  5. V. The Little ShepherdRobert Casadesus, piano · 11 April 1950, Columbia 30th Street Studio, New York
    2:16

    The piano becomes a shepherd’s pipe: a single line curls into the silence, then answers itself with a little dance. Debussy needs only two pages for three calls and their replies.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A pipe without accompaniment

      A single piano line winds down and up, with small ornaments and a pause at the end. The left hand is silent. Then the tune begins to dance in dotted rhythms, and soft chords arrive underneath it.

    2. Another breath, another call

      The dance settles on a held chord and the accompaniment falls silent. The shepherd’s line starts again, with changed notes and the same easy turns. Its answer comes in soft chords, then pauses over another long-held harmony.

    3. The shepherd’s last dance

      The third call is a little stronger, and the answer grows more animated. Then the dotted dance returns pianissimo. Debussy asks for it to be held back while keeping its rhythm; the final A-major chord is marked ppp.

    How it is joined

    1. A major rings, then one voice

      The first dance slows under Cédez and settles on a very soft A-major chord spread across the piano. After a breath, the right hand resumes alone at the original pace. Its new call reaches towards the black keys before the left-hand chords return.

    2. E major clears for the last call

      The second answer rests on E major, pianissimo. The right hand begins alone again, this time un poco più forte. Dotted rhythms and left-hand chords rejoin it under Plus mouvementé, and the answer grows into the piece’s fullest passage.

  6. VI. Golliwogg’s Cake-WalkRobert Casadesus, piano · 11 April 1950, Columbia 30th Street Studio, New York
    2:44

    Robert Casadesus plays Debussy’s cakewalk, a dry, springy dance with an unexpected guest: Wagner’s Tristan. The grand romantic phrase arrives “with great emotion” and gets a cheeky answer.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A dance with elbows

      The tune pushes its accents across the beat while the left hand keeps up a regular bass-and-chord step. Debussy marks it très net et très sec — very clear and very dry. Sudden loud notes poke through the quieter ones.

    2. Dancers and a racist doll

      In Paris in 1902–03, seven African American women, the Florida Creole Girls, help make the cakewalk a craze. Debussy’s title names a racist caricature: Florence Kate Upton’s blackface doll, introduced in an 1895 picture book.

    3. Tristan gets an answer

      The slower middle quotes the opening of Wagner’s Tristan und Isolde. Debussy writes avec une grande émotion — with great emotion — above the yearning phrase. Short, clipped chords answer it, as if the pianist has just remembered where the dance floor is.

    4. Back to the cakewalk

      The first tempo snaps back, with E♭ major and the familiar bass-and-chord step. The tune recovers its sharp elbows. Near the end, Debussy lets it dwindle to little fragments before two fortissimo chords shut the door.

    How it is joined

    1. The dance slows into G♭ major

      The E♭-major dance dwindles to small replies between the hands. Six flats replace three, and Un peu moins vite slows the pace. Over repeated bass notes, grace-note chords introduce the G♭-major middle; soon the piano stretches out into the chromatic phrase from Tristan.

    2. The bass puts the dance back

      The middle slows under Retenez and Toujours retenu. A short descending line is left alone before a breath mark. Then 1° Tempo restores E♭ major and the left hand’s bass-and-chord accompaniment; the right hand resumes its syncopated dance.
