Johann Sebastian Bach / I. The Baroque

Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050

1721, dedication · Flute, solo violin, obbligato harpsichord, strings and continuo

3 recordings · 21 min

In the Hall

  1. I. AllegroAdvent Chamber Orchestra; Roxana Pavel Goldstein, violin; Constance Schoepflin, flute; Matthew Ganong, harpsichord · February 2008
    9:59

    The first movement, Allegro, of Bach’s Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050, in which flute, violin and harpsichord share the solo passages. Bach wrote out sixty-five bars for the harpsichord unaccompanied.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening ritornello, strings

      The two violin parts play the same brisk D-major refrain over a moving bass. The flute is silent. This refrain is the ritornello, the music the strings bring back between the soloists’ excursions.

    2. Flute, violin and harpsichord soloists

      Flute and violin exchange short phrases, and the harpsichord’s right hand joins them while its left hand plays the bass. Bach wrote the keyboard’s solo passages out in full, alternating with figured-bass accompaniment.

    3. Arrest at Weimar, 1717

      In 1717 Bach signed a contract with Prince Leopold at Köthen before he had permission to leave Weimar. His employer would not accept his resignation and had him confined for a month. He was released and dismissed in December, and took up the post at Köthen.

    4. Harpsichord cadenza, 1721 version

      An earlier version gives the harpsichord an eighteen-bar solo. In the score dedicated to the Margrave of Brandenburg in 1721, Bach expanded it to sixty-five bars. The flute and violin have no solo of comparable length.

    5. Visit to Berlin, March 1719

      In March 1719 Bach travelled from Köthen to Berlin to collect a new harpsichord for Prince Leopold’s court. He probably played for Margrave Christian Ludwig on that visit. In the 1721 dedication of these concertos Bach recalled doing so “a couple of years ago”.

    6. Collegium Musicum, Leipzig, from 1729

      From 1729 Bach directed Leipzig’s Collegium Musicum, whose weekly concerts at Café Zimmermann moved outdoors in summer. The programmes included his orchestral suites and his violin and harpsichord concertos, and Bach appeared in them as a keyboard player.

    7. Harpsichord solo, bars 154–218

      The other instruments stop. The harpsichord keeps the bass and plays increasingly elaborate figures above it. The solo is written out in full in the score, down to its fastest notes, and the orchestra rests for sixty-five bars.

    8. Harpsichord cadenza

      Bach gives the keyboard broken chords and rapid alternations of the kind a concerto would usually give to a solo violinist. In the unaccompanied cadenza the left hand plays the bass notes while the right hand moves above it.

    9. Final ritornello, strings

      At bar 219, after the long keyboard solo, the strings return with the ritornello in D major, with the violins in unison. The harpsichord goes back to accompanying, and the flute rests to the end, as it did at the opening.

    Joins

    1. From opening refrain to first episode

      The eight-bar string refrain closes in D major. Flute and solo violin then exchange phrases above the harpsichord’s bass, and the keyboard’s right hand joins their runs. The pulse is unchanged. The solo episode modulates to A major, and the next string refrain enters in that key.

    2. Harpsichord solo before the ritornello

      The other players stop, and the harpsichord plays both the bass and the upper parts. Its solo prolongs A major, the dominant of D. Sequences and broken chords follow, in steadily smaller note values, until the strings return with their refrain.

    3. Cadenza into the final D major ritornello

      The harpsichord’s last running figures lead into the opening violin refrain in D major, which returns as the final ritornello from bar 219. The keyboard changes to accompaniment, marked accomp., and the string basses return beneath the violins. The flute rests through the closing refrain.

  2. II. AffettuosoRoxana Pavel Goldstein, violin; Constance Schoepflin, flute; Matthew Ganong, harpsichord; Advent Chamber Orchestra · February 2008
    5:56

    The second movement, Affettuoso, of the Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050, is scored for flute, violin and harpsichord alone, with the orchestra silent. The three instruments exchange dotted phrases in B minor, and the harpsichord plays both the bass line and solo passages.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Flute, violin and harpsichord

      The violin begins and the flute follows. Under their dotted phrases the harpsichord plays a walking bass line, then adds its right hand as a third melodic part. The accompanying strings rest for the whole movement.

    2. Dedication to the Margrave, 1721

      On 24 March 1721 Bach dated the dedication of six concertos to Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg. Each concerto calls for a different group of players. This one sets a flute beside a violin and a harpsichord.

    3. Marking changed from Adagio to Affettuoso

      The earlier version of this concerto marks the movement Adagio. In the 1721 score for the Margrave, Bach changed the marking to Affettuoso (tenderly, with feeling). Flute, violin and harpsichord play the movement without the orchestra.

    4. Third refrain in F♯ minor

      The refrain moves to F♯ minor. The flute leads and the violin follows. When the harpsichord takes over, its right hand keeps the dotted rhythm and decorates it while the left hand continues underneath.

    5. Refrain in G major

      The refrain moves to G major. The flute leads again, and Bach alters the phrase before the harpsichord takes it over. The harpsichord’s passage that follows is the longest in the movement, with short interjections from flute and violin.

    6. Violinist at Weimar, 1703

      In 1703 the eighteen-year-old Bach spent about half a year as a violinist in Duke Johann Ernst III’s private orchestra at Weimar. Later that year he took the post of organist in Arnstadt.

    7. Köthen and Leipzig, 1717–1723

      Bach signed his contract at Köthen in August 1717. As Thomaskantor in Leipzig from 1723 he owed the churches a cantata every Sunday and feast day, about sixty a year. Prince Leopold still commissioned a birthday cantata from him each year.

    8. Return of the opening phrase

      The violin plays the first phrase again in B minor, and the flute follows as at the start. The harpsichord’s right-hand solo ends, and its bass part supports the last exchange and the closing chord.

    Joins

    1. Flute refrain returns in F♯ minor

      The harpsichord’s running right-hand line ends, and the flute brings back the dotted refrain in F♯ minor. The violin follows over the keyboard bass. Bach marks the returning solo lines forte, and the harpsichord goes back to accompanying.

    2. The refrain in G major

      The flute and violin play the dotted phrase in G major, marked forte, and the keyboard plays the bass. This refrain is a variant of the opening melody. At its close the harpsichord’s right hand has another solo, and the flute and violin reply in short phrases.

    3. The violin returns to B minor

      The extended keyboard solo ends. Over the harpsichord’s bass, the violin starts the opening B-minor refrain and the flute follows. Both solo lines are marked forte. For the final cadence the keyboard’s right hand plays accompaniment.

  3. III. AllegroAdvent Chamber Orchestra; Roxana Pavel Goldstein, violin; Constance Schoepflin, flute; Matthew Ganong, harpsichord · February 2008
    4:57

    This is the third movement, Allegro, of Bach’s Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050, a fugue in a gigue rhythm in which the solo violin enters first, followed by the flute and harpsichord. The whole opening section returns at the end.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening entries, solo violin

      The solo violin begins with a dotted figure followed by a run of triplets. The flute enters next, then the harpsichord. The movement is in two beats to the bar, and the three parts start to overlap.

    2. Middle section in B minor

      After the close in D major, the middle section begins in B minor at bar 79, with the solo violin marked piano. The violin continues its triplets, and the same short figures now pass through minor keys. The harpsichord plays more running passages of its own.

    3. Violinist at Weimar, 1703

      In 1703 Bach played violin in Duke Johann Ernst III’s private orchestra at Weimar. He stayed for about half a year before he became an organist in Arnstadt.

    4. Harpsichord solo, bars 164–176

      The flute, violins, viola and bass instruments stop. For thirteen bars (bars 164–176 in the Mutopia edition) the harpsichord plays alone, its two hands exchanging running figures. It is the longest passage in the movement for the harpsichord without the other instruments.

    5. First publication, 1852

      In 1852, more than a century after Bach’s death, C. F. Peters published the Fifth Brandenburg Concerto. It was performed at the Leipzig Gewandhaus the following year. The score used here descends from Wilhelm Rust’s edition of 1871.

    6. Return of the opening section

      The opening returns in D major, beginning with the solo violin. The flute and harpsichord follow in their original order. Bach repeats the whole outer section, and the strings again enter after the soloists.

    7. Music of the Bach Family, 2008

      The Advent Chamber Orchestra played this concerto last in a programme called Music of the Bach Family, given on 10 and 17 February 2008. Just before it, the orchestra’s flautist Constance Schoepflin played the flute concerto in G, Wq 169, by Bach’s son Carl Philipp Emanuel.

    Joins

    1. Triplets turn to B minor

      The outer section closes on D major. The violin then begins broken-chord triplets in B minor, marked piano. The two-beat metre continues unchanged, and the middle section develops the soloists’ figures through a succession of new keys.

    2. Solo violin begins the reprise

      The middle section ends with a cadence, and the solo violin restores the opening D-major subject. Flute and harpsichord follow with their answering entries, and then the accompanying strings return. The short dotted figure and its triplets are heard again from the start of the movement.
