Edward Elgar / IV. Fin de siècle

Cello Concerto, Op. 85

1918–19 · Op. 85 · Cello and orchestra

The cello speaks first, in emphatic chords, before the violas introduce the rocking 9/8 melody. The finale brings back the Adagio and the opening chords, then ends with startling brevity.

16 min

The recording

  1. Abridged complete concerto · four acoustic sidesBeatrice Harrison, cello; Symphony Orchestra; Edward Elgar, conductor · 22 December 1919 and 16 November 1920
    15:52

    Beatrice Harrison plays with Elgar conducting, barely two months after the concerto’s première. Their four acoustic record sides condense the work into sixteen minutes, from the cello’s opening declaration to the impatient finale.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Harrison’s opening declaration

      Four insistent cello chords open the concerto. The orchestra answers quietly, and the soloist replies as though speaking. Soon the violas introduce the swaying E-minor tune that the cello takes over and carries through the movement.

    2. Salmond at Queen’s Hall

      Felix Salmond gives the première on 27 October 1919 at Queen’s Hall. Elgar conducts the London Symphony Orchestra after too little rehearsal. Harrison takes the new concerto into the recording studio that December, with its composer again on the podium.

    3. Alice posts the score

      On 8 August 1919 Alice takes the completed concerto to Fittleworth Post Office and sends it to London. Elgar has spent the summer working at Brinkwells, often rising at four or five in the morning to score it and add new passages.

    4. Plucked chords, flying bow

      The scherzo begins with plucked cello chords and little trials of a quick tune. Once the Allegro molto is under way, the bow scarcely rests: semiquavers climb, turn and tumble through the solo part.

    5. The Adagio’s long breath

      The cello sings in B♭ major, far from the concerto’s E minor. Elgar marks the melody molto espressivo. Small rises and falls join into an almost continuous line, with the orchestra leaving the soloist space to linger.

    6. Back to Hayes

      Harrison and Elgar return to Hayes on 16 November 1920 to record the slow-movement side used here. The other three sides come from December 1919. HMV issues the two discs in May and June 1921.

    7. A finale in four minutes

      The last record side gives the finale four minutes. Its brisk E-minor tune keeps returning to the cello, with firm accents and short orchestral replies. Harrison and Elgar compress the movement heavily to fit the disc.

    8. The Colvins’ concerto

      The dedication names Sidney and Frances Colvin, friends of the Elgars. In late July 1919, with the work almost finished, Elgar writes to Sidney that it is “a real large work and I think good and alive”.

    How it is joined

    1. The bow becomes a plucking hand

      The first movement ends with three plucked cello notes in E minor. The next movement answers with plucked chords, marked Lento, before the bow tests a quick semiquaver figure. Elgar writes attacca in the score; on these records the two movements occupy separate sides.

    2. The fast bow begins to sing

      The scherzo’s last running notes end in a few plucked strokes. On the third side, the cello begins the B♭-major Adagio with a rising, bowed phrase, marked molto espressivo. The orchestra accompanies a long melody after the scherzo’s bursts of semiquavers.

    3. Back to the brisk finale

      The B♭-major Adagio ends with the cello slowing into a held note. The fourth side returns to the finale’s E-minor argument: accented cello phrases and brisk orchestral answers. Its main tune carries the marking Allegro ma non troppo, with risoluto in the solo part.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. IV. Allegro — Moderato — Allegro ma non troppoSkidmore College Orchestra; cellist and conductor uncredited
    11:34

    Elgar’s finale starts with a swagger, then the cello stops it in its tracks. Near the end, memories of the Adagio and the concerto’s opening break into the music before its last, abrupt rush.

    Notes as it plays
    1. The cello stops the march

      The orchestra starts briskly, but the cello soon takes its own time. Elgar writes Moderato, quasi recitativo: a speech-like solo, with pauses, chords and a sudden sweep across the strings. The finale has to wait while the soloist speaks.

    2. Risoluto: the cello joins in

      The cello takes up the finale’s brisk tune, marked risoluto — resolute. Accents give the phrase a swagger, and the orchestra answers it. Even here, Elgar keeps interrupting the march with little rests.

    3. A piano at Brinkwells

      In August 1918 Elgar has an old Steinway upright brought to Brinkwells, his Sussex cottage. He begins the Violin Sonata the next day, then writes the String Quartet and Piano Quintet. The Cello Concerto follows in 1919.

    4. Good and alive

      In July 1919 Elgar tells Sidney Colvin that he has nearly completed a cello concerto: “a real large work and I think good and alive”. He dedicates it to Sidney and Frances Colvin. On 8 August Alice posts the finished score from Fittleworth.

    5. Too little rehearsal

      Felix Salmond gives the première at Queen’s Hall on 27 October 1919, with Elgar conducting. Albert Coates takes most of the available rehearsal for the rest of the programme. The concerto reaches its first audience underprepared.

    6. The cello takes its time

      Late in the finale, Elgar gives the cello room for an extended lament. The score asks it to broaden, press forward and linger again. Con passione appears over a phrase that pulls against the orchestra’s earlier march.

    7. One last E-minor rush

      The finale’s tune rushes back, now marked Allegro molto. Cello and orchestra exchange short, forceful phrases and close the concerto in E minor. After the long solo meditation, the ending is startlingly brief.
