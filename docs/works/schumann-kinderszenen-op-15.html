    Donald Betts plays “Von fremden Ländern und Menschen” (Of Foreign Lands and Peoples), the first piece of Kinderszenen, Op. 15. The tune is in G major, and triplets run between the melody and the bass.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening melody over triplets

      The melody leaps from B to G and comes down F♯–E–D. Under it, triplets pass between the hands while the bass moves in two beats to the bar.

    2. Repeat of the opening eight bars

      The opening eight bars are repeated. The melody again reaches the high G. Beneath it, a B♭ appears in the triplet accompaniment of bars 1 and 3 before the harmony returns to G major.

    3. Autograph for Marie Wieck, 1847

      In 1847 Schumann gave the manuscript of this piece to his sister-in-law Marie Wieck. It is now in the Robert-⁠Schumann-⁠Haus in Zwickau and is the only complete autograph of any of the thirteen Kinderszenen.

    Joins

    1. Second voice in the right hand

      The phrase ends in G major and the music moves to E minor. At bar 9 the right hand plays in pairs of notes, starting on G and B, over an E–B–E accompaniment, and the triplets continue underneath. The two-beat pulse stays the same, and the new phrase is marked piano.

    2. Fermata before the G major tune

      The new phrase ends with a ritardando and a fermata. The triplets then resume beneath the G major tune, marked piano. The tune starts on D, and the leap to G and the descending phrase after it are heard again.

    3. Repeat back to the paired notes

      The G major cadence ends on a long B in the melody. At bar 22 the repeat sends the pianist straight back to bar 9, where the paired G and B return over the E minor accompaniment. The six-bar excursion and the fermata are played again, followed once more by the return.

    1:37

    “Kuriose Geschichte” (A Curious Story) is the second piece of Schumann’s Kinderszenen, Op. 15. It is in D major and is built from clipped phrases and short flourishes, with a sudden singing line.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening theme, bars 1–⁠16

      An upbeat and two quick grace notes are followed by a short, clipped figure. Small rests break up the D major tune. At the end of each eight-bar phrase, both hands run upwards together.

    2. Contrasting phrase in G major

      Four quieter bars follow. The tune moves in even quavers over a walking bass, in G major. The clipped opening figure then returns, still marked piano.

    3. Letter to Clara Wieck, 1838

      Writing to Clara Wieck in March 1838, Schumann listed A Curious Story among his new pieces. “You will enjoy them,” he told her, “though of course you must forget yourself as a virtuoso.” He had chosen about twelve from thirty little pieces; thirteen reached print.

    Joins

    1. Into the slurred piano phrase

      The upward run reaches A. A pair of quavers leads into the new phrase, marked piano, with G in the melody over B in the bass at bar 17. The rests that broke up the tune stop, and both hands now play slurred lines.

    2. Return of the opening, bars 20–⁠21

      The bass reaches D and rises through D♯ to E. Above it, the upbeat and grace notes of the opening return in D major. The opening was marked mezzo-forte, and this return is marked piano.

    3. Ritardando to the D major cadence

      In bars 27–⁠30 the tune rises to F♯, slows under a ritardando and falls to D over a D major cadence. A quaver upbeat brings back the slurred passage, with G over B in the following bar. The left hand keeps up its movement throughout.

    4. Return of the opening in D major

      The bass climbs D–D♯–E again, and grace notes lead into the last statement of the opening in D major. Schumann marks it piano, raises it to mezzo-forte in its second half and writes a ritardando over the final descent to D.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, bars 1–⁠8

      Each short phrase begins sforzando-piano, a sharp accent followed at once by a soft dynamic. The semiquavers run up and down under staccato dots. The left hand plays short chords and descending semiquaver runs of its own.

    2. Second part, bars 9–⁠20

      The second part carries the same running figure onto G–B–D harmony at bar 9. In the slurred bars 13–⁠16 the upper notes are held while the left hand climbs by semitones into bar 17. The opening chase then starts again.

    3. Marie Wieck’s concert, 1853

      On 18 January 1853 Marie Wieck, Clara’s younger half-sister, played six of the Kinderszenen. She placed Schumann’s third piece last on the programme.

    Joins

    1. The same phrase in G major

      The B minor phrase ends on B. The next outburst keeps B in the bass and places G above it, moving to G major harmony. The sforzando-piano attack and the staccato semiquavers continue across the double bar without a break.

    “Bittendes Kind” (Pleading Child) is No. 4 of Kinderszenen, Op. 15. The piece is seventeen bars long and ends on an A-seventh chord.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening phrase, bars 1–⁠4

      The melody leaps up from B and falls back through G–F♯–E. Semiquavers fill the spaces beneath it, and the bass rests after each note. The two-bar phrase is played twice, first piano and then pianissimo.

    2. Presentation copy for Liszt, 1839

      In Vienna on 13 March 1839 Schumann inscribed an early copy of Kinderszenen “to F. Liszt in friendship”. Above Pleading Child he added a tempo direction by hand: Langsam (slowly).

    3. Final chord, bar 17

      The piece ends in bar 17 on a dominant seventh chord, A–C♯–E–G, held under a fermata. The chord does not resolve to D.

    Joins

    1. A second phrase over D

      The first phrase ends on A major harmony. A new phrase begins with C♮ over a repeated D in the bass, and E♭ and B♭ appear in the accompanying semiquavers. Schumann marks it piano, then repeats it pianissimo.

    2. Melody rises over a B bass

      The bass moves from D to B. Above it the melody rises F♯–G♯–A–B, reaches C♯ and then falls to A. This two-bar phrase is marked ritardando. Schumann repeats the phrase with the same ritardando, in bars 9 and 11 of the Breitkopf & Härtel first edition.

    3. Return of the opening phrase

      The rising phrase settles on A major harmony. The bass drops to a lower A and resumes its separated notes, and above them the opening leap from B to G returns. Bars 1–⁠4 are repeated, with a further ritardando in the final repetition.

    4. A fermata on the dominant

      The last ritardando leads to a low A, with C♯ and E in the middle and G above. Schumann holds this dominant seventh under a fermata, and the piece ends without resolving it. The next piece, Happy Enough, is in D major.

    “Glückes genug” (Happy Enough), No. 5 of Kinderszenen, Op. 15, is in D major, and its tune runs above a line of semiquavers. In the middle section a B♭ enters and the harmony moves briefly towards F major.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, bars 1–⁠8

      The melody rises, falls and breaks off on short rests. Semiquavers run underneath, and at times the left hand plays its own rising phrase. Schumann marks the whole opening piano.

    2. B♭ and modulation to F major

      A B♭ changes the direction of the tune. Over C in the bass, it forms a dominant-seventh chord, C–E–G–B♭, in bar 17, which resolves to F major in bar 18. The semiquavers continue without a break.

    3. Chromatic bass and final cadence

      The bass rises by semitones while the melody descends. A final ritardando slows the running notes to a cadence in D major.

    Joins

    1. G♯ pickup to the repeat

      The melody’s G♯ pickup rises to A, and the first eight bars start again. The bass has climbed by semitones to C♯ and holds it into the returning phrase. Schumann writes the repetition out in full.

    2. A rises to B♭

      This time the pickup is A, and the next melody note is B♭. A C in the bass carries across the barline beneath it and makes a C-seventh chord. The following bar is in F major, and the semiquavers continue.

    3. D major, then back again

      The bass moves A–D as the melody reaches D, with a ritardando. The printed da capo sends the pianist back to the opening music, and its G♯ pickup and semiquavers return at the earlier pace.

    4. The C-seventh chord returns

      The A rises to B♭ above the C bass again. The same C-seventh chord leads into F major. Later, octave bass notes climb by semitones towards the final D major cadence.

    “Wichtige Begebenheit” (An Important Event) is No. 6 of Schumann’s Kinderszenen, Op. 15. The piano plays accented A major chords and octaves in a marching rhythm.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, bars 1–⁠8

      The piece is in three beats to the bar, marked forte, with accents over thick chords and octaves in the left hand. In bar 6 the right hand moves into the bass clef as well.

    2. Bars 9–⁠16 in D major

      The music is now fortissimo and in D major. The right hand holds its chords longer while the bass descends in octaves beneath them. The whole eight-bar section is enclosed in repeat signs.

    3. Rellstab’s review, 1839

      Reviewing Kinderszenen in 1839, Ludwig Rellstab took the title to mean music for children to play. A child would need “three hands”, he complained. He added that even then some of it would be difficult.

    Joins

    1. A major close, then D major

      The opening closes in A major. An A major pickup becomes the dominant of D, and the next bar brings D major chords marked fortissimo. The right hand holds a chord while the bass octaves descend D–A–G–F♯.

    2. From D major back to A major

      The repeated section ends on D major and a rest. The opening A major chord then returns high in the right hand, above rising A–C♯ octaves in the bass. The dynamic drops back to forte, and the dotted rhythm resumes.

  7. No. 7 · Träumerei — DreamingDonald Betts · 2005
    3:23

    “Träumerei” (Dreaming), No. 7 of Kinderszenen, Op. 15, stands at the centre of the set. It is twenty-four written bars long, and its quiet piano melody repeatedly rises a little higher.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening phrase, bars 1–⁠4

      A single C rises to F and is held. The melody then climbs again towards the F an octave above. Underneath, the other voices move slowly, each with its own line.

    2. Diary entries, 24–⁠25 February 1838

      On Saturday, 24 February 1838, Schumann recorded in his diary that he had composed “the little thing Träumerei”. The following evening he worked on another Kinderszene, in F major, which he thought “very pretty”.

    3. Letter to Clara Wieck, 1838

      On 17 March 1838 Schumann told Clara that he had written “some thirty small droll things” and chosen about twelve for his Kinderszenen. He promised that she would enjoy them, though she would have to forget herself as a virtuoso.

    4. Early programmes, Dresden and Vienna

      When Marie Wieck played six of the pieces in Dresden on 18 January 1853, Träumerei was not among them. In Vienna on 17 March 1857 Clara’s pupil Nanette Falk played three, all from Marie’s list, so it was missing from that programme too.

    5. Return of the opening, bar 17

      After the middle passage’s cadences in G minor and D minor, the opening F returns at bar 17 and Schumann restores the first phrase. A high A is held under a fermata in bar 22. The final descent is marked ritardando.

    6. Illustrated editions and a film

      In 1877 Friederike Medem published silhouettes with verses for four of the pieces, Träumerei among them. A Leipzig edition of 1886 paired the music with poems by Albert Träger and pictures by Alexander Zick. In 1943–⁠44 UFA made a film called Träumerei.

    Joins

    1. Bar 8 into the repeat

      The music slows in the eighth bar onto C major harmony, the dominant of F. A low C in the melody becomes the upbeat back to F, while the bass moves beneath it to F. The repeat begins with the same upward figure as the opening.

    2. The climb reaches E♭

      The new phrase begins on F over an F bass, as at the opening. Its climb now reaches E♭ in bar 10, and the inner parts move towards G minor, with a cadence in that key at bar 12. In bar 13 the rising shape begins again on B♭, with B♭ in the bass.

    3. Return from D minor to F

      The middle phrase comes to rest in D minor and slows. A grace-note C leads up to the opening F, and a low F in the bass beneath it restores the home key. The first phrase of the tune begins again.

    1:57

    “Fast zu ernst” (Almost Too Serious) is No. 10 of Kinderszenen, Op. 15. It is in G♯ minor, and the piano’s melody runs across the barlines and stops repeatedly under fermatas.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Tied melody in 2/8

      The piece is in G♯ minor and in 2/8, so each bar holds two quavers. The melody ties its notes across the bar lines while the left hand plays semiquavers underneath. Schumann writes ritardandos before the fermatas, and both return through the piece.

    2. Return of the opening theme

      The opening pattern returns, with the melody tied across the barlines over a turning left-hand accompaniment. The phrases again stop on fermatas. Each new phrase begins quietly.

    3. Kempowski’s childhood memoir, 1995

      Walter Kempowski printed this piece in musical notation at the front of his childhood memoir, published in Berlin in 1995. The book was titled Weltschmerz. Kinderszenen fast zu ernst (world-weariness, childhood scenes almost too serious).

    Joins

    1. Return of the tied opening melody

      The opening pattern of the G♯ minor song returns. The right hand holds notes across the short barlines, and the left hand plays the same rolling semiquavers as before. The dynamic stays quiet, and the pauses at the ends of phrases are kept.

    2. Ritardando to the final G♯ minor chord

      In the final phrase the tied melody is drawn out above the rolling bass. A ritardando runs through the last bars, and both hands move towards a held G♯ minor chord. Fermatas in both staves stop the melody and the low bass together.

    “Fürchtenmachen” (Frightening) is No. 11 of Schumann’s Kinderszenen, Op. 15. A soft tune on the piano is followed by a sudden fast passage and then by sharp accented chords.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening bars, repeated B

      A B recurs in the melody. Below it, sharps and naturals move the inner parts by semitones. Schumann marks the opening pianissimo.

    2. Schneller passage

      In the passage marked Schneller (faster), the right hand plays short chords while the left runs up and down, still pianissimo. Later Schumann adds sforzando marks to the returning tune.

    3. Liszt’s open letter to Berlioz, 1839

      In October 1839 Liszt praised Kinderszenen in an open letter to Berlioz, calling Schumann “a poet full of feeling and a great musician”. Schumann kept the cutting in his scrapbook and marked the closing paragraph in red.

    Joins

    1. G major cadence to the first Schneller

      The opening phrase closes in G major. Over the next passage Schumann writes Schneller, and the dynamic stays pianissimo. The right hand plays short chords, and in the gaps between them the left hand plays fast figures. Both hands play lightly and detached.

    2. Return of the opening B

      The repeated G major Schneller section ends. The melody returns to the opening B, with chromatic inner notes beneath it. A few bars later the soft tune is interrupted by a forte passage with repeated sforzandos.

    3. Return of the Schneller passage

      The tune closes in G major, and the quick passage returns. Schneller and pianissimo are marked again above the staccato chords. The left hand plays fast figures beneath them, and a repeat sign sends both hands back through the passage a second time.

    4. Final return of the opening tune

      The short chords stop, and the opening tune returns over its chromatic accompaniment for the final eight bars. This time it continues to the end without the sforzando interruption. The bass and the closing chord end on G major.

    “Der Dichter spricht” (The Poet Speaks) is No. 13, the last piece, of Kinderszenen, Op. 15, and follows “Kind im Einschlummern”. A G major hymn is interrupted by an unbarred recitative in the treble, then returns with a longer ending.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening chords in G major

      Both hands begin with piano chords in G major, in common time. The inner voices move by step. The phrase swells under a hairpin and closes before the music begins again.

    2. Unbarred recitative

      The piano leaves the hymn. Short runs and grace notes move through the treble, pianissimo, and pause under fermatas. Schumann writes the passage without barlines, as an unbarred recitative in triplet groups (first edition, Breitkopf & Härtel, p. 20, third system).

    3. Return of the opening chords

      The opening chords return in G major. The ending is longer this time. The chords descend, the ritardando extends over the final bars and the bass reaches a low G, and the last chord is held under a fermata.

    Joins

    1. The hymn breaks into descending lines

      The G major hymn divides into separate descending lines. The bass moves beneath held upper notes. A ritardando and fermatas interrupt the regular four-beat bars, and each continuation follows a pause.

    2. Fermata before the unbarred recitative

      The descending bass reaches a fermata and stops. The right hand then plays small triplet figures marked pianissimo. From this point the passage has no barlines, and grace notes and chromatic turns take it up into the treble.

    3. Return to the G major hymn

      The free piano line descends through chromatic notes and slows with a ritardando. At the double bar the G major hymn returns, marked piano. Both hands go back to measured chords, and the four-beat bars begin again.
