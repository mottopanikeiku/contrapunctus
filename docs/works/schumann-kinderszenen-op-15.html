Robert Schumann / III. Romanticism

Kinderszenen, Op. 15

1838 · Piano

13 recordings · 22 min

In the Hall

  1. No. 1 · Von fremden Ländern und Menschen — Of Foreign Lands and PeoplesDonald Betts · 2005
    1:52

    Donald Betts opens Kinderszenen with a G-major tune that seems to remember its own childhood. Between the melody and the bass, little triplets keep turning.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A tune over triplets

      The melody leaps from B to G and comes down F♯–E–D. Under it, triplets pass between the hands while the bass keeps its own two-beat tread. Three things happen at once, softly.

    2. The tune comes round

      The same eight bars begin again. The high G is still the melody’s favourite note; beneath it, B♭ briefly colours the accompaniment before the music settles back into G major.

    3. A page for Marie

      In 1847 Schumann gives the manuscript of this piece to his sister-in-law Marie Wieck. It is now in the Robert-Schumann-Haus in Zwickau: the only complete autograph of any of the thirteen Kinderszenen.

    How it is joined

    1. The melody grows a second voice

      The G-major ending opens into E minor. The right hand now sings in pairs of notes, beginning with G and B, while the triplets continue underneath. Schumann keeps the two-beat pulse and marks the new phrase piano.

    2. A pause before G major

      A ritardando and fermata hold back the end of the new phrase. The triplets then resume beneath the G-major tune, marked piano. Its first note is D; the leap to G and the descending answer are familiar.

    3. Back to the paired notes

      The G-major cadence reaches a long B in the melody. The repeat sends the pianist straight back to the paired G and B over E-minor accompaniment. The six-bar excursion, the fermata and the homecoming all happen again.

  2. No. 2 · Kuriose Geschichte — A Curious StoryDonald Betts · 2005
    1:37

    A storyteller with a habit of starting again: clipped D-major phrases, little flourishes and a sudden singing voice. Schumann makes the piano sound wonderfully talkative.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A storyteller clears his throat

      An upbeat, two quick grace notes, then a clipped answer. The D-major tune keeps breaking its sentences with tiny rests. At the end of each eight-bar telling, both hands run upwards together.

    2. The piano starts singing

      Four quieter bars interrupt the chatter. The tune moves in even quavers, and the bass walks beneath it; G major softens the change. The clipped opening figure will return, still marked piano.

    3. Clara must forget the virtuoso

      Writing to Clara Wieck in March 1838, Schumann lists A Curious Story among his new pieces. “You will enjoy them,” he tells her, “though of course you must forget yourself as a virtuoso.” He has chosen about twelve from thirty little pieces; thirteen reach print.

    How it is joined

    1. From chatter to song

      The upward run reaches A. A pair of quavers leads into the new phrase, marked piano, with G in the melody over B in the bass. The little rests disappear from the tune; both hands now move in slurred lines.

    2. The grace notes return

      The bass reaches D and rises through D♯ to E. Above it, the familiar upbeat and grace notes restart the D-major story. This telling begins piano; the first began mezzo-forte.

    3. A slower sentence, then song

      The tune climbs to F♯, slows and descends to D over a D-major cadence. The quaver pickup starts the singing passage again, quietly, with G over B in the next bar. The left hand continues walking.

    4. One last upbeat

      The bass climbs D–D♯–E again, and the grace notes usher in the last D-major telling. Schumann begins it piano, raises it to mezzo-forte halfway through and slows the final descent to D.

  3. No. 3 · Hasche-Mann — Blind Man’s BuffDonald Betts · 2005
    0:37

    A game of chase in B minor, with sharp cries and scampering semiquavers. The piano scarcely has time to catch its breath.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A cry, then running feet

      Each little outburst begins sforzando-piano: a sharp accent followed at once by softness. Semiquavers race up and down, with staccato dots keeping them springy. The left hand adds short chords and its own downward scurries.

    2. A change of hiding place

      The second part carries the same running figure into G-major harmony. A smoother passage briefly holds the upper notes while the left hand climbs by semitones. Then the B-minor chase starts again.

    3. Marie saves the chase

      In Dresden’s Cosel-Palais on 18 January 1853, Marie Wieck plays six of the Kinderszenen. She saves this one for the end. Clara’s younger half-sister turns Schumann’s third piece into a finale.

    How it is joined

    1. The same cry in G major

      The B-minor phrase lands on B. The next outburst keeps B in the bass but puts G above it, turning to G-major harmony. The sforzando-piano attack and staccato semiquavers carry straight across the double bar.

  4. No. 4 · Bittendes Kind — Pleading ChildDonald Betts · 2005
    1:23

    A child asks again, more softly. Seventeen bars of rippling piano writing end on an A-seventh chord, still waiting for an answer.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Asking twice, more softly

      The tune leaps up from B and falls back through G–F♯–E. Little semiquavers fill the spaces beneath it, while the bass leaves a rest after each note. The two-bar plea comes twice: first piano, then pianissimo.

    2. Schumann writes slowly for Liszt

      Vienna, 13 March 1839: Schumann inscribes an early copy of Kinderszenen “to F. Liszt in friendship”. Above Pleading Child he adds a word by hand: Langsam, slowly.

    3. The answer never comes

      The last chord is A–C♯–E–G, held under a fermata. It wants to resolve to D. Schumann ends the piece here, with the child still waiting.

    How it is joined

    1. A different plea over D

      The first plea ends on A-major harmony. A new phrase starts with C♮ over a repeated D bass; E♭ and B♭ enter the accompanying semiquavers. Schumann marks it piano and then repeats it pianissimo.

    2. The melody climbs over B

      The D bass moves to B. Above it the melody climbs F♯–G♯–A–B, then reaches C♯ before settling on A. A written ritardando stretches this two-bar phrase; Schumann repeats the slowing with the phrase itself.

    3. The first plea returns

      The rising phrase settles on A-major harmony. The bass drops to a lower A and resumes its separated notes; above them the opening leap from B to G returns. The first plea is back, with another ritardando waiting in its final repetition.

    4. A fermata on the dominant

      The last ritardando leads to a low A, with C♯ and E in the middle and G above. Schumann holds this dominant seventh under a fermata. The expected D-major chord is left for the next piece, Happy Enough, to find.

  5. No. 5 · Glückes genug — Happy EnoughDonald Betts · 2005
    1:30

    Happy Enough bubbles along in D major, its tune hopping above a stream of semiquavers. A B♭ slips into the middle and takes the music briefly towards F major.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Happiness with busy hands

      The tune rises, drops and catches itself on little rests. Semiquavers keep running underneath, and the left hand sometimes sings back with its own rising phrase. Schumann marks the whole opening piano.

    2. A B♭ slips in

      B♭ changes the tune’s direction. Over C in the bass, it belongs to a dominant-seventh chord that leads to F major. The semiquavers continue without a break; the familiar little tune has wandered into a new key.

    3. The bass climbs home

      The bass climbs by semitones while the melody comes down. A final ritardando brings the running notes to rest on D major.

    How it is joined

    1. G♯ sends the tune round again

      The melody’s G♯ pickup rises to A, and the first eight bars start again. The bass has climbed by semitones to C♯, which carries over into the returning phrase. Schumann writes the repetition out in full.

    2. A rises to B♭

      This time the pickup is A, and the next melody note is B♭. A C bass carries across the barline beneath it, making a C-seventh chord. The following bar answers in F major, with the semiquavers still flowing.

    3. D major, then back again

      The bass moves A–D as the tune settles on D, slowed by a ritardando. The printed da capo sends the pianist back to the opening music. Its G♯ pickup and bubbling semiquavers restore the earlier pace.

    4. The C-seventh chord returns

      A rises to B♭ above the C bass again. The same C-seventh chord leads into F major; later, octave bass notes climb by semitones towards the final D-major cadence.

  6. No. 6 · Wichtige Begebenheit — An Important EventDonald Betts · 2005
    1:00

    Something terribly important has happened: the piano announces it in accented A-major chords and marching octaves. Schumann keeps a straight face.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A very grand announcement

      The piano makes a procession out of three beats to the bar. Accents sit over thick chords; the left hand marches in octaves. Halfway through the opening, both hands descend into the bass clef, still sounding immensely pleased with themselves.

    2. The procession grows bigger

      Now fortissimo, and D major. The right hand holds its chords longer while the bass octaves stride down beneath them. Schumann encloses the whole eight-bar section in repeat signs.

    3. A child needs three hands

      Reviewing Kinderszenen in 1839, Ludwig Rellstab mistakes the title for a promise of children’s music. A child would need “three hands”, he complains. Even then, he adds, some of it would be difficult.

    How it is joined

    1. The bass strides into D major

      The opening closes in A major. An A-major pickup becomes the dominant of D, and the next bar lands on D-major chords marked fortissimo. The bass octaves descend D–A–G–F♯ under a held right-hand chord.

    2. D major closes, A major answers

      The repeated section ends on D major and a rest. The opening A-major chord then returns high in the right hand, above rising A–C♯ bass octaves. The dynamic falls back to forte, and the dotted rhythm resumes.

  7. No. 7 · Träumerei — DreamingDonald Betts · 2005
    3:23

    At the centre of Kinderszenen, a quiet piano melody keeps reaching a little higher. Träumerei is only twenty-four written bars; each phrase seems reluctant to end.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. C reaches up to F

      A single C rises to F and waits. Then the melody climbs again, towards the F an octave above. Underneath, the other voices move slowly, each with a line of its own.

    2. Schumann’s little thing

      Saturday, 24 February 1838. In his diary Schumann records composing “the little thing Träumerei”. The following evening he is back at another Kinderszene in F major, which strikes him as “very pretty”.

    3. Thirty little droll things

      On 17 March 1838 Schumann tells Clara that he has written “some thirty small droll things” and chosen about twelve for his Kinderszenen. She will enjoy them, he promises, though she will have to forget herself as a virtuoso.

    4. Left out of the early programmes

      When Marie Wieck plays six of the pieces in Dresden on 18 January 1853, Träumerei is not among them. In Vienna on 17 March 1857, Clara’s pupil Nanette Falk plays three, all from Marie’s list, so it misses that programme too.

    5. The first F returns

      The opening F returns after the middle passage’s excursions through G minor and D minor. Schumann restores the first phrase almost exactly. Later, a high A is held under a fermata, and the final descent stretches out with a ritardando.

    6. Pictures, poems and a film

      In 1877 Friederike Medem publishes silhouettes with verses for four of the pieces, Träumerei among them. A Leipzig edition of 1886 pairs the music with poems by Albert Träger and pictures by Alexander Zick. In 1943–44 UFA makes a film called Träumerei.

    How it is joined

    1. The upbeat starts another dream

      The eighth bar slows onto C-major harmony, the dominant of F. A low C in the melody becomes the pickup back to F, while the bass settles beneath it on F. The repeat begins with the same quiet upward reach.

    2. The climb reaches E♭

      The new phrase begins on F over an F bass, just like the opening. Its climb now reaches E♭, and the inner parts lead towards G minor. Four bars later the whole rising shape starts again on B♭, with B♭ in the bass.

    3. D minor releases the opening F

      The middle phrase comes to rest in D minor and slows. A small grace-note C leads up to the opening F; beneath it, a low F restores the home key. The tune begins its first phrase again.

  8. No. 8 · Am Kamin — At the FiresideDonald Betts · 2005
    1:13

    The F major of Träumerei continues here, now with a quicker tune and little detached chords. A fire with some life in it.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A fire with a pulse

      The right hand sings in F major over short chords and bass notes. Rests keep opening little gaps beneath the tune. With two beats to the bar, the piano rocks gently without standing still.

    2. Long notes beneath the flame

      The second part begins mezzo-forte. Long bass notes now last a whole bar, while the melody and its short accompanying chords keep moving above them. The right hand leans on accents as the phrase grows.

    3. The fire burns down

      The closing bars briefly flare with accents in both hands. Then a long ritardando carries the music down the keyboard. The final F-major chord stretches from a low F to the quiet right hand and rests under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The bass holds while the tune moves

      The opening F-major phrase closes. The new phrase begins mezzo-forte, and the left hand lays down long bass notes beneath the short chords. The melody still moves in quavers and semiquavers, now with accents sharpening its turns.

    2. The second ending opens a farewell

      The second ending leads past the repeat into new descending figures. Flats enter the inner parts, and accents briefly animate both hands before the ritardando. The bass finally drops to low F beneath the F-major closing chord.

  9. No. 9 · Ritter vom Steckenpferd — Knight of the HobbyhorseDonald Betts · 2005
    0:44

    A child gallops on a stick horse, and the piano takes the adventure seriously. C-major chords lurch off the beat while the bass keeps its seat.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A slightly lopsided gallop

      The right hand misses the downbeat, then lands a chord halfway through the beat. Its long–short–long rhythm sits awkwardly in a three-beat bar. Accents make the toy horse buck.

    2. The horse keeps its rhythm

      The second part keeps the gallop exactly as it was. New flats and sharps slip into the chords and bass; later the music reaches fortissimo. Both halves have repeat signs.

    3. The children are getting older

      On 6 July 1838 Schumann chases his publisher Raimund Härtel for news of Kinderszenen. “They will grow too old with time,” he writes. “Please — do not forget them; I long to see them.”

    How it is joined

    1. New chords, the same horse

      The first strain closes in C major. The second keeps the right hand’s quaver rest and bounding chords, while the left hand moves into chromatic notes. Its longer bass notes continue underneath the offbeat attacks; later, fortissimo gives the returning C-major chords an extra kick.

  10. No. 10 · Fast zu ernst — Almost Too SeriousDonald Betts · 2005
    1:57

    The games stop for Almost Too Serious. In G♯ minor, the piano’s melody hangs across tiny barlines and repeatedly pauses under fermatas.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A melody between the beats

      The bars are only two quavers long. The melody keeps tying its notes across them, while the left hand rolls semiquavers underneath. In G♯ minor, even the pauses feel troubled: Schumann writes ritardandos before the fermatas.

    2. The same troubled song

      The opening pattern returns: a melody reaching across the barlines and a left hand that keeps turning beneath it. The phrases still break off at fermatas. Each restart is quiet.

    3. Kempowski borrows the title

      Walter Kempowski puts this piece at the front of his childhood memoir in 1995, printed in musical notation. His book is called Weltschmerz. Kinderszenen fast zu ernst: world-weariness, childhood scenes almost too serious.

    How it is joined

    1. The tied melody takes up again

      The G♯-minor song takes up its opening pattern again. The right hand holds notes across the short barlines, and the left hand supplies the same rolling semiquavers. The return retains the quiet dynamic and the pauses at phrase endings.

    2. The last pause holds

      The final phrase stretches the tied melody above the rolling bass. A ritardando runs through the last bars as both hands move towards a held G♯-minor chord. Fermatas stop the melody and the low bass together.

  11. No. 11 · Fürchtenmachen — FrighteningDonald Betts · 2005
    2:03

    Schumann stages a small fright: a soft piano tune, a sudden scamper, then sharp accented chords. The child who does the frightening seems to be enjoying it.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Something stirs under the tune

      A soft B keeps returning in the melody. Under it, sharps and naturals move the inner notes by semitones. The tune sounds innocent; the chords are less reassuring. Schumann marks the opening pianissimo.

    2. A very quiet fright

      Schneller: faster. The right hand throws in short chords while the left races up and down. The new passage is still pianissimo. The loud shocks come later, when Schumann peppers the returning tune with sforzando marks.

    3. Liszt’s praise in red

      In October 1839 Liszt praises Kinderszenen in an open letter to Berlioz, calling Schumann “a poet full of feeling and a great musician”. Schumann keeps the cutting in his scrapbook and marks the closing paragraph in red.

    How it is joined

    1. G major starts to scamper

      The quiet phrase closes in G major. Schumann writes Schneller over the new passage and keeps it pianissimo. The right hand’s short chords leave gaps for the left hand’s racing figures; both hands remain light and detached.

    2. The soft B returns

      The repeated G-major scamper ends. The opening B returns in the melody, with chromatic inner notes beneath it. A few bars later, forte and repeated sforzandos break into the quiet tune.

    3. The same trick again

      After the tune reaches its G-major ending, the same quick passage returns. Schneller and pianissimo appear again above the staccato chords. The left hand races beneath them, and the repeat sign sends both hands round once more.

    4. The fright is over

      The short chords stop, and the opening tune returns over its chromatic accompaniment. This time it continues to the end without the sforzando interruption. The bass and the final chord settle quietly on G major.

  12. No. 12 · Kind im Einschlummern — Child Falling AsleepDonald Betts · 2005
    2:01

    A lullaby in E minor turns to E major and back again. Repeated B’s keep the piano gently rocking, even when the music seems too drowsy to finish.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A B that keeps rocking

      B pulses in the middle of the piano while the upper notes rock above it. The left hand has the same dotted lilt. Schumann keeps the E-minor lullaby quiet, repeating the little figures as if the child is nearly asleep.

    2. The lullaby turns to major

      E major warms the middle passage, marked pianissimo. The rocking continues underneath a fuller right-hand melody. As the phrase descends, the right hand moves into the bass clef, close to the left.

    3. Sleep overtakes the phrase

      The E-minor opening returns, with its repeated B and rocking accompaniment. In the last phrase Schumann stretches a ritardando across several bars. The hands finally stop on a chord held under a fermata, leaving the lullaby hanging.

    How it is joined

    1. E minor turns to E major

      At the double bar, E minor turns directly to E major and the dynamic falls to pianissimo. The left hand keeps rocking on E while the right hand opens into fuller chords. The new G♯ changes the light.

    2. The minor-key rocking returns

      A ritardando slows the middle passage. The key signature returns to one sharp, and the familiar B pulses above E-minor harmony. Schumann marks the return piano; the left hand resumes the opening rocking figure.

  13. No. 13 · Der Dichter spricht — The Poet SpeaksDonald Betts · 2005
    2:45

    After the child falls asleep, the poet has the last word. A G-major hymn breaks into hesitant piano speech, then gathers itself for the quietest of farewells.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A hymn after bedtime

      Both hands begin with quiet G-major chords, like a small choir. The inner notes move with their own careful steps. Schumann lets the phrase swell a little, then closes it before beginning again.

    2. Speech without barlines

      The piano leaves the hymn behind. Little runs and grace notes wander through the treble, pausing under fermatas. Schumann removes the barlines: the phrase can hesitate, collect itself and begin another thought.

    3. The hymn finds its way back

      The opening chords return in G major. This time the ending takes longer: the chords sink, the ritardando stretches over the final bars, and the bass reaches a low G. The last sound is held under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The hymn begins to hesitate

      The G-major hymn opens into separate descending lines. The bass moves beneath held upper notes; a ritardando and fermatas interrupt the regular four-beat bars. The pianist has to wait before each continuation.

    2. A pause opens the recitative

      The descending bass reaches a fermata and stops. Above the silence, the right hand begins small triplet figures marked pianissimo. The barlines disappear; grace notes and chromatic turns carry the passage up into the treble.

    3. The choir returns to G major

      The free piano line descends through chromatic notes and slows with a ritardando. At the double bar the G-major hymn returns, marked piano. Both hands recover their measured chords, and the four-beat bars begin again.
