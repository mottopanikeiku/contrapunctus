Frédéric Chopin / III. Romanticism

Nocturne, Op. 9 No. 2

1831–32 · Piano

Recordings

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. Nocturne in E♭ major, Op. 9 No. 2Frank Lévy, piano · c. 2014
    4:31

    Frank Lévy plays Chopin’s E♭-major Nocturne for Musopen’s project to set the music free. The tune returns with ever more elaborate curls, until the piano breaks loose in a tiny cadenza.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A sixth opens the door

      A single B♭ rises to G, a leap of a sixth. That G hangs in the air while the left hand places a bass note and two chords on each beat. The tune begins to descend, with a little rise on the way.

    2. The tune grows curls

      The first phrase returns, but its long G now trails a flickering ornament. E♭, too, gathers small notes around it. The bass repeats its familiar route while the right hand makes a more elaborate version of the song.

    3. A recording to give away

      In 2014 Lévy joins Musopen’s recording of Chopin’s complete works. The pianist has taught at Juilliard and studied with Leon Fleisher in Baltimore. Here his E♭-major Nocturne becomes part of a library anyone can use.

    4. Minor chords under E♭

      The coda begins with the melody on E♭. Underneath, A♭-minor chords darken that note before E♭ major returns. The bass holds E♭ through both harmonies, so the change happens inside the chord.

    5. Four notes escape the beat

      Senza tempo: the regular beat stops governing the phrase. High octaves hang above a B♭-dominant chord, then four tiny notes circle round and round. The right hand finally uncoils into a descending run.

    6. E♭ after the flight

      The first tempo returns with softly rolling E♭-major chords. The right hand rises to G again, now as part of the accompaniment. Three final chords, marked ppp, bring the hands home.

    How it is joined

    1. E♭ sharpens under the melody

      The bass moves from E♭ to E♮. With G, B♭ and D♭ above it, the new note makes a diminished seventh. The melody leaps to B♭ and descends through A♮ to G; the left hand continues its bass-and-chord pattern beneath the unsettled harmony.

    2. A♭ minor starts the final climb

      The bass drops to A♭ under an A♭-minor chord, then returns to E♭ major. Above it, the tune turns around E♭ and F. Chopin next writes con forza, then stretto: the right hand climbs into octaves before the measured accompaniment stops for the cadenza.

    3. B♭ resolves beneath the falling run

      The cadenza unfolds above a held B♭ dominant seventh. As the right hand descends, its last C and D lead to E♭. The bass supplies E♭ underneath, the first tempo returns, and both hands roll softly through the tonic chord.

  2. Nocturne in E♭ major, Op. 9 No. 2Patrizia Prati, piano · 24 November 2015, Museum of Romanticism, Madrid
    5:02

    At Madrid’s Museum of Romanticism, with no audience in the room, Patrizia Prati plays Chopin’s E♭-major Nocturne in November 2015. The left hand keeps its easy tread while the tune gathers ornaments and finally takes off on its own.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Three steps beneath each beat

      The left hand places a low note, then two chords: three steps beneath each slow beat. Above it the melody rises from B♭ to G. Chopin asks for espressivo dolce, expressive and sweet.

    2. A pianist between languages

      Prati studies piano with Giancarlo Cardini, Aldo Ciccolini and András Schiff, and harpsichord with Anna Maria Pernafelli. Her work also takes her to the writing desk: she publishes on music, literature and literary translation.

    3. The bass walks down

      A new phrase starts on F, rises to G and sinks back. Underneath, the bass steps down from B♭ to A♮, then A♭. The right hand repeats E♭ softly, taking its time over the descent.

    4. The song comes back embroidered

      A quick sweep through G, B♭ and E♭ brings the melody back to its high G. This version has more twists, trills and accented little notes. The left hand resumes the familiar pattern under the returning tune.

    5. The coda keeps E♭

      The tune pares itself down to E♭, F and G. An A♭-minor chord gives its opening a sudden shadow; the bass keeps E♭ underneath. Soon the melody climbs again, marked ever more softly.

    6. The accompaniment stops walking

      The bass climbs through a B♭-dominant chord and holds it. Above, the high octaves pause, then the right hand spins a four-note figure. Senza tempo leaves this little cadenza free of the old tread.

    7. The final rocking chords

      E♭ returns in the bass beneath gently rocking chords. The piano is marked pp, then ppp. After all the right hand’s flights, the nocturne ends with three plain chords, the last held beneath a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The bass descends beneath repeated E♭s

      The bass steps down through B♭, A♮ and A♭ while the right hand repeats E♭. Over A♭, a major chord turns minor when C falls to C♭. The bass then reaches E♭; the melody falls to B♭ before leaping up an octave into the next phrase.

    2. A♭ minor beneath the last ascent

      A♭-minor chords return, now with A♭ itself deep in the bass. The right hand turns around E♭ and F before climbing under the marking con forza. High octaves take over the melody, pressing towards the B♭ dominant chord on which the cadenza will hang.

    3. A free run reaches E♭

      The final run descends over the held B♭ dominant seventh, ending with C and D. E♭ follows in both hands, resolving the chord. Tempo I restores the first pace, and the piano rolls through E♭ major, softly, before the last three chords.
