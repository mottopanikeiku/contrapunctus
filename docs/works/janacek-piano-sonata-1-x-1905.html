Leoš Janáček / IV. Fin de siècle

Piano Sonata 1. X. 1905

1905–06 · Piano

8 min

The recording

  1. II. Smrt (Death) — AdagioEric Xi Xin Liang · 2023
    7:36

    Janáček’s lament for a carpenter killed during the Brno university demonstrations begins with a broken piano phrase. It comes back again and again, gathering chords around it until the opening whisper becomes a cry.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A rest before the phrase

      Each little phrase begins after a rest. The right hand leaps from B♭ to E♭ and falls back; the left hand moves beneath it, then both settle on a held chord. Janáček marks the movement Adagio, with the quaver moving at 56.

    2. A carpenter in Brno

      In October 1905 Brno’s streets fill with rival demonstrations over a proposed Czech university. Troops are called in. The carpenter František Pavlík is fatally wounded; on 2 October, the day he dies, Janáček begins this sonata.

    3. The phrase starts again

      The first paragraph is repeated. The same small leap, the same pauses, the same brief flare of forte disturb its quiet. At the end of the repeat, a different ending takes the piano into new chords.

    4. The pianist saves a copy

      At a rehearsal on the day of the 1906 première, Janáček burns the sonata’s third movement. Later he throws the remaining manuscript into the Vltava. The pianist Ludmila Tučková keeps her copy of the first two movements; in 1924 she brings it forward, and they reach print.

    5. Quick answers below

      The melody keeps its clipped phrases, while the left hand grows restless. Quick figures answer beneath it and spread into repeated chords. Crescendos push the piano from piano through forte to fortissimo.

    6. The left hand persists

      The left hand’s rapid chords now continue through the right hand’s rests. Above them, the melody is pared down to accented fragments, sometimes only two notes. The piano has lost the breathing room of the opening.

    7. The whisper becomes a cry

      The opening phrase returns in E♭ minor, marked sforzando fortissimo. Its small leap is unchanged. Underneath it the left hand keeps moving, filling the gaps that once left the melody exposed.

    8. The last soft chord

      The returning phrases become quiet again. The last line reaches pianississimo; for the final E♭-minor chord Janáček asks for una corda, the piano’s soft pedal. The bass E♭ lies far below the closely packed notes above.

    How it is joined

    1. Back to the first rest

      The first ending dwindles to a held chord under the soft pedal. The repeat then restores the opening E♭-minor bass and the right hand’s small upward leap. The little rest before the phrase returns too.

    2. The second ending continues

      The second ending carries the piano past the repeat. Held bass notes support the clipped melody, then the left hand starts answering with quick chord figures of its own. The phrases still begin after rests, but the accompaniment increasingly fills the spaces between them.

    3. The opening returns with force

      A ritenuto checks the rapid left-hand chords. At a tempo the opening melody returns over an E♭-minor bass, marked sff. The left hand carries on with moving chords, so the familiar phrase arrives amid the motion built up in the middle.

    4. The phrases dwindle

      The right hand repeats the short falling phrases while the left hand continues its quieter chords. Janáček brings the final line down to ppp, then writes una corda over the last E♭-minor chord. A low E♭ anchors the close.
