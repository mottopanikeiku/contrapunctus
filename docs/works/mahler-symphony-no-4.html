Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Symphony No. 4

1899–1900 · Soprano and a classically sized orchestra (no trombones)

The lightest and most ironic. In the scherzo the leader plays a second violin tuned a tone high, for a scratchy “Death strikes up the dance”. The finale is a child’s vision of heaven from Des Knaben Wunderhorn.

4 recordings · 53 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Bedächtig. Nicht eilen
    15:07

    Sleigh bells rattle and the violins begin a comfortable G-major tune. Mahler’s Fourth seems to have wandered into an eighteenth-century drawing room, until its melodies start talking over one another and the trumpets sound an alarm.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Bells outside, violins within

      Flutes chatter above sleigh bells. The violins enter with a little upward flourish and settle into a G-major tune, while the lower strings pluck beneath them. Mahler marks the melody Recht gemächlich — very leisurely.

    2. Rain at Bad Aussee

      At Bad Aussee in the summer of 1899, poor weather and the resort’s music keep Mahler from composing. His holiday is nearly over when the Fourth begins to come. He works at speed, then finishes the symphony the following summer at Maiernigg.

    3. The cellos sing broadly

      The second tune belongs to the cellos, in D major. Its long notes stretch out after the violins’ little turns and scales. Mahler writes Breit gesungen — broadly sung — over a melody that sounds as though it has all afternoon.

    4. The ending comes first

      In 1892 Mahler writes Das himmlische Leben, a song about heaven’s food and music. He considers it for the Third Symphony, then makes it the end of the Fourth. These orchestral movements grow towards a song already several years old.

    5. The bells turn troublesome

      The opening flute figure returns in the minor, tangled with pieces of the violin tune. Horns make the little descending triplets suddenly forceful. A new flute melody rises from the low strings’ rumbling; it carries a hint of the finale’s song.

    6. Mahler explains the connections

      In 1911 Mahler writes to Georg Göhler: “Each of the three movements is joined thematically with the last one in a most intimate and meaningful way.” The finale’s song is already threading through this orchestra.

    7. The trumpet meets a wall

      The development’s melody reaches the trumpet in the major. Then a harsh orchestral chord stops its progress. As the tumult clears, trumpets and horns are left sounding military calls across the suddenly empty space.

    8. The violin tune returns

      The first theme comes back quietly in G major, changed in its details. The orchestra soon brightens around it, and the broad cello tune follows. Familiar melodies have survived a decidedly unfamiliar middle.

    9. A last quick farewell

      Bits of the first tune keep slipping into the quiet closing melody. They grow into the coda, which gathers itself for a lively ending. The violin tune gets the last word.

    How it is joined

    1. D major opens a longer phrase

      The first theme’s continuation accelerates towards D major, the dominant of G. The cellos then take the melody in long notes, marked Breit gesungen. The violin turns and short scales have led into a lower, more expansive singing line.

    2. The flute figure turns to minor

      The exposition settles back into G major and its quiet closing tune. The development reintroduces the opening flute figure in the minor, combining it with pieces of the first theme. The horns seize on the tune’s descending triplets.

    3. A dissonance cuts across the trumpet

      The development melody clears into the major and reaches the trumpet. A dissonant orchestral chord cuts across it. The massed winds and strings then fall away, exposing military fanfares in trumpets and horns.

    4. The fanfares leave room for violins

      The trumpet and horn calls subside. A quiet, altered version of the first theme restores G major in the strings. Its familiar phrase replaces the isolated fanfares, before cymbals announce a more jubilant continuation.

    5. The first tune interrupts its farewell

      The quiet closing melody returns in G major with small phrases from the first violin tune tucked inside it. Those fragments keep the music moving into the coda. The orchestra thins before gathering for the quick final bars.

  2. II. In gemächlicher Bewegung. Ohne Hast
    8:33

    The solo violin is tuned a whole tone high and made to sound like a rough country fiddle. Mahler gives it a lopsided dance, interrupted twice by a warmer Ländler; Death has borrowed the orchestra leader’s chair.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A fiddle tuned high

      A horn calls and the solo violin answers, its strings tuned a whole tone above their usual pitch. Mahler asks for a fiddle-like sound, played forcefully. Muted viola and cello solos stir beneath it; the dance already feels slightly off balance.

    2. Death takes the violin

      Bruno Walter supplies a name for this fiddler: “Freund Hein Strikes Up the Dance”. Freund Hein is Death, a familiar figure with an unsettling invitation. Walter passes on the title in a letter written at Mahler’s request in December 1901.

    3. A warmer dance in F

      The first trio turns to F major. Oboes and clarinets take over the dance, with held horn notes and plucked basses beneath them. The solo fiddle steps aside, and the woodwinds make the room feel much less crowded.

    4. A leaf for Arnold Rosé

      On 11 January 1902, the day before the Vienna performance, Mahler gives the violinist Arnold Rosé a manuscript leaf of this solo. In that draft the violin is tuned only a half-tone high; the printed score asks for a whole tone.

    5. The trio dies away

      The first trio stays in F major to the end. Low strings murmur, horns hold soft stopped notes, and Mahler writes morendo as the dynamic sinks to pppp before the scherzo breaks in.

    6. The fiddle grows passionate

      The scherzo returns in C minor. This time Mahler marks the solo violin leidenschaftlich — passionate. Other strings tap with the wood of their bows while a horn pushes its phrase into the foreground. The fiddler has plenty of company.

    7. Böcklin’s fiddling skeleton

      Alma Mahler later claimed that Arnold Böcklin’s Self-Portrait with Death Fiddling (1872), in which a skeleton plays into the painter’s ear, inspired this scherzo. She and Mahler had not yet met when he wrote it; he did admire Böcklin.

    8. The second trio lingers

      The woodwinds recover the gentler dance, now with more room to wander. The trio stretches beyond its first appearance, letting its major-key phrases linger before the fiddle’s music can return.

    9. Clarinets open into D major

      The trio reaches D major. Clarinets sing over harp arpeggios; the violins slide between notes while a contrabassoon reinforces the double basses. Mahler lets this country dance become extravagantly tender.

    10. An afterthought for the basses

      The contrabassoon that doubles the double basses here was an afterthought. Mahler added it in the proofs, in bars 246–51; the plate had no room for another staff, so the published score labels one line “Ctrfag. u. Cb. unisono”.

    11. The dance on tiptoe

      The opening tempo returns over plucked cellos and basses. Muted violins and violas run through their figures at ppp, while horns hold soft chords and piccolos pick out tiny ornaments. The same dance has become wonderfully light on its feet.

    How it is joined

    1. F major takes over the dance

      The C-minor scherzo gives way to the first trio in F major. A held oboe note and a playful clarinet figure emerge over horn harmony; the basses pluck the beat. Mahler eases the pace at Etwas gemächlicher, and the solo fiddle falls silent.

    2. C minor brings back the fiddle

      The trio fades and the score restores Tempo I. Muted inner strings begin moving before the solo violin re-enters in C minor, now marked leidenschaftlich. A prominent horn and col legno second violins give the return a rougher edge.

    3. Trilling woodwinds recover the Ländler

      The scherzo’s busy strings loosen their grip as the trio returns. Flutes and oboes trill over sustained horns and trumpets; harp and glockenspiel add bright touches. The familiar major-key dance now has more time to linger.

    4. Muted strings recover the opening tempo

      The enlarged trio slows and thins before Tempo I returns. Cellos and basses pluck beneath muted violin and viola figures marked ppp. Horns hold the harmony while harp and piccolos give the returning dance small, pointed accents.

  3. III. Ruhevoll (Poco adagio)
    20:13

    Cellos begin a long G-major melody above plucked bass notes, and Mahler lets it bloom into a set of variations. An oboe brings a second, sorrowing tune; near the end, the whole orchestra suddenly throws open E major.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A song low in the strings

      Divided cellos sing in G major above a gently plucked bass line. The violas hold the harmony; violins and winds wait. Mahler marks the cello melody sehr gesangvoll — very songful — and lets it unfold without hurrying.

    2. The same steps, again and again

      Under the cellos’ melody the double basses pluck the same slow steps over and over. The Mahler Foundation calls the opening theme “motionless and meditative with its passacaglia bass”.

    3. The oboe adds another line

      The melody rises into the violins. An oboe adds a quiet, independent line above it, marked zart, tender. The basses keep their plucked tread beneath music that is growing steadily more airborne.

    4. A smile on a tomb

      In 1901 Mahler described this Adagio: St Ursula presides with a smile, like the smile on the stone knights and prelates lying in churches with their hands joined. Its character, he wrote, is “solemn, blessed peace; serious, gentle gaiety”.

    5. His mother’s face

      While he wrote this movement, Mahler sometimes glimpsed his own mother’s face “smiling through her tears”: the face of a woman who had been able to solve and forgive all suffering by love.

    6. Saint Ursula smiles

      Bruno Walter offers “Saint Ursula Laughs As Well” as a possible title for this movement. He explains that the saint’s solemn face carries a smile. His letter, written at Mahler’s request in December 1901, imagines that smile beneath the music’s sorrow.

    7. The oboe’s E-minor lament

      The pace becomes much slower. An oboe begins the second theme in E minor, accompanied by bassoon and horn, with a direction to sound plaintive and deeply expressive. The violins then take over the tune and carry it towards a brass-filled climax.

    8. Flowing, then pressing

      The lament speeds into Fliessend, flowing, over plucked cellos and basses. A page later Mahler writes Etwas drängend. Leidenschaftlich: somewhat pressing, passionately. Flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons rise to fortissimo over a climbing bass.

    9. Bells up

      Oboes, clarinets, horns and trumpets raise their bells — Schalltrichter auf — for the movement’s first great outburst, over a timpani roll. Then Etwas zurückhaltend and Wieder langsam bring the slow tempo straight back.

    10. The first tune grows lighter

      Clarinets and violas bring back the opening melody in quicker notes. Cellos and double basses pluck underneath. Mahler asks for graceful motion, beginning very moderately and becoming gradually livelier as the variation unfolds.

    11. Ten days at Aussee

      Aussee, summer 1899: cold, wet weather and a rented villa within earshot of the bandstand. Discouraged, Mahler picks up a book, and the ideas begin to flow. He drafts the first three movements in about ten days, then puts the sketches in a desk drawer until the next summer.

    12. Two reeds share the lament

      The lament returns in G minor, now shared by oboe and cor anglais. Their dark lines overlap above quiet horns. The return is brief at first, then the strings take up the sorrowing tune and drive it to a larger outburst.

    13. The tragic climax

      The lament climbs to a fortissimo plateau, the strings at full force and the woodwinds answering with bells raised. It is the tragic climax of this variation, and the harmony wanders through many keys.

    14. Sinking into silence

      The lament dissolves. Zurückhaltend, molto dim., morendo: the instruments die away one by one, down to a quadruple piano, before the cellos bring back the opening tune.

    15. The melody learns to dance

      Cellos recover the opening tune over plucked violas and basses. Then the metre abruptly changes to 3/8 and the strings begin an Allegretto. Further variations grow faster still, until Mahler reins them back into the slow melody.

    16. Faster, in steps

      Mahler’s score says each change of tempo comes “just as suddenly and surprisingly as before” (ebenso plötzlich und überraschend wie vorhin). Three jumps in speed follow, the second restoring a two-beat pulse.

    17. Racing, then a sudden halt

      At Allegro molto the variation reaches full speed, with piccolo and glockenspiel sparkling above racing violins. Then, quite suddenly — ganz plötzlich — the variation’s opening tempo returns.

    18. Slow again, and hushed

      The slow opening tempo is back for two minutes: Poco adagio, then Wieder a tempo (Ruhig). Divided strings sing zart, tenderly, in long notes that stretch across the bar lines.

    19. Cheerfulness with something eerie

      At the first read-through of the symphony, on 12 October 1901, Mahler tells Natalie Bauer-Lechner that its first three movements hold “the cheerfulness of a higher world, strange to us, that has something eerie and dreadful about it for us”.

    20. E major breaks through

      After the quiet return, E major arrives in a blaze of running strings, harp and timpani. Horns and trumpets lift up a broad melody from the finale. The song of heaven is suddenly here, a movement before the soprano can sing it.

    21. Links to the finale

      Mahler scolded the critic Georg Göhler for ignoring the symphony’s thematic ties: each of the first three movements, he wrote, is joined to the last “in a most intimate and meaningful way”. This coda announces the finale’s main motif.

    22. The violins turn to harmonics

      The great brass sound has gone. Violins divide into four parts and play high harmonics above soft winds and harp. The final instruction is Gänzlich ersterbend — dying away completely. Mahler leaves only the thinnest thread of sound.

    23. Booed in Munich

      Mahler conducts the first performance in the Kaim-Saal, Munich, on 25 November 1901, with the soprano Margarethe Michalek. The audience expected another titanic symphony and found the Fourth’s innocence baffling. The performance is roundly booed.

    How it is joined

    1. An oboe opens E minor

      The G-major music slows and dies away through muted strings and held wind chords. At Viel langsamer an oboe begins the E-minor lament over bassoon and horn. The strings remove their mutes while resting, ready to take up the new tune.

    2. Plucked strings restore the first tune

      The lament’s closing phrases recede. Clarinets and violas recover the first theme over plucked cellos and basses at Anmuthig bewegt. Mahler keeps the underlying beat at first, then asks the variation to become gradually livelier.

    3. The oboe hands sorrow to cor anglais

      The first-theme variation dwindles through a solo violin phrase and quiet horns. An oboe begins the lament again; at Langsam, cor anglais joins it in G minor. The string accompaniment drops away, leaving the two reeds above soft horn chords.

    4. The cellos recover their quiet tune

      The second lament dwindles to pppp. The cellos begin the first theme at Andante, with violas and double basses plucking beneath them. Clarinets enter later, before the sudden change to the quicker, three-beat variation.

    5. A breath, then E major

      The slow theme settles into quiet, sustained chords. A marked breath separates them from the sudden E-major outburst: strings sweep up and down, harp arpeggios cross the orchestra and timpani reinforce the bass. Horns and trumpets then sound the finale’s broad melody.

  4. IV. Sehr behaglich — Das himmlische Leben
    8:40

    Heaven has a kitchen in Mahler’s Fourth: Saint Martha cooks, Peter fishes, and the saints make music. A soprano tells the story with a child’s delight, while sleigh bells and the slaughter of a lamb put a chill into the feast.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Clarinet, horn and harp

      A clarinet opens over horn, harp and muted violas and cellos. The violins and double basses wait. Mahler marks the music Sehr behaglich — very comfortable — and warns the conductor to accompany the singer with extreme discretion.

    2. A child’s view of heaven

      The soprano begins with heaven’s pleasures: no earthly commotion reaches this place. Mahler asks for a childlike, cheerful voice and explicitly rules out parody. Harp and lightly plucked strings give the singer plenty of room to tell the story.

    3. Angels who dance and hop

      The music quickens at Fliessend, flowing, for “We lead an angelic life”, and the rhythm livens further for “we dance and leap, we hop and sing”. Even in heaven there is a sprightly step.

    4. The bells announce a butcher

      Sleigh bells break into the song. The next verse turns to E minor and follows a lamb to slaughter: John leads it, Herod waits, and Saint Luke kills the ox. The child describes heaven’s dinner without a flicker of horror.

    5. Bleating and bellowing

      Over the second verse’s new melody, an oboe seems to bleat like a sheep and a bass clarinet to bellow like an ox, as the text turns to the slaughter of lamb and ox. The sleigh bells have stopped.

    6. A garden without prices

      Another burst of bells ushers in the returning first tune. Herbs and vegetables grow in heaven’s garden; fruit comes free. Deer and hares run straight into the kitchen, and Saint Martha must be the cook. The orchestra darts about with the animals.

    7. A song from Hamburg

      Mahler finishes the voice-and-piano version of Das himmlische Leben in February 1892 and orchestrates it the following month. Years later he builds the Fourth Symphony around this ending. The child’s song is the oldest music in the room.

    8. E major after the bells

      The final sleigh-bell interruption fades. Flute and cor anglais introduce a new melody in E major above harp triplets and muted strings. The soprano waits while the orchestra discovers the quiet sound in which the symphony will end.

    9. Mahler at the Welte-Mignon

      On 9 November 1905 Mahler records a piano version of this finale in Leipzig, on the Welte-Mignon reproducing piano. The Mahler Foundation lists it first among the symphony’s historical recordings.

    10. Music beyond the earth

      The soprano takes up the E-major tune. Heaven’s music surpasses anything on earth: eleven thousand virgins dance, and Saint Ursula laughs. The voice stays gentle over the harp’s steady triplets; there is no grand final chorus.

    11. The saint who smiled in the Adagio

      At “Sanct Ursula selbst dazu lacht” — Saint Ursula herself laughs at it — the music slows and holds a pause before the next phrase. In the Adagio Mahler had pictured the saint presiding with a smile.

    12. Cecilia and her companions

      Cecilia and her relations become splendid court musicians. Their angelic voices bring everything to life with joy. Mahler keeps the accompaniment small: rocking harp notes, soft strings and wind phrases around the soprano’s final words.

    13. The harp takes its time

      After the soprano finishes, cor anglais and bass clarinet fade over low strings. The harp continues alone between the held notes. Mahler writes sich Zeit lassen — take time — before the last E-major chord slips away.

    How it is joined

    1. The soprano enters the small orchestra

      The clarinet-led prelude settles into G major. The soprano begins above harp and plucked strings, with soft wind phrases around her. Mahler asks for a cheerful, childlike expression and leaves the accompaniment light enough for the words to carry.

    2. Bells disturb the G-major refrain

      The first verse closes like a quiet hymn over muted strings and horns. Sleigh bells and high winds interrupt, with strings striking col legno. The soprano then begins a new melody in E minor for the verse about the lamb’s slaughter.

    3. The first song returns after bells

      The second verse’s hymn-like close gives way to another sleigh-bell interruption. The soprano recovers the first verse’s melody for the garden stanza, with winds and strings returning to its lighter accompaniment. Faster figures soon animate the running animals and swimming fish.

    4. The last rattle of sleigh bells

      The verse about Saint Martha ends in a broad refrain. Sleigh bells, triangle, winds and strings break in once more. This interlude quickly dwindles, clearing the orchestra for a new key and a softer tune.

    5. Flute and cor anglais find E major

      The bells and forceful wind figures die away. E major settles over a harp accompaniment in triplets, and flute and cor anglais begin the last tune. Muted strings join beneath a direction to remain very tender and mysterious to the end.

    6. The singer takes the E-major tune

      The soprano enters the E-major music already established by the winds. Harp triplets continue beneath muted strings and soft wind chords. The orchestral melody becomes the song of heaven’s musicians without another bell interruption.

    7. Low winds answer the last words

      The soprano finishes over E-major harmony. Cor anglais and bass clarinet continue the line, then fade with the horns. Harp notes slow above sustained cellos and basses; the score asks the harpist to take time before the final chord.
