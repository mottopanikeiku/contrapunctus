Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Symphony No. 4

1899–1900 · Soprano and a classically sized orchestra (no trombones)

The lightest and most ironic of the symphonies. In the scherzo the leader plays a second violin tuned a tone high, for a scratchy “Death strikes up the dance”. The finale is a child’s vision of heaven from Des Knaben Wunderhorn.

4 recordings · 53 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Bedächtig. Nicht eilen
    15:07

    The first movement of Mahler’s Symphony No. 4, marked “Bedächtig. Nicht eilen”, opens with sleigh bells, and the violins then begin a tune in G major. Later its melodies overlap and the trumpets play a fanfare.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, flutes and sleigh bells

      Flutes play above sleigh bells. The violins enter with a short upward figure and continue into a tune in G major, while the lower strings play pizzicato beneath them. Mahler marks the melody Recht gemächlich, very leisurely.

    2. Bad Aussee, summer 1899

      At Bad Aussee in the summer of 1899, poor weather and the resort’s music kept Mahler from composing. He began the Fourth near the end of his holiday and worked fast. He finished the symphony at Maiernigg in August 1900.

    3. Second theme, cellos

      The cellos play the second theme, in D major. Its long notes follow the short turns and scales of the violins. Mahler marks the melody Breit gesungen (broadly sung).

    4. Origin of the finale, 1892

      In 1892 Mahler wrote Das himmlische Leben, a song about heaven’s food and music, in piano and orchestral versions dated February and March. He considered it for the Third Symphony, then made it the finale of the Fourth, so the song was several years older than the movements that come before it.

    5. Development, minor-mode opening

      The opening flute figure returns in the minor, combined with fragments of the violin tune. The horns play the small descending triplets with sudden force. Over a low rumble in the strings a new flute melody rises, with a hint of the song from the finale.

    6. Letter to Georg Göhler, 1911

      In 1911 Mahler wrote to Georg Göhler: “Each of the three movements is joined thematically with the last one in a most intimate and meaningful way.”

    7. Development, trumpet melody and fanfares

      In the development the melody passes to the trumpet in the major. A dissonant chord from the orchestra cuts it off. Trumpets and horns then play military fanfares.

    8. Recapitulation, first theme

      The first theme returns quietly in G major, with its details changed. A jubilant continuation follows, and then the broad cello tune returns.

    9. Closing theme and coda

      Fragments of the first theme enter the quiet closing theme. They grow into the coda and its lively conclusion. The violin tune ends the movement.

    Joins

    1. Transition to D major and cello theme

      The continuation of the first theme, with turns and short scales in the violins, accelerates towards D major, the dominant of G. The cellos then take the second theme in long notes in a lower register, marked Breit gesungen.

    2. The flute figure turns to minor

      The exposition returns to G major and its quiet closing tune. The development brings back the opening flute figure in the minor and combines it with pieces of the first theme. The horns take up the tune’s descending triplets.

    3. Dissonant chord cuts across the trumpet

      The development melody moves into the major and passes to the trumpet. A dissonant chord in the full orchestra cuts across it. The massed winds and strings then drop out, leaving military fanfares in the trumpets and horns.

    4. From the fanfares to the recapitulation

      The trumpet and horn calls die away. The strings play a quiet, altered version of the first theme, and the key returns to G major. The familiar phrase of the theme takes the place of the isolated fanfares, and the cymbals then mark the start of a more jubilant continuation.

    5. Closing theme and first-theme fragments into coda

      The quiet closing melody returns in G major, and small phrases from the first violin tune appear within it. These fragments carry the music into the coda. The orchestral texture thins, then the full orchestra returns for the quick final bars.

  2. II. In gemächlicher Bewegung. Ohne Hast
    8:33

    The second movement of Mahler’s Symphony No. 4, marked In gemächlicher Bewegung. Ohne Hast. The solo violin is tuned a whole tone high to sound like a rough country fiddle and plays a lopsided dance, which a warmer Ländler interrupts twice.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Solo violin tuned a tone high

      After the horn opening, the solo violin enters with its strings tuned a whole tone above their usual pitch. Mahler marked it to sound like a fiddle, played forcefully. Muted solo viola and cello play beneath it.

    2. Bruno Walter’s title, 1901

      Bruno Walter gave this fiddler a name: “Freund Hein Strikes Up the Dance”. Freund Hein is Death. Walter passed on the title in a letter to Ludwig Schiedermair of 5 December 1901, written at Mahler’s request.

    3. First trio in F major

      The first trio is in F major. Oboes and clarinets take over the dance, over held horn notes and pizzicato basses. The solo violin rests.

    4. Manuscript leaf for Arnold Rosé, 1902

      On 11 January 1902, the day before the Vienna performance, Mahler gave the violinist Arnold Rosé a manuscript leaf of this solo. In that draft the solo violin is tuned a half-tone high. The printed score asks for a whole tone.

    5. End of the first trio

      The first trio stays in F major to the end. Low strings play softly and horns hold pppp-bound stopped notes; Mahler marks morendo as the dynamic falls to pppp before the scherzo returns.

    6. Return of the scherzo, C minor

      At rehearsal 5 (Tempo I) the scherzo returns in C minor. This time Mahler marks the solo violin leidenschaftlich (passionate). The second violins play col legno, with the wood of the bow, and the first horn is marked sehr hervortretend, very prominent.

    7. Alma Mahler’s claim about Böcklin

      Alma Mahler later claimed that Arnold Böcklin’s Self-Portrait with Death Fiddling (1872), in which a skeleton plays into the painter’s ear, inspired this scherzo. She and Mahler had not yet met when he wrote it. He did admire Böcklin.

    8. Second trio

      The woodwinds return to the trio’s dance. The trio is longer than at its first appearance, with lyrical digressions in its major-key phrases, before the music of the solo violin returns.

    9. Second trio in D major

      The trio moves to D major. The clarinets play the melody over harp arpeggios, the violins play with portamento, and a contrabassoon doubles the double basses.

    10. Contrabassoon added in proof

      The contrabassoon doubling the double basses in bars 246–51 was not in Mahler’s original scoring. He added it in the proofs. The plate had no room for another staff, so the published score labels one line “Ctrfag. u. Cb. unisono”.

    11. Return of Tempo I

      The opening tempo returns over pizzicato cellos and basses. Muted violins and violas play their figures at ppp, horns hold soft chords and piccolos add small ornaments.

    Joins

    1. Into the first trio in F major

      The C-minor scherzo gives way to the first trio, in F major. The oboe holds a note and the clarinet plays a figure over sustained horn harmony, and the basses play the beat pizzicato. At Etwas gemächlicher Mahler slows the tempo, and the solo violin stops playing.

    2. Return of the solo violin in C minor

      The trio dies away and the score returns to Tempo I. Muted inner strings start moving first. The solo violin then re-enters in C minor, now marked leidenschaftlich. A prominent horn part and second violins playing col legno go with the return.

    3. Trilling woodwinds bring back the Ländler

      The scherzo’s busy string writing gives way as the trio returns. Flutes and oboes trill over sustained horns and trumpets, and harp and glockenspiel add bright touches. The familiar major-key dance comes back in expanded form and is given more time than before.

    4. Return to Tempo I with muted strings

      The enlarged trio slows and thins before Tempo I returns. Cellos and basses play pizzicato beneath figures in the muted violins and violas, marked ppp. Horns hold the harmony, and harp and piccolos add short accents to the returning dance.

  3. III. Ruhevoll (Poco adagio)
    20:13

    The third movement of Mahler’s Symphony No. 4, Ruhevoll (Poco adagio), is a set of variations on a long G-major melody, which the cellos play above plucked bass notes. An oboe introduces a second tune, and near the end the full orchestra moves suddenly to E major.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Cello melody in G major

      Divided cellos play a melody in G major above a pizzicato bass line. The violas hold the harmony, and the violins and winds rest. Mahler marks the cello melody sehr gesangvoll (very songful).

    2. Opening theme, pizzicato basses

      Under the cellos’ melody the double basses play the same slow steps pizzicato, over and over. The Mahler Foundation describes the opening theme as “motionless and meditative with its passacaglia bass”.

    3. Oboe line above the violins

      The melody rises into the violins. Above it an oboe plays an independent line, marked zart, tender. The basses continue pizzicato beneath.

    4. Mahler’s description, 1901

      In 1901 Mahler described this Adagio. St Ursula presides over it with a smile, like the smile on the stone knights and prelates lying in churches with their hands joined. Its character, he wrote, is “solemn, blessed peace; serious, gentle gaiety”.

    5. Mahler’s recollection of his mother

      While he wrote this movement, Mahler sometimes glimpsed his own mother’s face “smiling through her tears”. It was, he recalled, the face of a woman who had been able to solve and forgive all suffering by love.

    6. Bruno Walter’s letter, December 1901

      Bruno Walter proposed “Saint Ursula Laughs As Well” as a possible title for this movement, explaining that the saint’s solemn face carried a smile. He wrote the letter at Mahler’s request in December 1901.

    7. Second theme, oboe, E minor

      At rehearsal 2 the tempo becomes much slower (Viel langsamer). An oboe plays the second theme in E minor, marked klagend, sehr ausdrucksvoll, with bassoon and horn accompanying. The violins then take over the tune and lead to a climax with brass.

    8. Fliessend, then Etwas drängend

      The tempo quickens to Fliessend (flowing) over pizzicato cellos and basses. A page later Mahler marks Etwas drängend. Leidenschaftlich (somewhat pressing, passionately). Flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons rise to fortissimo over a rising bass.

    9. Bells raised, first outburst

      Oboes, clarinets, horns and trumpets raise their bells (Schalltrichter auf) for the movement’s first great outburst, over a timpani roll. Etwas zurückhaltend and Wieder langsam then restore the slow tempo at once.

    10. Opening melody, clarinets and violas

      Clarinets and violas play the opening melody again in quicker notes, with pizzicato cellos and double basses underneath. Mahler marks the passage Anmuthig bewegt (graceful motion). It begins very moderately and gets gradually faster.

    11. Aussee, summer 1899

      In summer 1899 Mahler rented a villa at Aussee within earshot of the bandstand; it was cold and wet. Discouraged, he picked up a book, and ideas followed. He drafted the first three movements in about ten days and left the sketches in a desk drawer until the next summer.

    12. Return of the lament in G minor

      The lament returns in G minor, shared by oboe and cor anglais. Their lines overlap above quiet horns. The return is brief at first; the strings then take up the tune and carry it to a larger outburst.

    13. Climax at rehearsal 7

      The lament rises to a sustained fortissimo, with the strings at full force and the woodwinds playing with bells raised. Basinger calls this the tragic climax of the variation. The harmony modulates through many keys.

    14. Diminuendo to pppp

      The music is marked Zurückhaltend, molto dim. and morendo. The instruments drop out one by one, and the timpani and strings reach pppp. Then the cellos bring back the opening tune.

    15. Allegretto in 3/8 and variations

      The cellos play the opening tune again over pizzicato violas and basses. The metre then changes suddenly to 3/8 and the strings begin an Allegretto. The variations that follow grow faster until Mahler returns to the slow melody.

    16. Allegro subito, rehearsal 10

      Mahler’s score says each change of tempo comes “just as suddenly and surprisingly as before” (ebenso plötzlich und überraschend wie vorhin). Three increases in speed follow. The second restores a two-beat pulse.

    17. Allegro molto, then Andante subito

      At Allegro molto the variation reaches full speed, with piccolo and glockenspiel above fast violins. The opening tempo of the variation then returns, ganz plötzlich.

    18. Return of the slow tempo

      The slow opening tempo returns for two minutes, marked Poco adagio and then Wieder a tempo (Ruhig). Divided strings play zart (tenderly) in long notes held across the bar lines.

    19. First read-through, 12 October 1901

      At the first read-through of the symphony, on 12 October 1901, Mahler told Natalie Bauer-Lechner that its first three movements held “the cheerfulness of a higher world, strange to us, that has something eerie and dreadful about it for us”.

    20. E major outburst, rehearsal 12

      After the quiet return, the music moves to E major, with running strings, harp and timpani. Horns and trumpets play a broad melody from the finale, a movement before the soprano sings it.

    21. Letter to Georg Göhler

      Mahler wrote to the critic Georg Göhler, faulting him for overlooking the symphony’s thematic connections. Each of the first three movements, he said, is joined to the last “in a most intimate and meaningful way”. The coda of this movement announces the principal motif of the finale.

    22. Closing bars, violin harmonics

      The full brass has stopped. Violins divide into four parts and play high harmonics above soft winds and harp. The final marking is Gänzlich ersterbend — dying away completely.

    23. First performance, Munich, 1901

      Mahler conducted the first performance in Munich on 25 November 1901, with the soprano Margarethe Michalek. The audience had expected another titanic symphony and was baffled by the Fourth’s innocence. The performance was roundly booed.

    Joins

    1. Oboe entry in E minor

      The G-major music slows and dies away, marked morendo, in muted strings and held wind chords. At rehearsal 2, Viel langsamer, an oboe begins an E-minor melody over bassoon and horn. The strings rest and are marked Dämpfer ab to remove their mutes.

    2. Plucked strings bring back the first theme

      The closing phrases of the lament die away. At rehearsal 4, marked Anmuthig bewegt, clarinets and violas return to the first theme over plucked cellos and basses. Mahler kept the underlying beat at first, then directed that the variation become gradually livelier.

    3. Oboe and cor anglais in G minor

      The variation of the first theme closes with a phrase for solo violin over quiet horns. At rehearsal 6 an oboe pickup starts the lament again. At Langsam the cor anglais, marked klagend, joins the oboe in G minor. The strings stop, and the two reeds play over soft horn chords.

    4. Cellos return to the first theme

      The second lament falls away to pppp. The cellos begin the first theme at Andante, and the violas and double basses play pizzicato beneath them. The clarinets enter later, before the abrupt change to the faster variation in three time.

    5. Luftpause, then E major

      The slow theme ends on soft, sustained chords. A Luftpause before rehearsal 12 separates them from the sudden E-major outburst, marked by a change of key signature. Strings run up and down, harp arpeggios cross the orchestra and timpani reinforce the bass. Horns and trumpets then play the finale’s broad melody, marked Pesante.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  4. IV. Sehr behaglich — Das himmlische Leben
    8:40

    The fourth movement of Mahler’s Fourth Symphony, marked Sehr behaglich, is a setting of ‘Das himmlische Leben’ for soprano, describing a heaven where Saint Martha cooks, Peter fishes and the saints make music. The orchestra includes sleigh bells, and the text describes the slaughter of a lamb.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening: clarinet, horn and harp

      A clarinet opens over horn, harp and muted violas and cellos. The violins and double basses rest. Mahler marked the music Sehr behaglich (very comfortable) and told the conductor to accompany the singer with extreme discretion.

    2. Opening vocal entry, soprano

      The soprano begins with the pleasures of heaven, where no earthly commotion reaches. At her entry Mahler marked the part Mit kindlich heiterem Ausdruck; durchaus ohne Parodie, asking for a childlike, cheerful voice and ruling out parody. Harp and lightly plucked strings accompany her.

    3. Fliessend, “We lead an angelic life”

      The tempo quickens at Fliessend (flowing) for “We lead an angelic life”. The rhythm livens further at “we dance and leap, we hop and sing”.

    4. Sleigh bells and second stanza

      The sleigh bells return between verses. The second stanza moves to E minor, and the child describes the food of heaven: John leads out a lamb, Herod waits for it, and Saint Luke slaughters the ox.

    5. Second verse, oboe and bass clarinet

      The second verse has a new melody. As the text turns to the slaughter of lamb and ox, an oboe imitates a sheep’s bleat and a bass clarinet an ox’s bellow. The sleigh bells have stopped.

    6. Third stanza, return of first melody

      Another passage for bells leads to the return of the first-verse melody. In the third stanza, herbs and vegetables grow in heaven’s garden and fruit is free. Deer and hares run into the kitchen, Saint Martha is to be the cook, and the orchestral accompaniment quickens.

    7. Composition of the song, 1892

      Mahler finished the voice-and-piano version of Das himmlische Leben on 10 February 1892 and completed the orchestral version on 12 March. Years later he built the Fourth Symphony around it as the finale.

    8. Final section in E major

      The last sleigh-bell interruption fades. At rehearsal 12 (Tempo I, Sehr zart und geheimnisvoll bis zum Schluss), flute and cor anglais play a new melody in E major above harp triplets and muted strings, while the soprano rests. The symphony ends in E major.

    9. Mahler’s recording, Leipzig, 1905

      On 9 November 1905 in Leipzig, Mahler recorded a piano version of this finale on the Welte-Mignon reproducing piano. The Mahler Foundation lists it first among the symphony’s historical recordings.

    10. Fourth stanza, soprano

      The soprano takes up the E-major tune. The text tells of heavenly music that surpasses any on earth, with eleven thousand virgins dancing and Saint Ursula laughing. The voice sings over steady triplets in the harp.

    11. Saint Ursula, rehearsal 14

      At “Sanct Ursula selbst dazu lacht” (Saint Ursula herself laughs at it), the music slows and holds a pause before the next phrase. In 1901 Mahler described the saint as presiding over the Adagio with a smile.

    12. Cecilia and her relations

      The poem names Cecilia and her relations as splendid court musicians, whose angelic voices bring everything to life with joy. Mahler keeps the accompaniment small. Rocking harp notes, soft strings and wind phrases surround the soprano’s final words.

    13. Closing bars, harp

      After the soprano finishes, cor anglais and bass clarinet fade over low strings. The harp plays alone between the held notes. Mahler marks it sich Zeit lassen (take time) before the last chord in E major.

    Joins

    1. First soprano entry after the prelude

      The prelude, led by the clarinet, closes in G major. The soprano enters above harp and plucked strings, and soft wind phrases surround the vocal line. Mahler asked for a cheerful, childlike expression and kept the accompaniment light.

    2. Sleigh bells follow the G-major refrain

      The first verse ends with a soft, hymn-like refrain over muted strings and horns. Sleigh bells and high woodwind break in, and the strings play col legno. The soprano then begins a new melody in E minor for the verse about the slaughter of the lamb.

    3. The first melody returns after the bells

      The hymn-like close of the second verse is followed by another sleigh-bell interruption. The soprano then sings the melody of the first verse again for the garden stanza, and winds and strings return to its lighter accompaniment. Faster figures soon follow for the lines about running animals and swimming fish.

    4. Final sleigh-bell interlude

      The verse about Saint Martha ends in a broad refrain. Sleigh bells, triangle, winds and strings enter once more. The interlude is short and the scoring thins out before rehearsal 12, where the music moves to a new key and a softer tune.

    5. E major at rehearsal 12

      The bells and forceful wind figures die away. At rehearsal 12 the music moves to E major. The harp plays a triplet accompaniment, and flute and cor anglais begin the last tune. Muted strings join under the marking Sehr zart und geheimnisvoll bis zum Schluss (very tender and mysterious to the end).

    6. The soprano takes the E-major tune

      The soprano enters with the E-major music the winds have already played. Harp triplets continue beneath muted strings and soft wind chords. The voice takes over the orchestral melody for the song of heaven’s musicians, and the bells do not interrupt again.

    7. Instrumental postlude after the soprano’s close

      The soprano finishes over E-major harmony. Cor anglais and bass clarinet continue the line, then fade out with the horns. Above sustained cellos and basses the harp notes slow down. On printed p. 353 of the full score the harpist is marked sich Zeit lassen before the final chord.
