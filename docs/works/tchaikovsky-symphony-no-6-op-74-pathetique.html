Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 6, Op. 74 “Pathétique”

1893 · Orchestra

3 recordings · 36 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Adagio — Allegro non troppoMusopen Symphony
    17:31

    A bassoon begins at the bottom of its range; the violins eventually answer with one of Tchaikovsky’s great love songs. Between them lies a movement capable of turning six pianissimos into an orchestral explosion.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Bassoon in the depths

      A bassoon starts low, above double basses that move down by semitones. Its little four-note phrase climbs, tries again higher, then passes to the violas. The symphony begins with instruments usually kept beneath the tune.

    2. The violas hurry

      The violas take the bassoon’s phrase into the Allegro, adding quick descending notes and playing it short and detached. Second violas and cellos accompany them.

    3. Four days at the desk

      In February 1893 Tchaikovsky tells his nephew Vladimir Davydov that the first movement’s sketches are ready after less than four days. “You can’t imagine how blissful I feel,” he writes, “in the conviction that my time is not yet passed, and to work is still possible.”

    4. The strings sing

      Muted violins and cellos open the D-major melody. Tchaikovsky asks for tenderness, singing tone and breadth: teneramente, molto cantabile, con espansione. After all those short viola notes, the tune seems to take an enormous breath.

    5. A secret for Davydov

      Tchaikovsky tells Davydov that the symphony has a programme which will remain an enigma: “Let them guess.” He also admits to weeping while composing it in his head on his travels. The finished score bears Davydov’s name as its dedicatee.

    6. Six pianissimos, then fortissimo

      The exposition ends with a bassoon line marked pppppp. The full orchestra breaks into that near-silence, and fast strings tear through pieces of the earlier tune. Tchaikovsky makes the join almost physically startling.

    7. A funeral chant

      The brass carry a fragment of the Orthodox funeral service into the first movement. Its words ask Christ to give peace to the soul of his servant. In the score, the quotation occupies just four bars.

    8. The song comes home

      The broad string melody returns in B major. The tune that first appeared in D now sings in the major version of the symphony’s home key, after the trombones and racing strings have spent their fury.

    9. Plucked steps downwards

      The closing wind and brass phrases rest on descending pizzicato strings. B major holds, and the movement ends quietly, its last steps plucked instead of bowed.

    How it is joined

    1. The bassoon’s tune becomes a viola tune

      At Allegro non troppo, the first violas reshape the bassoon phrase in B minor. Second violas and cellos accompany them; extra semiquavers and staccato dots turn the slow solo into a quick string tune.

    2. A pause before the strings sing

      The quick music slows, and an ascending arpeggio passes from cellos to violas. After a pause, muted violins and cellos begin the D-major Andante. The instruction is teneramente, molto cantabile, con espansione: tenderly, very singing, with breadth.

    3. The orchestra breaks the silence

      A descending clarinet solo passes its last notes to the bassoon, marked pppppp. The full orchestra answers fortissimo. A half-diminished chord sends the harmony towards D minor, and the strings launch into the development.

    4. B major after the trombones

      The trombones’ climax subsides through bass and brass phrases. The strings recover the broad Andante tune in B major, changing the home key’s D♮ to D♯. Its long notes replace the preceding rush of semiquavers.

    5. B major on plucked strings

      The returning melody quietens. Winds and brass carry the last phrases over pizzicato strings descending through the B-major close. The bowed song ends with small plucked steps beneath it.

  2. II. Allegro con graziaMusopen Symphony
    8:16

    The cellos dance in five beats, two followed by three, with the ease of a waltz. A B-minor middle brings a repeated timpani note beneath the melody.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Cellos dance in five

      The cellos sing a D-major tune above plucked strings. Each bar holds five beats, grouped as two and three. Clarinets and bassoons answer with little falling phrases; the horns fill the gaps.

    2. The last page of sketches

      At the end of this movement’s sketches, Tchaikovsky writes: “Today 24 March finished the rough sketches completely!!!” It is 1893, and he has already drafted the other three movements. The gentle dance is the last piece of the symphony to fall into place.

    3. The timpani stay on D

      The middle turns to B minor. Flute, first violins and cellos carry a descending melody while timpani and double basses repeat D beneath it. Tchaikovsky’s marking asks for sweetness and a plaintive voice: con dolcezza e flebile.

    4. D major again

      The opening dance returns in D major. Its tune still fits five beats with extraordinary ease, and the plucked accompaniment puts spring back beneath the long notes.

    5. Woodwinds on the way down

      Descending woodwind scales lead into the coda. The middle section’s falling phrases return to the D-major dance, softened for the close.

    How it is joined

    1. Five beats over a single note

      The D-major dance turns towards B minor. Timpani and double basses settle on repeated D, while flutes, first violins and cellos descend above them. The five-beat bar stays intact; the new marking, con dolcezza e flebile, asks for sweetness and plaintiveness.

    2. The dance regains D major

      The repeated bass note releases its hold, and the opening tune returns in D major with its plucked string accompaniment. The melody stretches across the same two-plus-three bar that carried the B-minor middle.

    3. Falling scales soften the close

      Woodwinds descend in scales as the returning dance quietens. The coda recalls the middle section’s falling phrases within the D-major close; the five-beat accompaniment carries on beneath them.

  3. III. Allegro molto vivaceMusopen Symphony
    9:53

    Racing strings give a march room to grow, from a quiet clarinet tune to a G-major finish with bass drum and cymbals. Tchaikovsky puts this apparently conclusive celebration one movement before the symphony’s slow lament.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Strings on the run

      The strings race in groups of three, with quick woodwind replies above them. A march begins to appear among the running notes, little scraps of a tune that will eventually command the whole orchestra.

    2. Clarinets claim the march

      The clarinets play the march in full, quietly and in E major, while the strings keep moving underneath. The tune acquires its boots before it acquires its drums.

    3. Back to the quick strings

      The opening scherzo returns. Running string figures and woodwind scales resume their exchanges, with the complete march now lodged in the memory.

    4. D holds the door

      The preparation rests on D, the dominant of G major. The orchestra gathers around that held note before the march returns in the movement’s home key.

    5. The march gets its drums

      The march returns in G major, now with bass drum and cymbals. The same tune that first belonged to quiet clarinets fills the orchestra.

    6. An ending planned first

      In his February 1893 sketches, Tchaikovsky reminds himself that the first job after returning from a trip is to compose this ending. He wants the G-major march “in a solemnly triumphant manner”.

    7. Four octaves down

      The ending sends scales plunging through four octaves in the violins and then the low brass. They land on G major with the force of a finale. One movement remains: Adagio lamentoso.

    How it is joined

    1. The clarinets finish the tune

      Fragments of the march collect into a complete E-major tune for unison clarinets. The strings continue their running accompaniment beneath its longer notes. The march emerges quietly from music already in motion.

    2. Two bars back to the scherzo

      A two-bar link takes the march back to the opening scherzo. The strings recover their quick groups of three, and wind replies break across them. Tchaikovsky moves straight into the return without a separate development section.

    3. The bass holds D

      The returning scherzo reaches a pedal on D. Holding this dominant beneath the continuing figures prepares G major, the key of the march’s final statement.

    4. From D to the G-major march

      The D pedal leads to the march in G major. Strings and winds broaden the tune, and bass drum and cymbals join the returning march. Its first quiet clarinet statement now has a full orchestral answer.

    5. Scales drive into the final chords

      The march’s closing phrases send descending scales through the violins and then the trombones and tuba. Their four-octave plunge lands on G major; brass and percussion reinforce the final chords.
