Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 6, Op. 74 “Pathétique”

1893 · Orchestra

3 recordings · 36 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Adagio — Allegro non troppoMusopen Symphony
    17:31

    The first movement, Adagio — Allegro non troppo, of Tchaikovsky’s Symphony No. 6 in B minor, Op. 74, “Pathétique”, opens with a bassoon at the bottom of its range, and the violins later play a long lyrical melody. In this movement, a passage marked with six pianissimos is followed by an orchestral explosion.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, solo bassoon

      A bassoon begins low, above double basses that descend by semitones. Its four-note phrase rises, is repeated higher, then passes to the violas.

    2. Allegro non troppo, violas

      The violas take the bassoon’s phrase into the Allegro, adding quick descending notes and playing it short and detached. Second violas and cellos accompany them.

    3. Letter to Vladimir Davydov, 1893

      In February 1893 Tchaikovsky told his nephew Vladimir Davydov that he had finished the sketches for the first movement in less than four days. “You can’t imagine how blissful I feel,” he wrote, “in the conviction that my time is not yet passed, and to work is still possible.”

    4. Second subject, muted strings

      Muted violins and cellos play the D-major melody after the short viola notes. Tchaikovsky marked it teneramente, molto cantabile, con espansione.

    5. Letter to Vladimir Davydov, 1893

      In a letter of 11/23 February 1893, Tchaikovsky told Vladimir Davydov that the symphony had a programme which would remain an enigma: “Let them guess.” He also said he had wept while composing it in his head on his travels. The autograph score bears a dedication to Davydov.

    6. Letter to Modest Tchaikovsky, 1893

      On 3 August 1893, scoring the symphony, Tchaikovsky wrote to his brother Modest: “Today I spent the whole day sitting over two pages — and nothing came out as I wanted it to.” Twenty years earlier he had gone full steam ahead without thinking; now he was timid and unsure of himself.

    7. End of exposition, bassoon

      The exposition ends with a bassoon line marked pppppp. The development opens with the full orchestra at fortissimo, and fast strings play fragments of the earlier theme.

    8. Orthodox Requiem quotation, brass

      In bars 202–205 of the first movement the brass play a fragment of the Orthodox funeral service. Its words ask Christ to give peace to the soul of his servant.

    9. Balanchine on the first movement

      George Balanchine, who made a ballet to the symphony’s finale, called this first movement “a blizzard”. In the development, fast strings play fragments of the earlier themes, and the brass, led by trombones and tuba, build the music in wider waves.

    10. Return of the second subject, B major

      The broad string melody returns in B major. First heard in D, it now comes back in the major of the symphony’s home key, after the passage for trombones and fast strings.

    11. Coda in B major

      The closing phrases in the wind and brass sit over descending pizzicato strings. The coda stays in B major to the end of the movement.

    12. The title “Pathétique”, 1893

      Modest Tchaikovsky later claimed that he thought of the title the day after the première. Weeks earlier, on 2 October 1893, the publisher Jurgenson had already written: “What is to be done with pathétique and W. Davidoff or Dawidow?” The dedicatee is Davydov, Tchaikovsky’s nephew.

    Joins

    1. Bassoon theme taken over by violas

      At Allegro non troppo the first violas play the bassoon phrase in B minor in a new rhythm, accompanied by the second violas and cellos. Added semiquavers and staccato dots turn the slow solo into a quick string theme.

    2. Pause before the D-major Andante

      The quick music slows, and an ascending arpeggio passes from cellos to violas. After a pause, muted violins and cellos begin the D-major Andante, marked teneramente, molto cantabile, con espansione (tenderly, very singing, with breadth).

    3. Clarinet solo into the development

      A solo clarinet plays a descending line, and its last notes pass to the bassoon, marked pppppp. The full orchestra then enters fortissimo. A half-diminished chord moves the harmony towards D minor, and the strings begin the development.

    4. B major after the trombones

      After the trombones’ climax, phrases in the bass and brass bring the dynamic down. The strings then play the broad Andante tune again in B major, so the D♮ of the home key becomes D♯. The tune moves in long notes, following the semiquavers of the previous passage.

    5. B major over pizzicato strings

      The returning melody grows quieter. Winds and brass play its last phrases while the strings, playing pizzicato, descend through the close in B major.

  2. II. Allegro con graziaMusopen Symphony
    8:16

    The second movement, Allegro con grazia, of the Symphony No. 6 in B minor, Op. 74 “Pathétique”, opens with a waltz-like melody for the cellos in five beats, grouped two plus three. In the B-minor middle section the timpani repeat a single note beneath the melody.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Cello theme in five-beat bars

      The cellos play a D-major tune above plucked strings. Each bar holds five beats, grouped as two and three. Clarinets and bassoons follow with short falling phrases, and the horns fill the gaps.

    2. Four-hand piano arrangement, 1893

      In August 1893, while he was still scoring the symphony, Tchaikovsky made his own arrangement of it for piano four hands. Jurgenson published the piano duet in November 1893 and the full score in February 1894.

    3. End of the sketches, 1893

      At the end of this movement’s sketches Tchaikovsky wrote: “Today 24 March finished the rough sketches completely!!!” The year was 1893, and he had already drafted the other three movements. The second movement was the last part of the symphony to be sketched.

    4. Middle section in B minor

      The middle section is in B minor. Flute, first violins and cellos play a descending melody while timpani and double basses repeat D beneath it. Tchaikovsky marked the passage con dolcezza e flebile, asking for sweetness and a plaintive tone.

    5. Return of the opening dance

      The opening dance returns in D major. The tune is again in five beats to the bar, and plucked strings accompany the long notes.

    6. Descending woodwind scales and coda

      Descending woodwind scales lead into the coda. The falling phrases of the middle section return in the D-major dance, softened for the close.

    Joins

    1. Five beats over a single note

      The D-major dance moves towards B minor. Timpani and double basses play a repeated D, while flutes, first violins and cellos descend above them. The five-beat bar continues. The new marking, con dolcezza e flebile, calls for sweetness and plaintiveness.

    2. The dance returns in D major

      The repeated bass note stops, and the opening tune returns in D major with its plucked string accompaniment. The melody runs across the same two-plus-three bar used in the B-minor middle section.

    3. Falling scales in the coda

      The returning dance grows quieter as the woodwinds descend in scales. In the coda the falling phrases of the middle section return within the D-major close, and the five-beat accompaniment continues beneath them.

  3. III. Allegro molto vivaceMusopen Symphony
    9:53

    This is the third movement, Allegro molto vivace, of Tchaikovsky’s Symphony No. 6 in B minor, Op. 74 “Pathétique”. Over fast string figures, a march begins as a clarinet tune played quietly and builds to a G-major ending with bass drum and cymbals. The symphony’s slow final movement follows it.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, strings and woodwind

      The strings play rapid groups of three, and the woodwind reply above them. Fragments of a march tune appear among the running notes. Later in the movement the full orchestra takes up this tune.

    2. March theme, clarinets

      The clarinets play the march theme in full, quietly and in E major. The strings keep moving underneath.

    3. Corrections after the première

      On the title page of his full score Tchaikovsky added a note, apparently after the first performance: “I made some corrections in the 2nd and 3rd movements, which need to go into the parts!!! Ask Mr Kleinecke to attend to this.”

    4. Return of the opening scherzo

      The opening scherzo material returns. Running figures in the strings alternate with scales in the woodwind, now following the full statement of the march.

    5. Dominant pedal before the march

      The preparation rests on a pedal on D, the dominant of G major. The orchestra plays over the held note before the march returns in the movement’s home key.

    6. Return of the march, G major

      The march returns in G major, now with bass drum and cymbals. The tune, first played softly by the clarinets, is taken up by the full orchestra.

    7. Sketch inscription, February 1893

      In his February 1893 sketches, Tchaikovsky noted that his first job after returning from a trip was to compose this ending. He wanted the G-major march “in a solemnly triumphant manner”.

    8. Reception of the march

      Critics and audiences have found this march confusing. David Brown read it as “a desperate bid for happiness so prolonged and vehement” that it confirms both the desperation of the search and its futility. The movement ends in G major, and the close often draws applause before the symphony is over.

    9. Closing scales in G major

      At the end of the movement, scales descend through four octaves, first in the violins and then in the low brass. The movement ends in G major. The Adagio lamentoso follows as the final movement.

    Joins

    1. Clarinets complete the march tune

      Fragments of the march collect into a complete E-major tune for unison clarinets. Beneath its longer notes the strings continue their running accompaniment.

    2. Two-bar link back to the scherzo

      A two-bar link leads from the march back to the opening scherzo material. The strings return to their quick groups of three, and short wind figures cut across them. The return follows at once. The movement has no separate development section.

    3. Pedal on D before the march

      The returning scherzo reaches a pedal on D. The bass holds this dominant beneath the continuing figures and prepares G major, the key of the march’s final statement.

    4. From the D pedal to the G-major march

      The D pedal leads to the march in G major. Strings and winds broaden the tune, and bass drum and cymbals join as the march returns. The clarinet first stated the march quietly. Here the full orchestra plays it.

    5. Descending scales into the final chords

      In the closing phrases of the march, descending scales pass through the violins and then to the trombones and tuba. The scales cover four octaves and end on G major. Brass and percussion reinforce the final chords.
