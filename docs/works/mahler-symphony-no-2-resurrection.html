Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Symphony No. 2 “Resurrection”

1888–⁠94 · Soprano, alto, chorus, orchestra, offstage brass and percussion

The symphony moves from a C minor funeral rite to a choral close in E♭ major. In the finale the offstage band exchanges calls with a flute and piccolo on stage (the “Great Summons”) before the choir enters at the edge of audibility.

  1. I. Allegro maestosoBarbara Schubert; DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 22 May 2004
    22:10

    The first movement of the Second Symphony, “Resurrection”, is an Allegro maestoso in C minor in the manner of a funeral march. Cellos and double basses open it under tremolo upper strings, and a long violin melody in E major returns several times over the course of the movement.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. First theme, cellos and basses

      Cellos and double basses play the first theme in C minor beneath tremolo in the upper strings. Their phrases break off and start again. Mahler marks the movement Allegro maestoso, with thoroughly serious and solemn expression.

    2. Mahler’s letter of 1896

      In a letter of 1896 Mahler described this movement as “the titanic struggles of a mighty being still caught in the toils of this world”, grappling with life and fate, and with “his death”. He had earlier called the movement Todtenfeier, funeral rites.

    3. Violin melody in E major

      At rehearsal 3 the violins play a long, connected melody in E major, marked “Im Tempo nachgeben”. Soft horns support it. The bass keeps moving below.

    4. Performance for Hans von Bülow, 1891

      In 1891 Mahler played the early version of this movement to Hans von Bülow at the piano, and Bülow covered his ears. At Bülow’s memorial service in Hamburg on 29 March 1894, Mahler heard the hymn that led him to the symphony’s choral finale.

    5. Second subject in C major

      The second subject opens the first development in C major. Winds and strings play it softly, and the harp plays figures between the phrases.

    6. “Todtenfeier” manuscript, Prague, 1888

      The title page of the earliest manuscript, dated Prague, 10 September 1888, reads “Todtenfeier”, Symphony in C minor, first movement. The title almost certainly comes from Siegfried Lipiner’s German translation of Mickiewicz’s Dziady, published in 1887.

    7. Mahler’s remark, summer 1893

      In the summer of 1893, when he resumed the symphony after five years, Mahler told Natalie Bauer-⁠Lechner: “My two symphonies treat exhaustively my entire life; it is experience and suffering that I have written down with my lifeblood.”

    8. Hans von Bülow, Hamburg, 1891

      In Hamburg in 1891 Mahler failed to persuade Hans von Bülow to perform Todtenfeier. According to the later recollection of Mahler’s friend Josef Foerster, Bülow said: “If that is still music, then I understand nothing about music anymore.”

    9. Second development, horn chorale

      The second development sets the march beside a solemn chorale for six horns. Its tune is related to the Dies irae, the old chant of the Day of Judgement. Similar hymn-like brass writing returns in the finale.

    10. Second autograph score, 1894

      On 29 April 1894 Mahler completed a new autograph score of this movement, revising it before the symphony was finished. He signed off the autograph of the whole work in Hamburg on 18 December 1894.

    11. Dissonant climax, bars 324–⁠329

      The Dies irae chant is quoted from bar 270, and shortly afterwards the movement reaches a harshly dissonant climax at bars 324–⁠329. Stephen Hefling noted that the finale later dissolves the approach to this same climax, well before the chorus proclaims redemption.

    12. Recapitulation of the first subject

      The music returns to C minor, and the low-string figures of the opening come back. The basses play in short, broken phrases, as at the start.

    13. Second subject returns in E major

      In the recapitulation the second subject returns in the violins in E major, the key of its first appearance. The strings play it broadly, with softer chords in the woodwind and horns.

    14. Coda in C minor

      The coda turns back towards C minor. After the violin melody, short string phrases, figures from the earlier wind parts and the march rhythms return.

    15. First performances, Berlin, 1895

      Mahler conducted the first three movements in Berlin on 4 March 1895 and the whole symphony on 13 December. Most critics were dismissive or hostile. The audience’s enthusiasm grew through the evening and ended in a long ovation.

    16. Pause of at least five minutes

      On the last page Mahler asks for a pause of at least five minutes between the funeral march and the A♭ major dance that follows.

    Joins

    1. Violins move to E major

      At rehearsal 3 the music moves from the C minor march into E major. The violins play a long melody over moving low strings, with soft horns and woodwind chords. The marking Im Tempo nachgeben tells the conductor to let the tempo yield with the phrase.

    2. The second subject returns to the march

      The E major violin phrase of the second subject runs into shorter orchestral figures at rehearsal 3. Winds and brass sharpen the accents, and the low strings take up the march’s repeated movement again beneath them.

    3. Development opens in C major

      The march dies away and the lyrical subject begins the development in C major. Wind phrases and strings extend the melody into longer spans, and the harps enter among the fading orchestral chords.

    4. March and horn chorale in the second development

      The first development ends quietly, and the march returns. In the second development, short string figures and a measured chorale for six horns are heard together. The chorale’s longer notes are held across the march rhythms.

    5. Return to the low strings

      The central orchestral outburst breaks off. Cellos and basses play the opening figures again in C minor, with rests between the attacks as at the start. The march returns from these low strings.

    6. Second subject returns in E major

      The march music recedes. The violins take up their long melody again in E major, with restrained winds and horns around the line. The theme returns in its original key, a major third above the movement’s C.

    7. From the violin melody to C minor

      The violin melody breaks into fragments and C minor returns. The low strings take up the funeral rhythm again beneath short phrases in the winds. In the coda, winds and strings play a rapid descending scale, followed by the short closing chords (Hofmeister score, movement I, pp. 49–⁠56; the descending close is on p. 56).

  2. II. Andante moderatoBarbara Schubert; DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 22 May 2004
    10:22

    The second movement of the Second Symphony, “Resurrection”, follows the opening funeral march and is a dance for strings in A♭ major. At its first return the cellos add a new melody, and at the next the strings play the whole tune plucked.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Ländler in A♭ major, strings

      The strings begin a Ländler, an Austrian country dance in three, in A♭ major after the first movement’s C minor. Mahler marks it Sehr gemächlich (very leisurely) and adds Nie eilen, never hurry.

    2. Triplets in the strings, rehearsal 3

      A horn figure introduces running triplets. Only a few string desks play them at first, and more desks join as the figures continue. The tune has moved to the minor, but Mahler marks the passage “Nicht eilen. Sehr gemächlich.”

    3. Steinbach am Attersee, July 1893

      At Steinbach am Attersee in July 1893, Mahler worked on three movements: this dance, the fish-sermon scherzo and Urlicht. He dated the Andante’s orchestral draft 30 July, eleven days after the song.

    4. Return of the Ländler, cello counter-melody

      The Ländler returns in A♭ major. The violins play the dance tune while the cellos add a new legato melody below it, and the double basses play the beat pizzicato.

    5. Publication by Hofmeister, Leipzig

      With the support of two wealthy Hamburg industrialists, Hofmeister of Leipzig published the symphony first in Hermann Behn’s two-piano arrangement in 1896, then as a full score in February 1897.

    6. Return of the minor-key episode

      The minor-key episode returns, and winds and brass join the strings. The triplets now drive a full orchestral passage.

    7. Mahler’s letter of 1896

      In a letter of 1896 Mahler called the Andante and the Scherzo “episodes from the life of the fallen hero”. In the same letter he described the first movement as the hero’s titanic struggle with life and fate, and his death.

    8. Partial première, Berlin, 1895

      On 4 March 1895 Mahler conducted the first three movements in Berlin. The Andante and scherzo followed the funeral march, and the concert stopped there. Urlicht and the choral finale were first heard in public on 13 December 1895.

    9. Return of the Ländler, pizzicato

      The Ländler returns in A♭ major, played pizzicato by the strings. Flute, piccolo and harp add short notes above.

    10. Steinbach am Attersee, June 1893

      By about 20 June 1893 Mahler and his household, Natalie Bauer-⁠Lechner among them, had moved to Steinbach am Attersee. He worked first on this Andante and finished its sketch in about seven days. He had not yet orchestrated it.

    Joins

    1. A horn starts the triplets

      At rehearsal 3 the A♭ major dance moves to the minor. A soft horn figure starts the triplets, and at first only a few string desks join it. Flute, clarinet and harp enter later in the passage. The marking Nicht eilen is repeated in the score, and the beat stays at the opening tempo while the note values get shorter.

    2. Cello countermelody under the returning Ländler

      The triplets slow and grow softer. At rehearsal 5, marked In Tempo I zurückkehren, the opening tempo returns with the A♭ major Ländler. The violins play the dance melody, the cellos play a sustained countermelody below it and the double basses continue pizzicato.

    3. Winds and brass take up the triplets

      The second triplet episode brings winds and brass into the running string figures. The short notes, first played by a few players, now pass through the whole orchestra, and longer wind phrases are held above them.

    4. Return of the opening tempo, pizzicato

      The running figures break into short fragments. At rehearsal 12 Mahler restores the opening tempo and A♭ major and marks the strings pizzicato. Flute, piccolo and harp decorate the plucked melody as it passes through the string sections.

  3. III. In ruhig fließender BewegungBarbara Schubert; DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 22 May 2004
    11:29

    The third movement of Mahler’s Second Symphony, the “Resurrection”, is a scherzo based on his song about St Anthony preaching to the fish, who enjoy the sermon and change nothing. The strings play continuous running figures until the full orchestra reaches a climax in B♭ minor.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, timpani and strings

      The timpani strike G and C. The strings then begin a continuous running figure, with short woodwind interjections above it. The heading asks for quietly flowing motion, and the string figures repeatedly turn back on themselves.

    2. E♭ clarinet solo, mit Humor

      The E♭ clarinet enters over the continuous string figuration with a short solo, which Mahler marks mit Humor, with humour. It plays in its high, shrill register above the strings.

    3. St Anthony’s sermon to the fishes

      The scherzo shares its music with Mahler’s Wunderhorn song Des Antonius von Padua Fischpredigt. In the song, St Anthony finds his church empty and takes his sermon to the river, where the fish gather with their mouths open and enjoy every word. Afterwards the pike still steal and the carp still gorge themselves.

    4. Melody in bassoons and cellos

      Bassoons and cellos take the melody into the lower voices of the orchestra. The short running figures continue around them, and they keep going when Mahler varies the tune.

    5. Scherzo and the fish-sermon song

      The scherzo is the fish-sermon song without the voice. The woodwinds play the vocal tune in fragments. The strings keep the song’s continuous semiquaver motion.

    6. Manuscript dates, summer 1893

      On 8 July 1893 Mahler finished the voice-and-piano manuscript of the fish sermon song. Eight days later he dated the orchestral draft of the scherzo. The orchestral version of the song followed on 1 August.

    7. Trio in C major

      The trio opens in C major. After a rumble in the brass and timpani, piccolo and flute hold a high note over low strings marked sehr wuchtig, very weighty. Horns and trumpets then play a fanfare that refers to the First Symphony, and at Vorwärts the full orchestra enters.

    8. First-trumpet melody

      The first trumpet plays a long melody over harp arpeggios and running strings. The other trumpets hold soft harmony around it.

    9. Return of Tempo I, rehearsal 43

      At rehearsal 43 the opening tempo returns. Running figures pass through the divided strings while the trumpets hold sustained harmony above them. The long melody ends and the scherzo’s earlier motion resumes.

    10. Return of the opening texture

      The scherzo’s opening texture returns in C minor, with running semiquavers in muted violins, plucked lower strings and a bundle of twigs on the bass drum. At rehearsal 46 the full orchestra enters fortissimo. Rushing runs in the winds and strings follow.

    11. B♭ minor climax

      The scherzo reaches a B♭ minor climax. Brass chords and percussion play over the running strings. The passage returns at the start of the finale, after the alto’s prayer.

    12. Return of the low-string figures

      Long chords in the winds and brass make a diminuendo. Beneath them, divided cellos and basses take up their quick figures again, and stopped horns add a pinched sound.

    13. Ritardando before rehearsal 53

      The scherzo slows by imperceptible degrees. Muted trumpets and soft woodwind chords hold above a timpani roll. Horns and trumpets play a quiet phrase that the violins take up, molto espressivo, and both harps play an upward glissando.

    14. Ending and transition to Urlicht

      At rehearsal 54 the woodwind play rushing scales, ff, above a falling harp glissando. Tempo I returns with the running strings, which thin out over the last pages until only pizzicato strings and a stroke on the tam-tam remain. Mahler directs that the fourth movement follows without any break.

    Joins

    1. Bassoons and cellos at rehearsal 32

      Descending figures pass down through the winds and strings. At rehearsal 32 the bassoons and cellos begin a low, quieter phrase, supported by the horns, with continued movement beneath. The middle section grows out of this thinning of the scherzo texture.

    2. Held trumpets over returning strings

      Mahler writes Zum Tempo I zurückkehren, returning to the first tempo. The trumpets hold chords while short figures return in the divided strings, beginning low and spreading upwards.

    3. The B♭ minor climax

      Rapid string figures crescendo into a B♭ minor climax for the full orchestra. Winds and brass broaden into emphatic chords, and percussion reinforces the bass. The running accompaniment continues within a much slower, heavier statement.

    4. Low strings beneath diminishing brass chords

      The brass and wind chords lengthen and diminish. Stopped horns hold above the quick figures, which return in divided cellos and basses. At rehearsal 51 Mahler marks the passage Wieder unmerklich zurückhaltend, and the lower strings take up the scherzo’s motion again.

  4. IV. Urlicht — Sehr feierlich, aber schlichtBarbara Schubert, the DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 2004
    4:59

    Urlicht (Sehr feierlich, aber schlicht), the fourth movement of the Symphony No. 2 “Resurrection”, follows three movements of marches and dances, and here an alto sings the symphony’s first words. The text is a short prayer in which even an angel cannot turn the traveller away from God.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Alto entry and brass chorale

      An alto begins alone, with “O Röschen roth!” in long, held notes. Trumpets and horns follow with a hymn. Mahler marks the song Sehr feierlich, aber schlicht (very solemn, yet simple) and adds Choralmässig, in the manner of a chorale.

    2. Brass chorale, rising theme

      The brass chorale moves in rising steps. An expanded version of this ascending theme returns in the finale as the resurrection theme.

    3. Alto, “Der Mensch liegt in größter Noth!”

      The alto sings “Der Mensch liegt in größter Noth! Der Mensch liegt in größter Pein!” The vocal line takes on chromatic notes, and the metre keeps changing. At “Je lieber möcht’ ich im Himmel sein” (I would rather be in heaven), Mahler marks a ritenuto and molto espress.

    4. Orchestral manuscript, Steinbach, 1893

      Mahler dated an orchestral manuscript of Urlicht at Steinbach am Attersee on 19 July 1893. That summer he was also working on the dance movements of the Second Symphony. The song already existed before he decided to place it in the symphony.

    5. Source of the text, 1808

      Urlicht is a folk poem from Des Knaben Wunderhorn. It was printed in the second volume (Heidelberg, 1808) under the heading Mündlich, meaning by word of mouth. Mahler repeats several lines, among them “Ach nein, ich liess mich nicht abweisen.”

    6. Central section, Etwas bewegter

      At Etwas bewegter the music turns to the minor. A clarinet figure, harp notes and a glockenspiel stroke mark the time as the traveller meets an angel. A solo violin plays a longer melody, and three sharps appear in the signature as the angel tries to turn the traveller back.

    7. “Ach nein!”, leidenschaftlich

      Mahler marks the line “Ach nein! Ich liess mich nicht abweisen.” leidenschaftlich, passionately, over string tremolo. “Ich bin von Gott, ich will wieder zu Gott” is marked zart drängend (gently urging). The phrases then rise with growing expression.

    8. Closing bars in D♭ major

      After the alto’s last word, “Leben”, the solo violins and strings continue in D♭ major with the harps. Mahler marks the ending gänzlich ersterbend, dying away completely, and writes at the foot of the page that the finale follows without a break.

    Joins

    1. Alto re-enters after brass chorale

      The brass chorale closes in D♭ major, and the alto re-enters over strings. At the words about human suffering, semitones enter her melody and the metre changes frequently.

    2. Oboe turn, then clarinet figures

      A solo oboe plays a turning phrase that closes the alto’s first section. The middle section begins in a minor key at a quicker tempo. Clarinet figures enter around the voice while harp and glockenspiel mark the time. A solo violin then extends these figures into a longer tune as the traveller tells of the angel.

    3. Alto line rises through semitones

      After the middle section’s move into three sharps, the alto enters at “Ach nein!”, marked leidenschaftlich, over string tremolo. At “Ich bin von Gott” the phrases are marked zart drängend and mit steigerndem Ausdruck, and the line rises. The strings play the final D♭ major cadence after the singer’s last word.

    Open in the Concert Hall

  5. V. Im Tempo des Scherzos — Wild herausfahrendBarbara Schubert; DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 22 May 2004
    35:27

    The fifth movement of Mahler’s Symphony No. 2 “Resurrection”, marked “Im Tempo des Scherzos — Wild herausfahrend”, opens with an orchestral outburst, and voices enter only after marches, distant brass and birdsong. The chorus first sings very softly, and at its last entry organ, bells and the whole orchestra join it in E♭ major.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Return of the scherzo’s outcry

      The orchestral outcry from the scherzo breaks in after the silence that follows Urlicht. The horns then play a phrase that later becomes the resurrection theme. The melody appears here before the chorus sings its words.

    2. Offstage horns and wind chorale

      Horns play calls from offstage. Descending triplets pass down through the winds, and a measured wind chorale moves over pizzicato string quavers. Pauses separate these sections, and each has its own instruments and its own distance.

    3. Wind chorale over pizzicato strings

      After a silence, flute, oboe and clarinet play a chorale pianissimo over pizzicato violas and cellos. It recalls the Dies irae from the first movement’s funeral march. Some of its intervals anticipate the resurrection theme.

    4. Bülow’s memorial service, Hamburg, 1894

      At Hans von Bülow’s memorial service in Hamburg on 29 March 1894, Mahler heard Klopstock’s resurrection hymn. He used its first two stanzas for the finale and wrote the rest of the text himself.

    5. Chorale, solo trombone and trumpet

      A solo trombone, marked bestimmt (decisively), takes up the chorale, and a trumpet follows. The horns then come to the front, the offstage horns play again, and the string tremolo dies away to nothing.

    6. Woodwind climax, rehearsal 7 to 8

      Figures pass between the winds and quicken. Mahler marks Etwas drängend, somewhat pressing, then Sehr drängend, very pressing, and the woodwinds rise to fortissimo. The sound then falls away to the low trombones and a faint timpani roll.

    7. Chorale for low brass, rehearsal 10

      After a general pause the trombones and tuba, with the contrabassoon, play a broad chorale, Wieder sehr breit. The trumpets join at Etwas energischer, and a long crescendo leads to a brass fanfare.

    8. Return of the fanfare in C major

      The brass fanfare returns in C major. Horns and trumpets exchange repeated calls over harps and cymbals, while the winds and strings play running figures. The resurrection theme is heard again, far more assertive than before.

    9. Percussion crescendo and march

      A percussion crescendo leads to a brass fanfare and the Allegro energico. Earlier themes return as a march, with sharply articulated rhythms and fast string figures. Mahler marks the passage Nicht zu schnell (not too fast).

    10. Dies irae as a march

      The Dies irae becomes a march, serious and at first upbeat, and the trumpets play a chorale-like variation of the same melody above it. At Kräftig (vigorously), Mahler’s note in the score states that a brisk march tempo has now been reached.

    11. Low bells during the march

      Deep bells of no fixed pitch sound against the march, and shortly before they enter the woodwinds raise their bells. Mahler wrote for three such bells, in three passages of the movement totalling twenty-two bars.

    12. Composition of the finale, 1894

      Mahler jotted down sketches for the finale as soon as he reached home from Bülow’s funeral, and completed it within about three weeks the following summer. He signed the autograph full score of the whole symphony on 18 December 1894.

    13. Cellos and bassoons, distant trumpets

      The march breaks off into speech-like phrases in the strings. Cellos and bassoons play the melody in the foreground, and distant trumpets and percussion continue behind them. At rehearsal 22 Mahler directed the conductor to keep the far-off fanfares from disturbing the melody.

    14. “O glaube” theme, cellos

      The cellos play the “O glaube” material in phrases that move between duple and triple metres. Much later the alto sings “O glaube, mein Herz” (believe, my heart) to a return of the same phrases.

    15. Offstage band

      Far off, a band of trumpets, triangle, cymbals and bass drum plays fanfare signals across the cellos’ line, repeatedly. Mahler sets the band at the greatest distance and at one point asks for it to sound much stronger.

    16. Climax, rehearsal 26

      At rehearsal 26 Mahler marks Più mosso and molto accelerando. Woodwind, horns and trumpets play fortissimo over triangle, bass drum, cymbals, tam-tam and timpani. Mahler’s programme for the passage read: “The earth trembles, the graves burst open, the dead arise and march forth in endless procession.”

    17. Langsam after the climax, rehearsal 27

      At rehearsal 27 the climax breaks off, and Mahler marks Langsam, immer noch mehr zurückhaltend. Three pairs of horns play pp chords over a timpani tremolo, sempre pp. The oboe and cellos play separate lines, and the clarinets are marked “(Echo)”.

    18. Distant brass and birdsong

      At the great summons the main orchestra drops out for long stretches. Trumpets play from opposing directions and horns hold distant notes. Flute and piccolo play birdsong figures, marked wie eine Vogelstimme, and at times a single instrument sounds alone.

    19. First complete performance, Berlin, 1895

      Mahler conducted the first complete performance of the symphony in Berlin on 13 December 1895. Nine months earlier the city had heard only the first three movements. The audience applauded warmly, and the next morning’s newspapers renewed the attacks.

    20. Mahler’s programme: the nightingale

      In Mahler’s programme the last trumpet sounds, and the silence that follows holds “a distant nightingale, a last tremulous echo of earthly life”. In the score the flute and piccolo play this passage, marked to sound like a bird’s voice.

    21. Entry of the chorus, G♭ major

      At rehearsal 31, marked Langsam. Misterioso, the chorus enters a cappella in G♭ major at ppp. Klopstock’s hymn promises that dust will rise again after a short rest. The soprano soloist sings within the choral sound, and Mahler marked her part “ohne im Geringsten hervorzutreten”.

    22. Mahler alters Klopstock

      Klopstock wrote that the one who made you (“der dich schuf”) will give you immortal life. Mahler sets “der dich rief”: the one who called you. He also turns “I shall be sown” into “you will be sown”, and drops the Hallelujahs.

    23. Orchestral interlude after first stanza

      The chorus completes its first stanza, with the soprano soloist singing within its line, and the orchestra continues alone. Mahler marks Sehr breit, very broad, for horns and trumpets in portamento, then Streng im Tempo, strictly in time, with long bows in the strings. The chorus returns for the second stanza.

    24. Klopstock’s second stanza

      Klopstock’s second stanza reads “To bloom again you are sown! The Lord of the harvest goes and gathers in, like sheaves, us who died.” The chorus sings it quietly. An orchestral interlude then ends Klopstock’s text, and Mahler’s own words follow.

    25. DuPage Symphony Orchestra, Wheaton, 2004

      This is Barbara Schubert’s DuPage Symphony Orchestra in Edman Memorial Chapel, Wheaton College, Illinois, on 22 May 2004. Eric Pancer taped the concert on a Sony MiniDisc recorder. He put it on the Internet Archive that December.

    26. Alto solo, B♭ minor

      Mahler’s own words begin with an appeal to the heart. The alto sings in B♭ minor beside a cor anglais, over muted strings and plucked bass notes.

    27. Soprano solo, rehearsal 41

      The soprano follows the alto: “O believe, you were not born in vain!” A solo violin on the D string continues her line over tremolo strings. At rehearsal 42 a harp glissando, its first note strongly accented, leads upward into the next section.

    28. Rehearsal 42, Mahler’s own text

      Low voices bring back the resurrection theme with Mahler’s own words, “Was entstanden ist, das muss vergehen”, over trombones, tuba and a high violin trill. The chorus sings “auferstehen”. Alto and chorus then sing “Hör’ auf zu beben!” (stop trembling) at ppp, followed by “Bereite dich zu leben!” (prepare to live).

    29. Duet for alto and soprano

      Alto and soprano sing “O Schmerz! Du Alldurchdringer!” (O pain, all-penetrating one). Mahler marks the passage Mit Aufschwung, aber nicht eilen (with uplift, but not hurrying), and later Vorwärts and Drängend. The duet briefly refers to the melody of Urlicht.

    30. Return of the choral melody

      The first choral melody, first heard without accompaniment, returns in E♭ major with brass and organ. Soprano and alto join the chorus. The organ is marked volles Werk, full organ.

    31. Mahler on the final climax

      Mahler told Natalie Bauer-⁠Lechner that the build-up and surge of the closing pages were “so immense that afterwards even I do not know how I came to write it”.

    32. Letter to Anna von Mildenburg, 1895

      On 8 December 1895, five days before the première, Mahler wrote to the soprano Anna von Mildenburg about the bells he needed. He had taken the train through snow-covered fir woods to a foundry at Zehlendorf, because “only a bell founder could help me”.

    33. Orchestral close in E♭ major

      After the singers finish, the orchestra continues with organ and bells in E♭ major. On the last page Mahler marks Mit höchster Kraftentfaltung (with the utmost force), then a sharply cut-off ending, scharf abgerissen. The whole orchestra releases the final chord together.

    34. Trumpets over the closing chord

      Over a sustained E♭ major chord the trumpets play a short two-note figure again and again, loud and then louder. All ten horns play the long closing chord with the full orchestra, organ and bells.

    Joins

    1. Horn calls emerge from the outburst

      The opening orchestral outburst dies away into horn phrases separated by pauses. Offstage horns enter in reply, and descending triplets pass down through the winds. The prelude then sets a wind chorale over pizzicato quavers in the strings.

    2. Percussion leads into the brass fanfare

      The percussion enters as the prelude dies away and leads into a Maestoso brass fanfare. In the Allegro energico that follows, the strings play running figures beneath sharply accented march figures. Mahler marks the passage Nicht zu schnell, which keeps the beat firm under the quick notes.

    3. Cello recitative after the march

      The march breaks off, and the cellos play irregular phrases in the manner of a recitative. Bassoons join them above restrained strings. Distant trumpet and percussion calls continue separately. At rehearsal 22 the score gives an explicit balance instruction that the trumpet and percussion calls must not cover the cello and bassoon melody.

    4. Distant brass and birdsong

      The strings stop. Horns and trumpets play calls separated by long held notes and pauses, and the score directs the trumpets to sound from opposite sides. Between the brass notes, flute and piccolo play short, quick birdsong figures from nearby.

    5. Unaccompanied chorus in G♭ major

      The distant calls and the birdsong stop. At Langsam. Misterioso the chorus enters in G♭ major, a cappella and ppp. The soprano soloist sings within the upper choral line, and the basses take the lowest part, which the orchestra no longer plays.

    6. Alto and cor anglais in B♭ minor

      After the choral stanzas and the orchestral replies between them, the alto enters in B♭ minor. A cor anglais plays a line around the vocal part, accompanied by muted divided strings and pizzicato basses. The soprano then takes up the appeal.

    7. From the soprano’s phrase to the chorus

      The soprano’s phrase slows and the strings drop back. A harp glissando leads to the entry of the lower choral voices, and trumpets, trombones and tuba follow their measured phrases. Mahler marks the passage Langsamer. Misterioso. The soloists’ duet then begins and rises.

    8. Organ joins the E♭ major chorus

      Overlapping choral lines rise through a crescendo. The resurrection melody returns in E♭ major, marked Pesante, and the soloists join the chorus. At rehearsal 48 the full organ enters, marked volles Werk. With broad brass chords, it holds the harmony beneath the voices through their long phrases.

    9. Choral close to orchestral final page

      The voices finish and the instruments continue in E♭ major. Brass, strings and organ hold the closing harmony, and bells sound above the bass. On the final page the tempo slows to half speed, marked Noch einmal so langsam, and the orchestra cuts off the last chord together, marked scharf abgerissen.
