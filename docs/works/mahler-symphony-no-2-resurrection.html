Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Symphony No. 2 “Resurrection”

1888–94 · Soprano, alto, chorus, orchestra, offstage brass and percussion

From a C minor funeral rite to a choral E♭ major. In the finale, the offstage band and an onstage flute and piccolo trade calls — the “Great Summons” — before the choir enters almost inaudibly.

5 recordings · 1 h 24 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro maestosoBarbara Schubert; DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 22 May 2004
    22:10

    Cellos and double basses tear into the Second Symphony beneath trembling upper strings. This C-minor funeral march keeps finding a long violin melody in E major, as though a door keeps opening onto another room.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The basses lead

      Cellos and double basses hurl out the first theme beneath trembling strings. Their phrases lunge forwards, stop, and begin again. Mahler marks the movement Allegro maestoso, with thoroughly serious and solemn expression; C minor has rarely sounded less willing to sit still.

    2. A door into E major

      The violins find a long, connected melody in E major. Soft horns support it while the bass keeps moving below. Mahler asks the tempo to yield: after the opening’s broken speech, the tune finally has room for a whole sentence.

    3. Bülow covers his ears

      In 1891 Mahler plays the early version of this movement to Hans von Bülow at the piano. Bülow covers his ears. Three years later, at Bülow’s memorial service in Hamburg, Mahler hears the hymn that unlocks the symphony’s choral finale.

    4. The song in C major

      The lyrical theme begins the development in C major, bringing its long breath into the march’s home territory. Quiet winds and strings let it spread out; harp figures glint between the phrases.

    5. A hymn among the marches

      The second development sets the march beside a solemn chorale for six horns. Its tune is related to the Dies irae, the old chant of the Day of Judgement. Mahler will bring this hymn-like brass writing back in the finale.

    6. The march comes back

      C minor and the opening’s low-string figures return. The basses still speak in short, interrupted phrases; the long central struggle has left them with the same fierce business to finish.

    7. The violins keep E major

      The violin song returns in E major, the same distant key as before. Mahler lets it remain a separate place: the strings sing broadly while softer wind and horn chords surround them.

    8. The funeral resumes

      The coda turns back towards C minor. Short string phrases, wind recollections and renewed march rhythms fill the space the violin song leaves behind. The movement still has a funeral to complete.

    9. Five minutes of silence

      On the last page Mahler asks for a pause of at least five minutes. It is an extraordinary demand to make of a concert hall: the funeral march must stand apart before the A♭-major dance can begin.

    How it is joined

    1. The violins find E major

      At rehearsal 3 the C-minor march opens into E major. The violins stretch out a melody over moving low strings, soft horns and woodwind chords. Im Tempo nachgeben asks the conductor to let the pace yield with the phrase.

    2. The march breaks into the song

      The E-major violin phrase runs into shorter orchestral figures. Winds and brass sharpen the accents, and the low strings recover the march’s insistent movement beneath them.

    3. C major lets the melody breathe

      The march subsides and the lyrical subject begins the development in C major. Quiet wind phrases and strings carry the melody into longer spans; the harps enter among the receding orchestral chords.

    4. The march gathers around a chorale

      The quiet end of the first development leaves space for the march to gather again. In the second development, short string figures and a measured six-horn chorale share the orchestra; the chorale’s longer notes stretch across the march rhythms.

    5. Back to the low strings

      The central orchestral outburst breaks off. Cellos and basses recover the opening figures in C minor, their attacks separated by the same stubborn rests. The returning march grows from those low strings.

    6. E major survives the march

      The march recedes. The violins recover their long melody in E major, with restrained winds and horns around the line. Its original key returns intact, still a major third above the movement’s C.

    7. The violin song falls away

      The violin melody thins to fragments and C minor returns. Low strings resume their funeral tread beneath short wind phrases. The coda eventually sends winds and strings down a rapid descending scale before the clipped closing chords.

  2. II. Andante moderatoBarbara Schubert; DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 22 May 2004
    10:22

    After the Second Symphony’s funeral march comes a dance for strings in A♭ major. Each return brings a small surprise: a new cello melody, then the whole tune plucked with the fingertips.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A dance for strings

      The strings begin a Ländler, an Austrian country dance in three. A♭ major sounds wonderfully comfortable after the first movement’s C minor. Mahler writes Sehr gemächlich — very leisurely — and adds another warning: Nie eilen, never hurry.

    2. Triplets under the horn

      A horn ushers in running triplets. At first only a few string players take them up; the little figures spread through the desks. The tune has turned to the minor, but Mahler still asks for a leisurely pace.

    3. July at the lake

      At Steinbach am Attersee in July 1893, Mahler works on three very different movements: this dance, the fish-sermon scherzo and Urlicht. He dates the Andante’s orchestral draft 30 July, eleven days after the song.

    4. Two tunes at once

      The Ländler returns to A♭ major. Above, the violins have their old dance; beneath, the cellos sing a new, flowing melody. The cellos keep their bows on the strings while the double basses pluck the beat.

    5. The triplets grow teeth

      The minor-key episode returns with winds and brass joining the strings. Those once-delicate triplets now drive a full orchestral passage. The country dance has a storm in its middle.

    6. Berlin hears three movements

      On 4 March 1895, Mahler conducts the first three movements in Berlin. The Andante and scherzo follow the funeral march, and the concert stops there. Urlicht and the choral finale reach the public that December.

    7. The dance on fingertips

      The strings pluck the returning Ländler. Its familiar tune becomes a collection of small, dry notes, with flute, piccolo and harp adding bright touches above. A♭ major is home again.

    How it is joined

    1. A horn starts the triplets

      The A♭-major dance turns to the minor. A soft horn figure starts the triplets; only a few string desks join at first. Flute, clarinet and harp enter as the passage grows. The score repeats Nicht eilen: the quicker notes keep the leisurely beat.

    2. A cello melody joins the dance

      The triplets slow and subside. At rehearsal 5 the score asks for the opening tempo, and the A♭-major Ländler returns. The cellos add a sustained countermelody below the violins while the double basses keep plucking.

    3. Winds and brass take up the triplets

      The second triplet episode brings winds and brass into the running string figures. The short notes that began with a few players now occupy the orchestra, with longer wind phrases stretching above them.

    4. The strings put down their bows

      The running figures dwindle to short fragments. At rehearsal 12 Mahler restores the opening tempo and A♭ major, but marks the strings pizzicato. Flute, piccolo and harp decorate the plucked melody as it passes through the string sections.

  3. III. In ruhig fließender BewegungBarbara Schubert; DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 22 May 2004
    11:29

    St Anthony preaches to the fish, who enjoy the sermon and change nothing: Mahler turns his song on that story into the Second Symphony’s scherzo. The strings circle tirelessly until the whole orchestra breaks into a cry in B♭ minor.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Two strokes, then circles

      Timpani strike G and C. The strings start running, with little woodwind comments poking through their uninterrupted figures. The movement’s heading asks for quietly flowing motion; the notes keep turning back on themselves.

    2. The fish enjoy the sermon

      In Mahler’s song, St Anthony finds his church empty and takes his sermon to the river. The fish gather with their mouths open. Afterwards the pike still steal and the carp still gorge themselves. They have enjoyed every word.

    3. The tune sinks lower

      Bassoons and cellos bring the melody down into the orchestra’s lower voices. The little running figures persist around them. Even when Mahler changes the tune, the water keeps moving.

    4. Eight days between manuscripts

      On 8 July 1893 Mahler finishes the voice-and-piano manuscript of the fish sermon. Eight days later he dates the scherzo’s orchestral draft. The orchestral song follows on 1 August: song and symphony grow side by side that summer.

    5. A trumpet sings

      A trumpet stretches a long melody over harp arpeggios and running strings. Other trumpets hold soft harmony around it. The brass sounds unexpectedly tender after the woodwinds’ busy gossip.

    6. Back into the current

      The opening tempo returns. Little figures spread through divided strings while the trumpets hold on above them. The long song drains away, leaving the scherzo’s old restless motion.

    7. The orchestra cries out

      The circling scherzo reaches a huge B♭-minor outburst. Brass chords and percussion tower over the running strings. This cry will return at the start of the finale, after the alto’s quiet prayer.

    8. The current starts again

      Long chords fade in the winds and brass. Underneath, divided cellos and basses resume their quick figures; stopped horns add a pinched, distant sound. The machinery of the scherzo is still turning.

    How it is joined

    1. Bassoons take the lower path

      Descending figures pass down through winds and strings. At rehearsal 32 the bassoons and cellos begin a low, quieter phrase, with horn support and continued movement beneath. The middle section grows from this thinning of the scherzo.

    2. Held trumpets over returning strings

      Mahler writes Zum Tempo I zurückkehren, returning to the first tempo. Trumpet chords linger while short figures reappear in divided strings, beginning low and spreading upwards. The broad melody releases its hold on the orchestra.

    3. B♭ minor breaks across the dance

      Rapid string figures swell into a B♭-minor orchestral outcry. Winds and brass widen into emphatic chords, and percussion reinforces the bass. The running accompaniment is caught inside a much slower, heavier declaration.

    4. Low strings move beneath fading chords

      The brass and wind chords lengthen and diminish. Stopped horns remain above the quick figures returning in divided cellos and basses. Mahler slows the retreat almost imperceptibly while the lower strings recover the scherzo’s motion.

  4. IV. Urlicht — Sehr feierlich, aber schlichtBarbara Schubert, the DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 2004
    4:59

    After three movements of marching and dancing, an alto sings the Second Symphony’s first words. Urlicht is a small, stubborn prayer: even an angel cannot turn its traveller away from God.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One voice, then brass

      An alto begins with three rising notes. Trumpets answer with a hymn, unfolding that small upward phrase into an arch. Mahler marks the song Sehr feierlich, aber schlicht — very solemn, yet simple.

    2. Suffering bends the tune

      The alto sings of human misery. Semitones creep into the vocal line and the harmony beneath it; the brass hymn’s plain steps become harder to climb. When the singer longs for heaven, the hymn returns.

    3. A summer manuscript

      Steinbach am Attersee, 19 July 1893: Mahler dates an orchestral manuscript of Urlicht. He is also working on the Second Symphony’s dances that summer. The song already exists before he decides to give it this place in the symphony.

    4. The angel on the road

      The prayer becomes a story. In B♭ minor, a nimble clarinet tune accompanies the traveller’s encounter with an angel; a solo violin spins a longer melody as the harmony moves towards A major. The angel tries to send the traveller back.

    5. The traveller stands firm

      The traveller refuses. The alto’s phrases climb again and again over trembling strings, each new chord lifting the reply higher. The poem’s speaker comes from God and insists on returning to God; the little prayer has acquired a fierce will.

    6. Strings finish the sentence

      The singer stops before the harmony has come to rest. The strings complete the cadence in D♭ major, quietly carrying the prayer beyond its last word.

    How it is joined

    1. The alto resumes the prayer

      The brass chorale settles in D♭ major. The alto resumes over strings, and the words about human suffering draw semitones into the melody. Mahler keeps the invocation’s upward reach while moving its plain hymn chords into more troubled harmony.

    2. An oboe turn, then clarinet

      The alto leaves the cadence unfinished; an oboe completes it with a turning phrase. The middle section begins in B♭ minor. Clarinet figures spring up around the voice, and a solo violin extends them into a longer tune as the traveller tells of the angel.

    3. The reply climbs through semitones

      After the middle section’s excursion to A major, rising chromatic chords carry the alto’s refusal upwards. String tremolo runs beneath the voice. The ascending phrases recall the first stanza’s account of suffering; the final D♭-major cadence waits until after the singer has finished.

  5. V. Im Tempo des Scherzos — Wild herausfahrendBarbara Schubert; DuPage Symphony Orchestra · 22 May 2004
    35:27

    Mahler’s resurrection begins with an orchestral cry and reaches the human voice only after marches, distant brass and birdsong. The chorus’s first answer is almost a whisper; its last brings organ, bells and the whole orchestra into E♭ major.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The cry returns

      The scherzo’s orchestral outcry bursts into the silence after Urlicht. Then the horns offer a hesitant phrase that will become the resurrection theme. The melody is here long before anyone can sing its words.

    2. Calls across an empty space

      Horn calls sound from beyond the orchestra. Descending triplets travel down through the winds; a measured wind chorale walks over plucked string quavers. Pauses separate these little scenes, each with its own instruments and its own distance.

    3. A hymn at Bülow’s funeral

      Hamburg, 29 March 1894. At Hans von Bülow’s memorial service, Mahler hears Klopstock’s resurrection hymn. It supplies the words he has been searching for; he takes its first two stanzas and writes the rest of the finale’s text himself.

    4. The drums start a march

      A percussion crescendo leads to a brass fanfare and the Allegro energico. Earlier tunes now march, with sharply bitten rhythms and fast string figures. Mahler adds Nicht zu schnell: energetic, but not too fast.

    5. Cellos against distant trumpets

      The march breaks down into speech-like string phrases. Cellos and bassoons sing in the foreground while distant trumpets and percussion continue behind them. Mahler tells the conductor to keep those far-off fanfares from disturbing the melody.

    6. Brass calls, birds answer

      The great summons leaves the main orchestra almost empty. Trumpets call from opposing directions; horns hold distant notes. Flute and piccolo answer with birdsong. There are long spaces in which only one small sound remains.

    7. The chorus begins softly

      The chorus begins in G♭ major, unaccompanied and almost inaudible. Klopstock’s hymn promises that dust will rise again after a short rest. The soprano soloist sings within the choral sound; Mahler asks her to avoid standing out.

    8. The alto’s private appeal

      Mahler’s own words begin with an appeal to the heart. The alto sings in B♭ minor beside a cor anglais, over muted strings and plucked bass notes. The huge public hymn contracts to two dark, intimate voices.

    9. The whisper becomes a proclamation

      The first choral melody returns in E♭ major with brass and organ. Soprano and alto join the chorus; the organ is marked volles Werk, full organ. The tune that began without accompaniment now has the whole building behind it.

    10. Bells after the voices

      The singers finish and the orchestra carries on, with organ and bells sounding through E♭ major. On the last page Mahler asks for the utmost force, then a sharply cut-off ending. The final chord is released together.

    How it is joined

    1. Horn calls emerge from the outburst

      The opening orchestral cry recedes into horn phrases and pauses. Offstage horns answer, and descending triplets travel down through the winds. The prelude then sets a wind chorale over pizzicato string quavers.

    2. Percussion lifts the brass fanfare

      Percussion grows out of the retreating prelude into a Maestoso brass fanfare. The following Allegro energico puts running strings beneath sharply accented march figures. Mahler writes Nicht zu schnell, keeping the beat firm under the quick notes.

    3. The cellos begin to speak

      The march breaks off and the cellos take up irregular, recitative-like phrases. Bassoons join them above restrained strings. Distant trumpet and percussion calls continue separately; the score explicitly protects the cello and bassoon melody from their interruption.

    4. The brass moves into the distance

      The strings withdraw. Horns and trumpets call across long holds and pauses, with the trumpets directed to sound from opposite sides. Flute and piccolo supply the nearby birdsong, small quick figures between the brass notes.

    5. G♭ major, without the orchestra

      The distant calls and birdsong disappear. At Langsam. Misterioso the chorus begins in G♭ major, a cappella and ppp. The soprano soloist blends into the upper choral line, while the bass voices supply the foundation the orchestra has left empty.

    6. Alto and cor anglais in B♭ minor

      After the choral stanzas and orchestral replies, the alto begins in B♭ minor. A cor anglais winds around the vocal line; muted divided strings and pizzicato basses leave room for both. The soprano takes up the appeal afterwards.

    7. The chorus answers the soloists

      The soprano’s phrase slows and the strings retreat. A harp glissando leads towards the lower choral voices; trumpets, trombones and tuba answer their measured phrases. Mahler marks the passage Langsamer. Misterioso before the soloists’ duet begins its climb.

    8. Organ joins the E♭-major chorus

      Overlapping choral lines climb through a crescendo. The resurrection melody returns in E♭ major, marked Pesante, with the soloists joining the chorus. Full organ and broad brass chords stand beneath the voices, sustaining the harmony through their long phrases.

    9. The bells outlast the words

      The voices finish while the instruments continue in E♭ major. Brass, strings and organ sustain the closing harmony, and bells sound above the bass. The final page broadens to half speed before the orchestra cuts off the last chord together.
