Maurice Ravel / V. The French Line

Boléro

1928 · Ballet for large orchestra, including saxophones and two snare drums

Two tunes and a two-bar drum pattern, repeated through a long crescendo. After so much C major, the sudden turn to E major near the end feels like the floor moving. Nijinska’s production for Ida Rubinstein opened at the Paris Opéra on 22 November 1928.

13 min

The recording

  1. BoléroBerlin Symphonic Orchestra, Ferenc Fricsay
    13:26

    A snare drum keeps its two-bar rhythm while a flute tune grows into a full orchestra. Ferenc Fricsay conducts Ravel’s extraordinary exercise in staying put, with its one startling escape into E major near the end.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A drum and a flute

      The snare drum starts quietly over plucked strings. A flute supplies the first tune, curling round the notes of C major. Beneath it, the bass alternates C and G. Those two notes will keep their place through almost the entire piece.

    2. A dancer on a table

      Paris Opéra, 22 November 1928: Ida Rubinstein dances on a tavern table while men gather around her. Bronislava Nijinska supplies the choreography. Ravel’s unbroken crescendo begins its life in the theatre.

    3. The bassoon sings high

      The second tune belongs first to a bassoon, uncomfortably high in its range. It stretches across two octaves and introduces flattened notes against the same C-and-G bass. The small E♭ clarinet takes the next turn.

    4. The oboe’s quieter cousin

      The first tune returns on oboe d’amore, the lower-pitched cousin of the oboe. Its long phrases float over the same clipped accompaniment. Ravel’s next combination is stranger: muted trumpet with a flute an octave above.

    5. An instrument Ravel invents

      A horn plays the tune with celesta and two piccolos above it. The piccolos trace it in E and G while the horn stays in C: separate instruments pick out the upper harmonics of a single, imaginary instrument.

    6. The trombone steps forward

      A solo trombone takes the second tune into its high register. Its sliding notes make the long phrase sound almost vocal. The drummer carries on underneath, still counting the same two bars.

    7. The violins finally sing

      The first violins join the melody with the woodwinds. For much of the piece the strings have been plucking or repeating the accompaniment; now the tune spreads across the desks. The second violins join it in the following statement.

    8. A factory behind the dancers

      “It was a factory which inspired my Boléro,” Ravel tells the London Evening Standard in 1932. “I would like it always to be played with a vast factory in the background.” His preferred scenery has machinery behind the dancers.

    9. Eight bars elsewhere

      After all that C major, the orchestra breaks into E major. The drum keeps going. Eight bars are enough: the change in the bass makes the whole dance seem to have slipped sideways.

    10. The dance breaks apart

      C major returns through trombone glissandos and crashing percussion. The bass drum, cymbals and tam-tam have waited until these last bars. The orchestra cuts the dance short at full strength.

    How it is joined

    1. The horn acquires upper voices

      The snare rhythm and C-and-G bass continue through the handover. Horn, celesta and two piccolos take the first melody together. The horn keeps it in C; the piccolos add E and G versions high above, while the celesta doubles the horn in octaves.

    2. The bass leaves C

      The bass abandons C and the orchestra moves directly into E major: E–G♯–B replaces C–E–G. The snare keeps its two-bar rhythm and the music stays in three beats. The new key lasts just eight bars.

    3. C major, with trombone slides

      The orchestra returns to C as the trombones slide across the repeated rhythm. Bass drum, cymbals and tam-tam enter for the final six bars. A dissonant stack of B♭-minor and F-minor notes falls onto the last C-major chord.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. BoléroOrchestre des Concerts Lamoureux, Maurice Ravel · 1930
    15:52

    Ravel conducts the Lamoureux orchestra in Boléro, recorded in 1930. The composer himself presides over the snare drum’s obstinate rhythm and the long parade of soloists.

    Notes as it plays
    1. The drummer’s two bars

      A snare drum repeats its two bars over plucked strings. The flute’s first melody moves through C major while the bass stays with C and G. Ravel builds almost the entire piece on this small foundation.

    2. A dance hall for the record

      Ravel’s Polydor session is held at the Bal Bullier, a Paris dance hall. Albert Wolff has prepared the Lamoureux players, and Ravel conducts. Wolff conducts the other piece recorded that day, Ravel’s orchestration of his Menuet antique.

    3. Fandango, then Boléro

      On 13 July 1928 Ravel hurries back to Paris: Albéniz’s Iberia cannot be orchestrated, Joaquín Nin has told him, so he works on a piece of his own. Its working title is Fandango; the definitive title becomes Boléro.

    4. One finger at the piano

      At Saint-Jean-de-Luz, Ravel picks out the theme with one finger for his friend Gustave Samazeuilh and asks whether it has “an insistent quality”. He means to repeat it “a number of times without any development”, growing the orchestra as he goes.

    5. “Not so fast”

      At Piero Coppola’s own recording of Boléro, Ravel sits beside the conductor. As the end nears, Coppola quickens by a fraction. Ravel jumps up, pulls at his jacket — “not so fast” — and the orchestra begins again.

    6. Ravel on four record sides

      Ravel conducts the Lamoureux players for Polydor in January 1930. The performance fills four sides of two 78 rpm discs. The long crescendo has to cross three breaks before it reaches its final chord.

    7. An oboe d’amore

      Flute, clarinet, bassoon and E♭ clarinet have each had their turn. Now an oboe d’amore, a double reed pitched between the oboe and the cor anglais, takes the tune. A flute and a muted trumpet will share the next one.

    8. A flute and a muted trumpet

      A flute and a muted trumpet play the tune an octave apart, so closely matched that they sound like one new instrument. It is the first time in the piece that two instruments share the melody.

    9. Priam and Mule on saxophones

      Tenor saxophone, then the higher saxophones, take the tune. On Ravel’s Polydor record the tenor part goes to Priam and the soprano to Marcel Mule.

    10. Four instruments, one organ

      Two piccolos, a celesta and a solo horn play the tune together: the horn in C, the celesta two and three octaves above, the piccolos in G and E. Spaced like the overtones of the horn’s note, the four blend into one sound that Jennifer Beavers likens to an organ stop.

    11. More than one mouth

      The parade of solitary wind players opens into groups. Ravel combines horn, celesta and two piccolos, then gives the tune to a choir of reeds. The same eighteen-bar phrase now has several players breathing through it.

    12. A publisher’s death

      On 23 August 1928 Ravel breaks off the orchestration of Boléro to go to Avon, where his publisher Jacques Durand died the day before. He finishes the piece on 15 October and, the next day, signs a contract with Durand’s firm for its sale: 20,000 francs.

    13. “Orchestral tissue without music”

      Ravel calls Boléro “orchestral tissue without music”: seventeen minutes of one long crescendo. He tells Joaquín Nin it has “no form, properly speaking, no development, no or almost no modulation”. In 1931 he adds that it contains practically no invention except the plan.

    14. Violins above the drum

      The violins join the melody with the woodwinds. Ravel has kept them accompanying for most of the piece; their arrival in the tune broadens it across the orchestra. The drum’s rhythm remains unchanged underneath.

    15. A tavern in Spain

      The Paris Opéra gives Boléro as a ballet on 22 November 1928: Bronislava Nijinska’s choreography, Alexandre Benois’s sets, a woman dancing on a tavern table. Diaghilev, Stravinsky and the princesse de Polignac are in the house; Ravel is on tour in Spain.

    16. A factory in the background

      Ravel tells the London Evening Standard in 1932: “It was a factory which inspired my Boléro. I would like it always to be played with a vast factory in the background.”

    17. Toscanini takes it faster

      On 4 May 1930 Toscanini conducts Boléro at the Paris Opéra, faster than Ravel likes. Backstage, in one report, Ravel says “That’s not my tempo” and Toscanini replies that at Ravel’s tempo it is not effective. Ravel answers: “Then do not play it.”

    18. The bass finally moves

      The closing passage breaks into E major after the long stay in C. Brass, winds and strings carry the change together. The drum continues through the eight-bar excursion.

    19. A crash in C

      Trombones slide, the percussion crashes, and the orchestra recovers C major. The ending takes only a few bars. Nothing in the long crescendo has prepared a gentle departure.
