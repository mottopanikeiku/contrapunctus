Igor Stravinsky / VI. Modernism

The Rite of Spring (Le Sacre du printemps)

1911–13 · Ballet in two parts; large orchestra

The solo bassoon at the opening plays a Lithuanian folk song; the metre of the last dance changes from bar to bar. Roerich helped devise the pagan ceremonies of the scenario and designed the 1913 production.

2 recordings · 32 min

In the Hall

  1. Part I. Adoration of the EarthColumbia Symphony Orchestra; Igor Stravinsky · 5–6 January 1960
    14:52

    Part I of The Rite of Spring, “Adoration of the Earth”, opens with a solo bassoon on a high C, and a few minutes later the strings play a repeated, stamping chord. Stravinsky conducts the Columbia Symphony Orchestra in this 1960 recording.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening bassoon solo

      The bassoon opens alone, high in its range, with a short melody that turns around C. Other wind instruments then enter one by one, each with a phrase of its own.

    2. Letter to Nicholas Roerich, 1911

      Stravinsky wrote to the painter Nicholas Roerich on 26 September 1911, while composing the Augurs of Spring: “The picture of the old woman in a squirrel fur sticks in my mind.” He pictured her running before the dancers, stopping them and breaking into their rhythm.

    3. Repeated chord with irregular accents

      The strings repeat a chord that combines F♭ major with an E♭ dominant seventh. Horns accent selected notes. The bars are written in 2/4, and the accents fall at irregular points within them.

    4. Ritual of Abduction, opening

      Presto. The violins play running figures, the high winds play short bursts and the horns hold notes underneath. Each phrase is short, and the next one cuts it off.

    5. E♭ and bass clarinets in octaves

      The E♭ clarinet and the bass clarinet play the same tune in octaves beneath trills in the flutes. The strings then enter, marked Sostenuto e pesante, with a heavy, even tread for the round dance.

    6. Recording sessions, Brooklyn, 1960

      The orchestra recorded the work in the ballroom of Brooklyn’s St George Hotel on 5 and 6 January 1960, with the composer conducting. The music had first been heard with Nijinsky’s choreography for the ballet.

    7. Two groups combined, figure 57

      Trombones and tubas play a heavy phrase. Faster figures follow in the upper register. Five bars into rehearsal figure 57 the two sound together, the low phrase continuing beneath the rapid upper parts.

    8. Procession, tubas and horns

      The tubas repeat their tune while the horns play a different pattern. Bassoons and low strings keep moving underneath. The phrases are of unequal length, so the points where they coincide shift as the procession continues.

    9. The Sage kisses the earth

      The Sage bends to kiss the earth. Bassoons and contrabassoon play over timpani and a low bass, and the strings hold a chord.

    10. Final dance of Part I, Prestissimo

      The bass drum starts the next dance. Winds and strings rise, and the full orchestra plays repeated figures. The marking is Prestissimo, and the dance follows the brief stillness of the Sage.

    Joins

    1. Introduction into the stamping chord

      The bassoon’s opening phrase returns and the other wind instruments drop out. Plucked violins start a short repeating figure. At Tempo giusto, second violins, violas, cellos and basses stamp the F♭-major/E♭-seventh chord, and horns mark the irregular accents.

    2. Augurs to Presto, violins running

      The heavy close of the Augurs breaks into Presto. Running violin figures and short high-wind calls cut across held horn notes, and the low strings play sharply repeated attacks.

    3. Clarinets in octaves under flute trills

      The fast dance stops. At Tranquillo, the flutes trill while E♭ clarinet and bass clarinet play in octaves; brass and strings are silent. The bass clarinet is now the lowest voice. The wind passage leads into the slower, heavy string chords of Sostenuto e pesante.

    4. Round dance to low brass, Molto allegro

      The round dance ends with its quiet music for flutes and clarinets. At Molto allegro the trombones and tubas enter, and the bassoons and horns reply. Low strings reinforce the tread. Within a few bars, quick high figures break across the heavy phrase.

    5. Tuba entry before the procession heading

      The tuba tune begins at figure 64, before the printed procession heading at figure 67. When the Sage arrives, the tuba is repeating its tune over bassoon and low-string figures, and the horns play a separate pattern above it. The march begins inside the crowd music. More players join, and the phrases of each part keep their own lengths.

    6. The procession breaks off into Lento

      The procession breaks off and the tempo changes to Lento at figure 71. Bassoons and contrabassoon repeat their low notes over quiet timpani and a solo bass. The strings then hold a hushed chord, coloured by harmonics, before figure 72. The Sage touches the ground here.

    7. Bass-drum roll at the Prestissimo

      The string chord is held under a pause. A bass-drum roll begins at Prestissimo, and horns and trumpets enter. An upward rush through the winds and strings follows. The bass drum continues to sound beneath the new figures.

  2. Part II. The SacrificeColumbia Symphony Orchestra; Igor Stravinsky · 5–6 January 1960
    16:47

    Part II of The Rite of Spring, The Sacrifice, opens with a quiet night scene, after which one girl is chosen to dance herself to death. Stravinsky conducts, and the final dance is built from short chords, with plucked cellos and basses playing on the chords.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Introduction to Part II

      The marking is Largo. Flutes and clarinets play soft repeated chords while the oboes hold long notes, and horns and muted trumpets join them over divided strings. The ballet scenario sets the second part at night.

    2. The elders in bearskins

      In Nicholas Roerich’s imagined ancient world, the elders wear bearskins and the girls are gathered into a circle. One girl remains in the middle. The elders watch her final dance.

    3. Six solo violas, figure 91

      At figure 91, Andante con moto, six solo violas play the melody together, each on a different note. Two solo cellos add high harmonics. Stravinsky marks the violas molto cantabile ma non forte.

    4. Sketchbook entry, Clarens, 1912

      On 17 November 1912, at the Châtelard Hotel in Clarens, Stravinsky wrote in his sketchbook: “with an unbearable toothache I finished the music of the Sacre”.

    5. Eleven chords and the Glorification

      Eleven repeated chords interrupt the circling, with strings, timpani and bass drum striking together. The Vivo follows, a fast dance in bars of changing length, opening with 5/8 and 9/8. The chosen girl stands at the centre while the others acclaim her fate.

    6. Evocation of the Ancestors

      Bass clarinets and low brass play the invocation, and high winds and brass follow with hard, repeated chords. The phrases are blunt and short.

    7. Cor anglais solo

      Horns, plucked strings and soft percussion play a repeating figure. Above it, the cor anglais plays a short phrase. The accompaniment continues beneath the solo.

    8. Concert performance, Paris, 1914

      On 5 April 1914 Pierre Monteux conducted The Rite of Spring in concert at the Casino de Paris. Afterwards admirers carried Stravinsky on their shoulders, less than a year after the uproar at the ballet’s premiere.

    9. Opening of the Sacrificial Dance

      The last dance begins with short, sharp chords. Cellos and basses play pizzicato beneath them. Rests separate the chords, and the next one enters before a regular pattern can form.

    10. Revision of the final dance, 1943

      In 1943 Stravinsky revised the bars, note values and scoring of the final dance. Much of the notation was doubled in size, so that quavers became crotchets. This 1960 performance uses that revision.

    Joins

    1. Six solo violas at Andante con moto

      The low horn phrases of the introduction die away. At Andante con moto, six solo violas begin a harmonised melody, with high cello harmonics around it and low strings beneath. The violas are marked molto cantabile ma non forte.

    2. Eleven chords into the Glorification

      Climbing runs lead into eleven hammering chords in strings, timpani and bass drum. The other winds and brass are silent for these chords. The Glorification then begins at Vivo, with short orchestral bursts and changing bar lengths.

    3. General pause before the summons

      A general pause separates the Glorification from the summons, between rehearsal figures 120 and 121. Bass clarinets and low brass enter over held low strings, followed by higher wind and brass chords. The new crotchet equals the preceding dotted quaver, so the pulse carries across the silence, and the phrases become broader.

    4. Invocation to Ritual Action at Lento

      The invocation slows to Lento, with descending figures in the bassoons. The Ritual Action opens with an accompaniment of low horns, plucked strings and percussion. A cor anglais melody enters above it.

    5. Into the final dance

      Winding phrases for clarinet and bass clarinet fade over the low, quiet accompaniment of the ritual. The final dance cuts in with short orchestral chords, sharpened by pizzicato cellos and basses. The bar lengths change and abrupt rests break up the chords. The girl’s solo begins over this irregular metre.
