Igor Stravinsky / VI. Modernism

The Rite of Spring (Le Sacre du printemps)

1911–13 · Ballet in two parts; large orchestra

The solitary bassoon at the start borrows a Lithuanian song; the last dance destroys the comfort of regular bars. Roerich helped invent its pagan ceremonies and designed the 1913 production.

2 recordings · 32 min

In the Hall

  1. Part I. Adoration of the EarthColumbia Symphony Orchestra; Igor Stravinsky · 5–6 January 1960
    14:52

    A bassoon reaches for a high C; a few minutes later, the strings stamp on a chord that seems to bruise the floor. Stravinsky conducts the Columbia Symphony Orchestra in this 1960 recording of his pagan spring.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The bassoon’s high C

      The bassoon begins high in its range, bending a little melody around C. Its soft, pinched voice has the stage to itself. Other winds gradually find their way in, each with a phrase of its own; the spring landscape fills with calls.

    2. The woman in squirrel fur

      Writing to the painter Nicholas Roerich on 26 September 1911, Stravinsky says: “The picture of the old woman in a squirrel fur sticks in my mind.” He imagines her running before the dancers, stopping them, breaking into their rhythm. The ballet is already moving in his head.

    3. One chord, uneven blows

      The strings repeat a chord made by pressing F♭ major against an E♭ dominant seventh. Horns strike selected notes harder. The written bars stay in two beats, yet the accents keep landing somewhere else: the dancers have a floor, and it keeps jolting under them.

    4. The abduction breaks loose

      Presto: the violins run, high winds cry out in short bursts, and the horns hold notes through the commotion. A phrase gets just enough time to show its face before another shoulders it aside. The dance feels crowded with bodies.

    5. Two clarinets, far apart

      The little E♭ clarinet and the bass clarinet sing the same tune in octaves beneath trilling flutes. Then the strings begin their broad, heavy tread. Stravinsky writes Sostenuto e pesante: the round dance carries its weight on every step.

    6. A ballroom in Brooklyn

      This orchestra assembles in the ballroom of Brooklyn’s St George Hotel on 5 and 6 January 1960. The composer stands at the podium. The music that once accompanied Nijinsky’s stamping dancers now fills a hotel ballroom for Columbia’s microphones.

    7. The tribes answer back

      Trombones and tubas put down a heavy phrase. Faster figures answer higher up. Soon the two are sounding together: the low music holds its ground while the upper parts rush across it. The rivalry is written into the orchestra.

    8. The tubas keep coming

      The tubas repeat their lumbering tune while horns insist on a different pattern. Bassoons and low strings keep moving underneath. The phrases have unequal lengths, so their meeting points shift as the Sage’s procession pushes forward.

    9. A kiss on the ground

      The Sage bends to kiss the earth. Bassoons and contrabassoon murmur over quiet timpani and a low bass; the strings hold a faint, strange chord. After all those feet and horns, the ceremony has almost nothing left to say aloud.

    10. The earth breaks loose

      The bass drum starts the next dance. Winds and strings sweep upwards, and the whole orchestra hurls itself into repeated figures. Prestissimo is the marking. The Sage’s brief stillness gives way to a dance with scarcely room for a breath.

    How it is joined

    1. The strings plant their feet

      The bassoon’s opening phrase returns as the other wind voices fall away. Plucked violins begin a short repeating figure. At Tempo giusto, second violins, violas, cellos and basses stamp the F♭-major/E♭-seventh chord; horns pick out the irregular accents. The wandering introduction has acquired a hard floor.

    2. The violins start running

      The heavy end of the Augurs breaks into Presto. Running violin figures and short high-wind calls cut across held horn notes; low strings supply sharply repeated attacks. The sudden quicker pace sends the dancers scattering before the earlier stamping has time to settle.

    3. Clarinets sing under trilling flutes

      The fast dance stops. At Tranquillo, flutes trill while E♭ clarinet and bass clarinet sing in octaves; brass and strings are silent. The bass clarinet is now the lowest voice. This little wind choir opens the door to the slower, heavy string chords of Sostenuto e pesante.

    4. Low brass answers the quiet

      The round dance ends with its quiet flute-and-clarinet music. Molto allegro brings in trombones and tubas, answered by bassoons and horns. Low strings reinforce the tread. Within a few bars, quick high figures break across the heavy phrase: the two tribes have met.

    5. The procession is already approaching

      The tuba tune begins before the printed procession heading. By the time the Sage arrives, it is repeating over bassoon and low-string figures, with horns carrying their own pattern above it. The march grows out of the crowd; its phrases keep their separate lengths as more players join.

    6. The procession falls silent

      The crowded procession falls away into Lento. Bassoons and contrabassoon repeat their low notes over quiet timpani and a solo bass. The strings then hold a hushed chord, coloured by harmonics. The Sage touches the ground in this sudden clearing.

    7. The bass drum breaks in

      The string chord hangs under a pause. A bass-drum roll begins Prestissimo; horns and trumpets enter, followed by an upward rush through winds and strings. The low drum keeps sounding beneath the new figures. The ceremony’s stillest moment opens straight into its fastest dance.

  2. Part II. The SacrificeColumbia Symphony Orchestra; Igor Stravinsky · 5–6 January 1960
    16:47

    After a hushed night scene, one girl is chosen to dance herself to death. Stravinsky conducts a final dance of short, savage chords, with plucked cellos and basses sharpening the blows.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Night in the orchestra

      Flutes and clarinets murmur soft repeated chords while the oboes hold long notes. Horns and muted trumpets add their voices; divided strings spread the sound further. The marking is Largo. After the first part’s pounding finish, this slow opening seems to find the dancers standing in darkness.

    2. The elders wear bearskins

      Nicholas Roerich’s imagined ancient world gives the elders bearskins and gathers the girls into a circle. One girl will remain in the middle. The elders wait for her last dance: the whole ceremony narrows down to a single body.

    3. Six violas in a circle

      Six solo violas carry the melody together, each on a different note. Two solo cellos add high harmonics. Stravinsky asks the violas to sing warmly without getting loud; the girls’ circling begins with a tenderness that the coming choice will destroy.

    4. A toothache in Clarens

      On 17 November 1912, Stravinsky writes in his sketchbook at the Châtelard Hotel in Clarens: “with an unbearable toothache I finished the music of the Sacre”. The grand ritual has a very ordinary witness: a composer who needs a dentist.

    5. Eleven blows choose her

      Eleven repeated chords interrupt the circling. Strings, timpani and bass drum strike together. Then Vivo releases a fast dance whose bars keep changing length. The chosen girl stands at the centre while the others acclaim her fate.

    6. The ancestors are summoned

      Bass clarinets and low brass begin the invocation; high winds and brass answer with hard, repeated chords. The phrases are blunt and short. This summons has no room for the circling melody’s gentleness.

    7. A cor anglais prayer

      Horns, plucked strings and soft percussion set out a quiet repeating figure. Above it, the cor anglais curls through a small, plaintive phrase. The accompaniment keeps going beneath the solo, as patient and implacable as the elders around the girl.

    8. Paris carries the composer

      On 5 April 1914, Pierre Monteux conducts The Rite of Spring at the Casino de Paris. This time it is a concert. Admirers carry Stravinsky on their shoulders afterwards, less than a year after the uproar at the ballet’s premiere.

    9. Chords cut into silence

      The last dance begins in short, sharp chords. Cellos and basses pluck underneath them, making each blow bite. Rests split the gestures apart; the next chord arrives before the body can settle into an easy pattern of steps.

    10. The composer rewrites the dance

      In 1943, Stravinsky rewrites the final dance’s bars, note values and scoring. Much of the notation doubles in size: quavers become crotchets. This 1960 performance uses that revision, giving the orchestra a later set of instructions for the girl’s ordeal.

    How it is joined

    1. Six violas start the circle

      The introduction’s low horn phrases subside. At Andante con moto, six solo violas begin a harmonised melody, with high cello harmonics around it and low strings beneath. The violas are marked molto cantabile ma non forte: full of song, still quiet. The circle starts to turn.

    2. Eleven chords break the circle

      Climbing runs lead into eleven hammering chords in strings, timpani and bass drum. The other winds and brass fall silent for these blows. Then Vivo begins the Glorification, with short orchestral bursts and changing bar lengths. The girl has been chosen; the music gives her no time to answer.

    3. A pause before the summons

      A general pause separates the Glorification from the summons. Bass clarinets and low brass enter over held low strings; brighter wind and brass chords answer them. The new crotchet takes the speed of the preceding dotted quaver, keeping a small pulse alive across the silence while the phrases grow broader.

    4. The cor anglais takes up the rite

      The invocation slows to Lento, with descending bassoon figures. The Ritual Action settles into a quiet accompaniment of low horns, plucked strings and percussion. A cor anglais melody rises over it. The busy summons has brought the elders into place; their ceremony can proceed at a measured tread.

    5. The last dance cuts in

      Winding clarinet and bass-clarinet phrases fade over the ritual’s quiet low accompaniment. The final dance cuts in with short orchestral chords, sharpened by plucked cellos and basses. Changing bar lengths and abrupt rests break up the blows. The girl’s solo begins on a floor that will not hold still.
