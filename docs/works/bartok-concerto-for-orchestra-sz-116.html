Béla Bartók / VI. Modernism

Concerto for Orchestra, Sz. 116

1943 · Sz. 116 · BB 123 · Orchestra

Koussevitzky commissioned it in memory of his wife Natalie and conducted the 1944 Boston première. The second movement gives each wind pair a different interval; Bartók lengthened the finale’s ending in 1945.

5 recordings · 37 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Introduzione — Andante non troppo; Allegro vivacePittsburgh Symphony Orchestra; Fritz Reiner, conductor · 1946
    9:55

    Cellos and basses climb through fourths; later, the brass pursue one another in a fugue. Fritz Reiner conducts the Pittsburgh Symphony in this 1946 recording of the concerto’s opening movement.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Fourth after fourth

      Cellos and double basses begin together, climbing in fourths. Muted violins enter above them, then a flute. The low phrase keeps returning as more of the orchestra wakes around it.

    2. Plunging into the unknown

      In October 1940 Bartók leaves Hungary for America. The move, he writes on the eve of leaving, is “like plunging into the unknown from what is known, but unbearable”. Columbia University gives him a modest research post in folk music, for a number of months.

    3. Two friends make a suggestion

      With Bartók ill and short of money in early 1943, his old friends, the violinist Joseph Szigeti and the conductor Fritz Reiner, suggest to Serge Koussevitzky that he commission an orchestral work in memory of his wife, Natalie.

    4. Koussevitzky comes to the hospital

      In 1943 Serge Koussevitzky visits Bartók in a New York hospital with a commission worth $1,000. Bartók accepts only half the money at first: he fears his health will prevent him from finishing the piece. The work is dedicated to Koussevitzky’s late wife, Natalie.

    5. The violins break loose

      Allegro vivace. The violins launch a quick, twisting tune, with horns and bassoons punching out replies beneath them. Oboes and clarinets add sharp chords. The long introduction has finally released a dance.

    6. An orchestra of soloists

      Bartók’s note for the Boston première explains the title: the work treats single instruments or groups in a “concertant”, soloistic manner. His first example of this “virtuoso” treatment is the brass fugato in this movement’s development.

    7. An oboe sings softly

      The oboe has a gentler tune, marked dolce, over rocking violins and cellos. Muted horns hold the harmony. Then clarinets take over, with the harp slipping between their phrases.

    8. Climbing through the orchestra

      The violins throw out the quick figure again. Trumpets answer; bassoons and clarinets pick it up, then oboes and flutes. Each new group starts before the previous one has finished.

    9. Pittsburgh before Chicago

      This is the first commercial recording of the Concerto: Reiner and the Pittsburgh Symphony make it on 4–5 February 1946, and it is issued in 1948. Reiner records the work again in Chicago for RCA on 22 October 1955.

    10. A clarinet, then cor anglais

      The clarinet sings over violins and violas, with most of the orchestra silent. The cor anglais takes over its winding line; a flute floats above it. The bass clarinet later adds its darker voice.

    11. The brass pursue one another

      The trombones start a fugue, the same theme entering at different heights before the earlier parts have finished. Trumpets join the pursuit. Bartók marks the entries ben marcato: every note needs a clear edge.

    12. The gentler tune returns first

      The great brass passage ends, and the clarinet brings back the Tranquillo tune over strings and harp. Bartók reverses the earlier order: the lyrical tune returns before the fast violin theme.

    13. The violins head for home

      The strings accelerate and recover the first fast tune. Horns keep repeating their short figure below. What began with a long, lonely bass phrase ends with the orchestra in full chase.

    How it is joined

    1. The violins take over

      The introduction gathers speed through rising woodwind figures and a repeated low-string phrase. At Allegro vivace, both violin sections take the lead. Horns, bassoons and lower strings answer with accented notes; oboes and clarinets add short chords between the violin phrases.

    2. The oboe softens the pace

      Bartók gradually relaxes the pace into Tranquillo. The oboe begins a tune marked piano, dolce. First violins and cellos rock between chords, two muted horns sustain above them, and the harp adds a short answer. The double basses are silent.

    3. The quick figure passes upwards

      At Tempo I, unmuted violins bring back the quick figure above a timpani stroke. Trumpets answer it. Bassoons and clarinets enter together, followed by oboes and flutes, while horns, trombones and tuba hold a chord beneath the climbing lines.

    4. A clarinet remains after the outburst

      The orchestra broadens its last loud phrase, then drops away. At Tranquillo, a solo clarinet sings over first violins and violas, soon joined by second violins. The cellos and double basses rest. Cor anglais later takes the melody, with flute above.

    5. Trombones enter one by one

      The bass clarinet and strings slow down at the end of the quiet passage. Tempo I restores the quick figure in bassoons and low strings. The trombones begin their overlapping entries, marked ben marcato; trumpets join above them as the strings fall silent.

    6. The brass stop, the clarinet sings

      The full orchestra broadens its repeated chords before Tranquillo. Quiet rocking strings and harp notes replace the brass. Two horns put on mutes; the clarinet enters with the lyrical tune, marked espressivo, above plucked double basses.

    7. The strings accelerate into the return

      The wind figures thin out over repeated low-string notes. At the accelerando, the violins take off their mutes and begin climbing. Horns repeat a short figure with the cellos and basses; the quick violin theme returns as the pace gathers.

  2. II. Giuoco delle coppie — Allegretto scherzandoPittsburgh Symphony Orchestra; Fritz Reiner, conductor · 1946
    5:50

    Bartók sends his wind players out two by two, each pair keeping its own distance between notes. A brass chorale interrupts their parade; when the bassoons return, a third player tags along.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A drum without snares

      The side drum begins alone, with its snares switched off. Seven bars of dry taps introduce the first pair: two bassoons, moving together a sixth apart over plucked strings.

    2. Oboes closer together

      The oboes keep only a third between them. Their tune has its own quick turns and little pauses; the bassoons’ wide-stepping duet has left the stage.

    3. A kolo for two bassoons

      The opening bassoon duet, a major sixth apart, imitates a Yugoslav kolo, a round dance, in the account of Bartók’s editor Benjamin Suchoff. In America Bartók has spent months at Columbia University working on South Slavic folk music.

    4. The scherzo gets an exemption

      For the Boston première in 1944, Bartók describes the concerto’s progress from sternness to a “life-assertion” in the finale. He makes one cheerful exception: this “jesting second movement”.

    5. Same beat, brass choir

      The trumpets take off their mutes for a five-part brass chorale. The notes stretch out, yet Bartók marks the same tempo. Beneath the solemn chords, the drum carries on with its little pattern.

    6. A third bassoon joins

      The original bassoon pair returns, still a sixth apart. A third bassoon wriggles beneath them in quicker notes. The plucked string accompaniment is back too.

    7. Reiner’s old teacher

      Fritz Reiner studied in Budapest with Bartók among his teachers. His biographer Philip Hart writes that Reiner had “an understanding and devotion of Bartók’s music that no other conductor of his time equaled”.

    8. The drum closes the door

      The strings stop and the winds hold a last chord. The side drum, still without snares, finishes the movement on its own: the same dry taps that let everybody in.

    How it is joined

    1. The bassoons step out

      After seven bars of side drum, the two bassoons begin their duet in sixths. Second violins, violas, cellos and double basses pluck a spare accompaniment. The drum dwindles to isolated taps and falls silent; the bassoons keep the same Allegretto scherzando pace.

    2. The trumpets take off their mutes

      The trumpet duet and strings settle on held notes while the drum resumes its opening rhythm. At lo stesso tempo, two unmuted trumpets and two trombones begin the chorale, with tuba below. The beat stays unchanged beneath the longer notes.

    3. Two bassoons acquire a companion

      Short flute, oboe and clarinet figures stir above the last held horn and tuba chord. The bassoons reclaim their opening tune over plucked strings. This time a third bassoon runs beneath the duet, marked staccato.

    4. The wind chord outlasts the strings

      The strings stop after their last held chord. Flutes, oboes, clarinets and bassoons repeat a chord, then sustain it over the horns. The side drum returns underneath, without snares. As the winds fall silent, its taps dwindle to pianissimo.

  3. III. Elegia — Andante non troppoPittsburgh Symphony Orchestra; Fritz Reiner, conductor · 1946
    7:50

    The concerto’s central lament grows out of a low string line, with flute and clarinet runs flickering above it. After the great outbursts, a piccolo is left calling into the dark.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The basses remember

      The double basses begin, with timpani sounding quietly beside them. Cellos, violas and second violins enter in turn. The phrase reaches back to the concerto’s first movement: the same slow climb from the bottom of the orchestra.

    2. Eleven notes in a breath

      Flute and clarinet sweep up and down in groups of eleven notes. The harp adds glissandos, while the oboe holds a much longer line. The strings barely move beneath them.

    3. A lugubrious death-song

      In his note for the Boston première Bartók calls this movement a “lugubrious death-song”. The concerto, he writes, moves from the sternness of the first movement and this death-song to the life-assertion of the last.

    4. A summer at Saranac Lake

      In 1943 Bartók works on the concerto at Saranac Lake in upstate New York, recovering from illness. Koussevitzky’s commission has reached him in hospital. By autumn the five movements are finished.

    5. The cellos pluck

      A sharp horn chord sinks into a long, quiet note. Beneath the wavering oboes and clarinets, solo cellos pluck quick figures. The violins and violas tremble close to the bridge; the tam-tam rings softly behind them.

    6. The violas find a voice

      The violas sing a connected line, marked molto espressivo, legato. Bartók also asks for molto rubato: the phrase can pull against the beat. Muted violins and two harps answer in shorter figures.

    7. A quasi boycott

      At the end of 1942 Bartók complains: “The quasi boycott of my works by the leading orchestras continues; no performances either of old works or new ones.” Within months Koussevitzky’s commission reaches him in hospital.

    8. High chords, moving bassoons

      Flutes and violins hammer out high chords while clarinets, bassoons, violas and cellos keep winding below. The upper parts strike; the lower ones refuse to stand still.

    9. Back to the low strings

      The opening phrase returns in basses, cellos and violas. Flute and clarinet runs begin circling again. The muted horn holds a quiet note beneath them.

    10. Pittsburgh, 1946

      Fritz Reiner conducts the Concerto in Pittsburgh on 18 and 20 January 1946, then records it on 4–5 February. Bartók has been dead since 26 September 1945.

    11. The piccolo calls alone

      The piccolo’s short calls reach above a muted horn and the last string notes. As the strings fall silent, piccolo and timpani carry the ending into pianissimo.

    How it is joined

    1. The wind runs spread through the strings

      The flute and clarinet runs spread into several parts. A muted horn holds its note over cellos and violas, the violas bowing close to the bridge. Divided violins take up the winding figures. Piccolo calls and short brass phrases draw the music towards its first full lament.

    2. The horn chord leaves plucked cellos

      The horns strike a chord and immediately drop to piano. Oboes and clarinets rock between neighbouring notes. Solo cellos pluck rapid figures below them; the double basses are silent. Upper strings bow near the bridge, with quiet tam-tam strokes behind the held horn sound.

    3. The violas begin to sing

      The wind oscillations broaden and stop. A few plucked cello and bass notes clear the way for the violas, marked molto espressivo, legato. The new Poco agitato phrase has freedom to linger: Bartók adds molto rubato. Muted violins and the two harps answer it.

    4. High chords cut across the melody

      At Tempo I, cor anglais and clarinets start a rising line, joined by bassoons. High flute and violin chords then cut across it. Violas and cellos continue the lower melody while the upper parts repeat their accented chords.

    5. The low-string phrase returns

      The outburst dwindles into flute and clarinet runs over a fading violin note. Low strings recall the opening phrase, marked piano. At Tempo I, the woodwind runs resume above held strings and a muted horn, with quiet timpani underneath.

  4. IV. Intermezzo interrotto — AllegrettoPittsburgh Symphony Orchestra; Fritz Reiner, conductor · 1946
    4:29

    An oboe tune and a broad viola song are enjoying themselves when a clarinet barges in with a march from Shostakovich’s Seventh Symphony. The trombones answer with a pair of rude slides.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. An uneven little song

      After a short string introduction, the oboe sings a tune whose bars keep changing length. Plucked viola and cello notes put small bumps in its path.

    2. The violas sing out

      Calmo: the violas have a broad, singing melody, with two harps supplying the chords. The violins take it up, and the cor anglais joins them. For a while, everybody has time to finish a sentence.

    3. An uninvited march

      The clarinet brings in the march tune from Shostakovich’s Seventh Symphony. The strings hurry it along, and the brass breaks in. Then the trombones slide upwards: an orchestral raspberry.

    4. Whose joke is it?

      Bartók’s march parodies the “invasion” theme of Shostakovich’s Seventh Symphony, which itself refers to “Da geh’ ich zu Maxim” from Lehár’s operetta The Merry Widow. Whether Bartók mocks Shostakovich, Lehár or both has been argued ever since.

    5. The song after the commotion

      The broad song returns in muted violins and violas, over harp chords and plucked lower strings. Bartók writes non crescendo as it rises. It keeps its voice down.

    6. A ballet left on paper

      In January 1944 Bartók signs a contract for a ballet version of the concerto with American Ballet Theatre. He prepares a piano score; the conductor Antal Doráti helps with the negotiations. The ballet never reaches the stage.

    7. The flute takes its time

      A little flute cadenza loosens the beat. Horns and strings hold their notes underneath while the flute turns back on itself, pauses and runs on. The final exchanges are short and quiet.

    How it is joined

    1. Violas take the longer phrase

      The flute and oboe finish their short phrases. At Calmo, the violas begin a melody marked cantabile, with two harps striking chords beneath it. The cellos and double basses fall silent for the first stretch; timpani supply the low notes.

    2. The oboe recovers its tune

      The harp chords stop as the broad song ends. At Tempo I, the oboe resumes the opening tune, with flute and clarinet answering it. Violins and violas hold chords; plucked cellos and double basses keep the accompaniment light.

    3. The clarinet brings a march

      Repeated string figures start beneath a held horn note; the cellos and basses pluck their accompaniment. The clarinet introduces the march. An accelerando carries it into Più mosso, where muted trumpet and trombone chords cut across it. Two upward trombone slides follow.

    4. Mutes soften the returning song

      The wind chatter dwindles and the tuba holds its last note. The Calmo song returns in muted violins and violas. Harp chords and plucked second violins, cellos and double basses accompany it; Bartók asks the rising phrase to remain quiet.

    5. The strings leave the winds alone

      The oboe begins its old tune over the last harp and string chord. The accompaniment drops out. Flute, oboe and clarinet exchange short phrases alone before the strings rejoin them at Tempo I.

    6. The flute stretches the beat

      A slowing phrase and a fermata leave room for the flute. Its quasi Cadenza runs over held horn and string chords, with the double basses silent. The score allows the flautist to repeat a small turn freely before a descending run and the return to a tempo.

  5. V. Finale — Pesante; PrestoPittsburgh Symphony Orchestra; Fritz Reiner, conductor · 1946
    9:03

    The horns give a summons, and the strings answer with strummed chords and a headlong Presto. Bartók’s finale sends the whole orchestra chasing its tunes towards F major.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Horns, then string guitars

      The horns call out, marked Pesante — heavily. After a long pause, violas and cellos put their bows aside and strum chords like guitars. The second violins start the Presto, playing at the tips of their bows.

    2. Trouble with the finale

      On 26 September 1943 Bartók writes to his son Péter: “The first 4 are already finished. Now I am having trouble with the last, which for certain reasons is the most difficult.” He is working on the concerto for most of each day.

    3. The bassoon steps forward

      The rushing figures drop away, leaving a bassoon with a sharply pointed tune. Bartók marks it ben marcato. A second bassoon joins, then clarinets, while muted strings tick away underneath.

    4. The best orchestra piece

      Koussevitzky conducts the première with the Boston Symphony on 1 December 1944. Bartók tells a pupil that Koussevitzky calls it “the best orchestra piece of the last 25 years”, the works of his idol Shostakovich included.

    5. Harps and quiet violins

      The pace eases to Un poco meno mosso. Two harps strike chords above divided violins; short wind phrases begin darting between the string lines. Some violinists bow the tune while their neighbours pluck the accompaniment.

    6. Bartók’s pencil in Reiner’s score

      Reiner conducts from a score corrected by Bartók himself. Paper strips carrying revised passages are pasted over the old ones, and the new ending is inserted after the last page. The score survives at Northwestern University.

    7. Horns above the running strings

      The horns stretch a phrase across the violins’ quick notes. Cellos and double basses repeat a short figure below. Trumpets and trombones join with accented replies; then the fast line races through the strings again.

    8. The violins put on mutes

      Tranquillo. The first violins sing softly, while the other strings play muted chords. Flutes, oboes, clarinets and horns answer in longer notes. Bartók keeps asking for greater calm.

    9. Long notes over running strings

      The strings keep their flowing scales while winds and brass lay longer phrases above them. Muted trombone and tuba add a dark line; later the horns and trumpets broaden the sound. The double basses hold notes beneath the rushing bows.

    10. Two endings

      Koussevitzky’s radio broadcast of 30 December 1944 uses Bartók’s original ending. Reiner’s Pittsburgh recording, made in February 1946, uses the longer alternative ending that Bartók supplied.

    11. The ending Bartók added

      Reiner plays Bartók’s expanded ending. A fresh acceleration sends the tune between strings and winds; horns and trombones sweep upwards in glissandos. Side drum and cymbal reinforce the final rush into F major.

    How it is joined

    1. Bows aside, then Presto

      The horn call ends on a long pause. Violas and cellos put their bows aside, then strum repeated chords, marked quasi chitarra. An acceleration leads to Presto: the second violins run above those chords, with muted horns, timpani and plucked bass notes underneath.

    2. A bassoon emerges from the rush

      The woodwinds stop their running figures one group at a time. Violins, violas and cellos carry on briefly, then dwindle to piano. A bassoon enters with a pointed tune marked ben marcato; another bassoon joins above it. Muted strings beat out short notes beneath them.

    3. Harps introduce the quiet string entries

      Un poco meno mosso begins with quiet harp chords and a held second-violin note. First violins join in divided parts. The low strings rest; plucked notes within the violin sections supply the accompaniment as short flute, clarinet and bassoon phrases appear above.

    4. The horns stretch above the violins

      The first violins keep running while unmuted horns begin a longer phrase. Cellos and double basses repeat a three-note figure below. Wind chords and brass accents join the crescendo; after a sharp chord, the quick figure starts again in bassoons and low strings.

    5. Muted strings soften the violin tune

      The pace relaxes to Tranquillo. First violins sing piano, dolce, accompanied by muted second violins and violas, with quiet cellos and basses below. Woodwinds answer in long phrases. Horns sustain across the change and later put on mutes.

    6. Low brass beneath the string scales

      Violin, viola and cello scales continue over held double-bass notes. Clarinets sustain above them while muted trombone and tuba enter quietly. The tune passes through cor anglais and other winds; horns and unmuted trumpets join as the strings climb.

    7. A longer road to F major

      The revised ending adds another acceleration. Short wind and string phrases pass over horn and trumpet notes; the double basses rest until the last page. They rejoin the final upward rush, with glissandos from horns and trombones and a side-drum roll. The orchestra lands on F major.
