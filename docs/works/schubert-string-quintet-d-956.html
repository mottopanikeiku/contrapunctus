Franz Schubert / III. Romanticism

String Quintet, D. 956

1828 · D. 956 · Two violins, viola and two cellos

The Adagio, in E major, breaks into F minor; the C major scherzo is interrupted by a slow trio in D♭ major. The first public performance took place in 1850.

13 min

Recording

  1. II. AdagioIsaac Stern, Alexander Schneider, violins; Milton Katims, viola; Pablo Casals, Paul Tortelier, cellos · 1952
    13:14

    The Adagio of Schubert’s String Quintet in C major, D. 956, opens with three inner voices playing while a violin and a pizzicato cello frame them; an F minor middle section follows, and the opening returns altered. Pablo Casals and his Prades colleagues perform.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening melody, inner voices

      The second violin, viola and first cello play a slow melody in E major. Around them, the first violin plays short, dotted phrases and the second cello plucks the bass.

    2. Letter to Probst, 2 October 1828

      On 2 October 1828 Schubert, then thirty-one, wrote to the Leipzig publisher Heinrich Albert Probst offering three piano sonatas, Heine songs and a new quintet. The quintet was about to be tried out. The letter is Schubert’s only mention of the work.

    3. Jonathan Blumhofer on the Adagio, 2024

      The composer Jonathan Blumhofer found this Adagio closely related to the slow movement of Schubert’s B♭ major piano sonata. He wrote that its nocturnal mood foreshadows later movements by Mahler and Bartók.

    4. Middle section in F minor

      The music moves to F minor, a semitone above E, fortissimo. The second cello changes to the bow, and the first violin and first cello play a long melody together. The inner parts accompany them with off-beat chords.

    5. Violin and cello in octaves

      The first violin and first cello play the melody in octaves, set wide apart. Beneath them the second cello plays an accompaniment, so the two cellos have different parts.

    6. First performance, Vienna, 1850

      The first known public performance took place in Vienna on 17 November 1850, twenty-two years after Schubert’s death. The work was published in 1853. Schubert’s manuscript is lost, and the print is the only source.

    7. Brahms’s F minor string quintet, 1862

      In autumn 1862 Brahms sent Clara Schumann the first three movements of an F minor quintet for two violins, viola and two cellos, the ensemble Schubert used. He later destroyed it and recast the music for two pianos, then for piano quintet. The middle section of this Adagio is in F minor.

    8. Return of E major

      E major returns. The three inner players take up their broad melody again, while the second cello now plays running demisemiquavers and exchanges flourishes with the first violin.

    9. Casals at Prades

      Pablo Casals made the small French town of Prades his home. Its first festival, in 1950, was devoted to Bach. The 1952 festival, recorded by Columbia and heard here, was the first given entirely to chamber music.

    10. Percy Scholes on chamber music

      In The Oxford Companion to Music, Percy Scholes found “an intimacy” in the best chamber music “that comes from the impression of a conversation amongst the participants”. His poetic definition of it was “The Music of Friends”. The Columbia sleeve for this recording quoted him.

    11. Tully Potter in The Strad

      Reviewing this recording in The Strad, Tully Potter called Tortelier’s pizzicatos in the Adagio “superb”. He wrote that “groans from Casals” “only increase the tension”, and he heard “tremendous thrust” in the central section.

    12. F minor in the final bars

      The last three bars turn briefly to F minor, the key of the middle section, before closing in E major.

    Joins

    1. E major to F minor, fortissimo

      E major breaks off and the music moves to F minor, fortissimo. The second cello changes from plucked bass to bowed motion. The first violin and first cello play the long melody in octaves, with syncopated chords between them.

    2. Separated chords return to E major

      The F minor passage ends in quiet, separated chords. These lead back to E major and the sustained inner melody. The first violin and second cello now exchange quick flourishes around the other three players.

    3. The F minor chord returns

      In the last three bars, F minor again briefly interrupts E major. The five strings return to the home key at once and close softly.
