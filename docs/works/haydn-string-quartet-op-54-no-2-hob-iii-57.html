Joseph Haydn / II. Classical

String Quartet, Op. 54 No. 2, Hob. III:57

1788 · String quartet

4 recordings · 18 min

In the Hall

  1. I. VivaceThe Danish String Quartet · May 2015
    4:23

    The first violin strides into C major, then Haydn stops all four players in their tracks. The Danish String Quartet plays a movement whose silences are as pointed as its running notes.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Four players stop short

      The first violin throws out a bold phrase above held notes. Then everyone stops. Haydn writes five-bar phrases and pauses for the whole quartet; even the silences refuse to fall into a comfortable four-square pattern.

    2. A violinist goes into business

      Johann Tost leaves the Esterházy orchestra in 1788 and takes six new Haydn quartets to Paris. These include Op. 54. He arranges their publication with Sieber; his name stays attached to the music.

    3. The violin starts again

      The quiet close in G major is followed by a loud new statement of the first violin’s opening phrase. The lower strings change the chords beneath it, and the development sends the familiar figure off through new keys.

    4. C major, with its shadow

      The first violin recovers its opening phrase in C major. The cello’s A♭ returns too, colouring the phrase just as it did at the start. Haydn brings back the interruption along with the tune.

    5. The first violin’s fire

      In all twelve Tost quartets the first violin has an unusually prominent role. Rosemary Hughes hears it at its best in this quartet’s first movement, “with its bold, striding gestures and big sonorities”, where the virtuosity lends the music “fire and eloquence”.

    6. The gentle tune comes home

      The second-group tune, marked piano dolce, returns in C major. Its lilting phrases provide a softer answer to the first violin’s opening declaration.

    7. Tel Aviv, May 2015

      The Danish String Quartet plays this at the Tel Aviv Museum of Art in May 2015, and the museum’s music channel films the concert. Three of the players met as children at a summer camp for amateur musicians, where they played football and chamber music; the Norwegian cellist Fredrik Schøyen Sjölin joined them in 2008.

    How it is joined

    1. A quiet close, then a loud restart

      The exposition closes quietly in G major. The first violin restarts its opening phrase forte over sustained lower strings, with altered notes in the accompaniment pulling it away from G. Haydn puts the bold opening back on stage before allowing it to settle in a key.

    2. The cello leads back to C

      The bass settles on G before the first violin brings back the opening phrase in C major. A few bars later the cello moves to A♭, restoring the unexpected harmony beneath that phrase. The return includes both the bright opening and its darkened answer.

  2. II. AdagioThe Danish String Quartet · May 2015
    3:08

    The Danish String Quartet plays a C minor lament in which the first violin seems to improvise above three players singing a hymn. Haydn leaves the ending suspended and sends the music straight into the minuet.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A violin above a hymn

      The second violin, viola and cello sing a slow melody in C minor. Above them the first violin winds through quick, elaborate flourishes, as if the four players have quite different ideas about how much time a phrase needs.

    2. Three steady companions

      The lower instruments hold their notes while the first violin runs on. Its little turns and sudden leaps stretch across the chords; the accompaniment keeps its patient pace.

    3. Tost takes the quartets

      In late 1788 Johann Tost heads for Paris carrying six new Haydn quartets and two symphonies. Until March he has been the Esterházy orchestra’s principal second violin; now he is doing business with the publisher Sieber.

    4. A wild Hungarian lament

      Haydn’s biographer Rosemary Hughes hears the first violin break into “impassioned arabesques above the dark and brooding melody, in the manner of a wild Hungarian lament”. The first violin has an unusually prominent role in all twelve of the Tost quartets, Opp. 54, 55 and 64.

    5. The unfinished ending

      The closing passage is marked morendo — dying away. A held G leaves C minor waiting for an answer. Haydn writes attacca: the C major minuet follows without a break.

  3. III. Menuetto: Allegretto — TrioThe Danish String Quartet · May 2015
    3:20

    The C major minuet steps straight out of the Adagio’s suspended ending. Its C minor Trio brings all four players together on one stern melody, then pulls them apart into uneasy chords.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Straight into the dance

      Haydn joins the Adagio to this C major minuet without a pause. After the slow movement’s wandering violin, the four players have a dance to share, marked Allegretto.

    2. Four voices become one

      The Trio turns to C minor, the Adagio’s key. All four instruments play the same melody; tense augmented chords disturb it. The dance has acquired a grim face.

    3. An earlier C major quartet

      Haydn has tried this close connection between slow movement and minuet before, in the C major Quartet, Op. 20 No. 2. Here the Trio also borrows the Adagio’s C minor, so the earlier movement keeps returning inside the dance.

    4. Back to the minuet

      Menuetto da capo sends the quartet back to the beginning. C major returns, with the first violin leading the dance once more. Its rising scale and final chords supply the ending.

    5. Another Adagio to come

      The quartet ends with another surprise: a second Adagio, with a Presto in its middle. Laurie Shulman says two slow movements shift “the entire balance and emotional weight” of the quartet.

    How it is joined

    1. A rising scale, then C minor

      Rising scales carry all four instruments to the minuet’s fortissimo C major close. The Trio changes the signature to three flats and begins in C minor with all four players sharing the melody. The cello joins the tune instead of supplying a separate bass.

    2. The Trio hands back to the Menuetto

      The Trio ends in C minor. Menuetto D.C. sends the players straight back to the C major opening: the first violin takes the tune, and the other three resume their accompaniment. The rising scales now lead to the movement’s final stop.

  4. IV. Finale: Adagio — Presto — AdagioThe Danish String Quartet · May 2015
    7:17

    Haydn ends the quartet with an Adagio, interrupted by a brief Presto that seems ready to run away with the finale. The slow music comes back and has the last word, pianissimo.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A second Adagio

      The finale begins slowly, with the two violins phrasing together above the viola and cello. Haydn has already given this quartet one Adagio. He makes room for another, and saves the quick music for the middle.

    2. A murmur between the violins

      The second violin and viola repeat their notes in semiquavers. Above that quiet murmur the first violin speaks in short phrases, while the cello has a moving line of its own.

    3. What did Tost get?

      On 5 April 1789 Haydn writes to the Paris publisher Jean-Georges Sieber about the man carrying his music: “And did he also sell you the 6 quartets, and for what sum?” Tost’s business dealings are making the composer anxious.

    4. Paris first, then London

      Sieber publishes the six quartets in Paris in June 1789, as Opp. 54 and 55. Longman and Broderip’s London parts follow in July 1789 and January 1790, marked “Performed at the Professional Concert Hanover Square 1789”, a series Haydn was then supplying with new music.

    5. Foreshadowing Beethoven and Mahler

      Laurie Shulman calls this finale, with its second Adagio, an experiment that “foreshadows the late quartets of Beethoven and even the symphonies of Bruckner and Mahler”. A Presto in the middle suggests a conventional finale, and the return of the Adagio takes it back.

    6. The quick finale arrives

      The Presto breaks out in C major. Both violins move together over off-beat cello notes; the viola waits before joining them. Haydn punctures the rush with pauses for all four players.

    7. The Adagio takes it back

      After the Presto’s emphatic chords, the Adagio returns with its opening phrase. The quick music has had its say. The quartet still has a slow ending to play.

    8. Four players fading

      The last Adagio grows quieter, until the four players reach the final C major chord at pianissimo. The word Fine sits beneath this small, unhurried ending.

    How it is joined

    1. A fermata, then the Presto

      The Adagio stops pianissimo under a fermata. The Presto starts in C major, with the violins together and the cello off the beat; the viola rests. A held chord separates the two speeds, with no connecting run to ease the change.

    2. Loud chords, then the Adagio again

      Repeated accented chords bring the Presto to a fermata. Haydn writes Adagio again, and the first violin recovers the opening phrase with its rests and grace notes. The return stays in C major and is much shorter than the first Adagio.
