Pierre Boulez / VII. After 1945

Le Marteau sans maître

1952–55; revised 1957 · Mezzo-soprano and six instrumentalists; poems by René Char

The nine interleaved movements are based on three poems, and the instruments change partners constantly. After the voice’s final wordless humming, flute and deep metallic percussion end the work.

2 recordings · 31 min

In the Hall

  1. I–V. First half, to “Bel édifice et les pressentiments”, first versionMarie-Thérèse Cahn, voice; J. Tiberge, flute; Collot, viola; A. Stingl, guitar; J. Delecluse, vibraphone; P. Naudin, xylorimba; A. Cavaille, percussion; Pierre Boulez, conductor · 1956 season
    14:10

    Pierre Boulez conducted this recording of the complete Le Marteau sans maître for Vega, with Marie-Thérèse Cahn as the voice. The two sides of the LP are published as two parts, and this one holds movements I to V.

    Listening notes
    1. Movement I: two duos

      Avant “L’Artisanat furieux” begins as two duos, flute with vibraphone and guitar with viola. After a short viola cadenza the four instruments mingle more freely.

    2. Domaine musical, 1956 season

      The sleeve calls this the fourth concert of the Domaine musical’s 1956 season, recorded under the auspices of the International Music Council with UNESCO’s help. Boulez had conducted the French première, with Marie-Thérèse Cahn, at the Petit Marigny on 21 March 1956.

    3. Movement II: flute, then xylorimba

      Commentaire I de “Bourreaux de solitude” is roughly in three parts. A long flute solo over the soft murmur of the other instruments is followed by a harsher section led by the xylorimba, then a brief return of the flute in a less relaxed mood.

    4. Central African wood percussion

      Boulez told Jed Distler in 2000 that the start of this second piece was “very much under the influence of central African music”, with its wood percussion and xylophone. In 1945–46 Mady Sauvageot, curator of music collections at the Musée Guimet, had introduced him to recordings from Africa and Asia.

    5. Nine parts, unequal silences

      Pierre Souvtchinsky’s sleeve note says the work divides into nine parts separated by silences of unequal length, each with its own meaning. The next movement follows one of those pauses.

    6. Movement III: voice and flute

      In “L’Artisanat furieux” the voice sings Char’s poem, which begins “La roulotte rouge au bord du clou”, with the flute alone. Lonchampt notes that this scoring cites “Der kranke Mond” from Schoenberg’s Pierrot lunaire, and that the melodic lines alternate between superposition and overlap.

    7. Movement IV: no flute

      Commentaire II is the only movement without the flute. It moves in blocks of different lengths, separated by stops and sometimes filled with vibraphone resonance. The viola plays pizzicato until a section where it takes up the bow, and the triangle enters at the end of a rapid passage.

    8. Movement V: first “Bel édifice”

      “Bel édifice et les pressentiments”, first version, alternates unstable instrumental sections with entries for the voice over a more homophonic accompaniment. It is the first of two settings of the same Char poem; the second ends the work.

    9. First performance, Baden-Baden, 1955

      Hans Rosbaud conducted the first performance on 18 June 1955 at the International Society for Contemporary Music festival in Baden-Baden, with Sybilla Plate as soloist. The work was chosen after a hard struggle within the French selection committee.

  2. VI–IX. Second half, from “Bourreaux de solitude” to “Bel édifice et les pressentiments”, second versionMarie-Thérèse Cahn, voice; J. Tiberge, flute; Collot, viola; A. Stingl, guitar; J. Delecluse, vibraphone; P. Naudin, xylorimba; A. Cavaille, percussion; Pierre Boulez, conductor · 1956 season
    17:00

    The second side of Boulez’s Vega recording of Le Marteau sans maître holds movements VI to IX: the sung “Bourreaux de solitude”, two instrumental pieces, and the closing “Bel édifice et les pressentiments”, for which the whole ensemble plays.

    Listening notes
    1. Movement VI: “Bourreaux de solitude”

      The sung setting of Char’s “Bourreaux de solitude”, the poem that the earlier Commentaires comment on. Lonchampt calls it the most austere piece of the work, and hears complementary rhythms among the instruments at the start, perhaps inherited from Webern’s Symphony, Op. 21.

    2. The companion disc, C 30 A 66

      Vega’s companion disc C 30 A 66, headed as the fourth concert of 1956, holds extracts of Le Marteau with Webern’s Symphony and Lieder, Nono’s Incontri and Stockhausen’s Kontra-Punkte, all conducted by Boulez.

    3. Movement VII: three instruments

      Après “L’Artisanat furieux” is the shortest movement. Lonchampt finds it close to the first movement, written for three instruments, with a rhythmic play that is easy to follow.

    4. Movement VIII: Commentaire III

      Commentaire III resembles Commentaire I: Lonchampt hears the flute solo and the harsh double notes of the xylorimba laid over one another. The percussion has a formal role, playing claves, then a double bell, then bongos.

    5. Grand Prix du Disque

      L’Express listed this disc on 12 November 1959 as the complete version of the work, conducted by Boulez with M. T. Cahn, and noted that it had won the Grand Prix du Disque of the Académie Charles-Cros.

    6. Boulez and René Char

      The three poems come from René Char’s collection Le Marteau sans maître. Boulez had already set Char in Le Visage nuptial, composed in 1946.

    7. Movement IX: the whole ensemble

      In the second “Bel édifice et les pressentiments” the whole ensemble plays together for the only time in the work. Lonchampt hears a free, recitative-like tempo at the start and quotations from earlier movements.

    8. Voice and flute without words

      The voice is soon freed from the text and uses Sprechgesang at moments. It then alternates wordless phrases with the flute, supported by the ensemble except for the percussion.

    9. Flute and deep percussion

      The movement closes with the flute against two low tam-tams and a gong. Lonchampt describes the mood as recollected (“recueillie”).
