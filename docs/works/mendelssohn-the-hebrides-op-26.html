Felix Mendelssohn / III. Romanticism

The Hebrides, Op. 26

1830–32 · Op. 26 · Orchestra

The opening phrase was sketched in Tobermory on 7 August 1829, before the visit to Fingal’s Cave. Several revisions lie between that page and the familiar B minor overture.

11 min

The recording

  1. Allegro moderatoMusopen Symphony
    11:23

    A Scottish seascape begins in the violas, cellos and bassoon: a six-note falling phrase, with the violins holding still above them. Mendelssohn turns that small phrase into the storms and long swells of The Hebrides.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Six notes below the violins

      Violas, cellos and first bassoon play a falling phrase in B minor. It starts after a quaver’s rest: F♯, D, C♯, D, B, F♯. Above it, the violins hold two quiet notes. The sea is already moving before anything rises to the surface.

    2. A letter from the Hebrides

      On 7 August 1829, Mendelssohn writes to his family: “In order to make you understand how extraordinarily the Hebrides affected me, the following came into my mind there.” Beneath the words are twenty-one bars of music. His visit to Fingal’s Cave comes the next day.

    3. Cellos and bassoons sing

      The second tune opens out in D major, marked cantabile. Cellos and bassoons sing it together while the upper strings keep turning over their quick notes. The melody takes its time; the water beneath it never stills.

    4. London hears the islands

      On 14 May 1832, The Hebrides reaches a London audience on a programme that also includes Mendelssohn’s overture to A Midsummer Night’s Dream. He goes back to the score after the concert and revises it again.

    5. Winds call across the strings

      In the development, forceful wind calls break across quiet, rocking strings. The opening phrase passes through the violas and cellos, sometimes reduced to a few notes. The trumpets answer with a sharply repeated rhythm.

    6. Turner’s steamship at Staffa

      Turner visits Staffa in 1831. In his painting Staffa, Fingal’s Cave, a steamship passes the island’s basalt cliffs: smoke and spray fill the air together. Mendelssohn has been there two years earlier.

    7. The low phrase comes back

      The falling phrase returns in B minor, with violas and cellos marked tranquillo. The violins keep moving above it. After the development’s wind calls and rushing scales, the low melody sounds close and familiar again.

    8. A clarinet in B major

      The long second tune returns in B major. A clarinet sings it almost alone above held string chords, marked tranquillo assai — very calm. A second clarinet joins later in the phrase. The busy accompaniment of the first statement has fallen still.

    9. Animato breaks the calm

      Animato, in tempo: the violins start running again, and the oboe picks out a short, detached phrase. The clarinet’s long song is over. B minor returns, and the quick notes spread through the orchestra.

    10. A few notes remain

      The orchestra draws back. A clarinet recalls the falling phrase, the flute answers, and the strings pluck their last few notes. The ending leaves B minor hanging in a remarkably small space.

    How it is joined

    1. Cellos and bassoons take the long tune

      The music reaches D major, B minor’s relative major. The double bass supplies a quiet D as cellos and bassoons begin the new melody, marked cantabile. Violins and violas continue their semiquavers, now sempre pp, always very soft.

    2. Wind calls against rocking strings

      The D-major closing music breaks off at rehearsal C. Horns and trumpets repeat their clipped rhythm as the strings fall to piano. The violins rock between notes while the low strings recall the falling phrase; wind chords interrupt at fortissimo, then immediately diminish.

    3. The low strings recover the opening

      The rushing scales subside, and the violas and cellos bring back the B-minor opening phrase, marked piano tranquillo. The double bass holds beneath them. Above, the violins continue rocking pairs of notes while the last wind calls fade.

    4. The clarinet finds B major

      The strings descend and grow quieter, then hold a B-major chord. Over their low B, the clarinet begins the second tune, tranquillo assai. D♯ takes the place of B minor’s D♮; the bassoons and cellos that first sang the tune now leave it to the clarinet.

    5. Animato sets the violins running

      The clarinets slow and diminish over the strings. Animato, in tempo restarts the violins’ semiquavers, with a detached oboe phrase above a held F♯ in the low strings. The harmony turns back towards B minor, and the bass soon begins to move again.

    6. The clarinet has the last recollection

      The final B-minor passage contracts from full orchestra to a clarinet’s falling phrase and a brief flute answer. Strings change to pizzicato and pianissimo. Low B remains in the bass; the last plucked notes are followed by a written pause.
