Frédéric Chopin / III. Romanticism

Ballade No. 4, Op. 52

1842 · Op. 52 · Piano

The principal tune gathers ornaments and answering voices at each return. Quiet chords make a final pause before the coda.

13 min

The recording

  1. Andante con motoFrank Lévy
    12:42

    A quiet F-minor tune keeps returning with more voices around it, until five soft chords stop everything before the coda. Frank Lévy plays Chopin’s Fourth Ballade, his most intricate conversation at the piano.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. C major opens the door

      Repeated Gs sound high in the piano over C-major harmony. The melody falls gently, then rests on E. For seven bars, Chopin keeps the Ballade’s F minor waiting outside.

    2. The F-minor tune

      The first theme winds through F minor, its melody repeatedly leaning on notes a semitone from their neighbours. A quiet accompaniment rocks beneath it. When the phrase returns, Chopin adds little turns and faster notes inside the same line.

    3. Charlotte on the title page

      Chopin gives the Fourth Ballade to Baroness Charlotte de Rothschild in its dedication. He writes it in 1842, a year after the Third; this is the last of his four works to bear the title.

    4. A swaying B♭-major song

      The second theme sings in B♭ major, piano e dolce — softly and sweetly. The right hand plays a chordal melody above a gently swaying bass. After the first theme’s curling line, these phrases move in broader steps.

    5. The opening in A major

      The introductory melody returns in A major. Its repeated notes and falling phrase are familiar, but the bass has moved from C to A. The piano then unfolds a delicate cadenza, slowing over an A-major chord.

    6. The tune answers itself

      The first theme returns in D minor, and the left hand answers the right after half a bar. More voices join as the music finds F minor again. The tune now has to sing through its own echoes.

    7. D♭ major across the keyboard

      The second theme returns in D♭ major. The left hand sweeps through arpeggios while the right sings above it in chords. The gentle B♭-major song has become a passage that reaches across the whole piano.

    8. Five chords over C

      After the great rush, five quiet chords sound over a bass C. The upper notes change; the bass stays. The last C-major chord hangs in the air for several bars, then leaves a silence before the coda.

    9. The coda breaks loose

      A low F releases the coda. Semiquaver triplets dart between the hands, with sharp accents and chromatic turns. The soft C-major chord has led straight into the Ballade’s most strenuous F-minor writing.

    How it is joined

    1. C major opens onto F minor

      The introduction holds E over C-major harmony and a fermata. A C pickup starts the first theme, which curls through F minor at a tempo. The left hand settles into a regular accompaniment beneath the quieter melody.

    2. The hands settle into B♭ major

      The first theme’s running notes settle into a B♭-major song, piano e dolce. The right hand sings in chords, with D at the top, while the left hand rocks between bass notes and supporting chords.

    3. A major recalls the first page

      The developing passage reaches A major, with A in the bass. The introduction’s repeated-note melody returns above it. Its last phrase slows into a cadenza marked dolcissimo, whose arpeggios spread the A-major chord across both hands.

    4. One hand answers the other

      The A-major cadenza ends under a fermata. The right hand starts the first theme in D minor, marked legato; half a bar later, the left hand repeats its opening notes. The answering lines lead back towards F minor.

    5. D♭ major carries the second song

      The first theme’s decorated phrases lead into the second song in D♭ major. The left hand opens out into wide arpeggios, and the right-hand melody rides above them in chords. The gently swaying rhythm survives the much larger accompaniment.

    6. The bass stops on C

      The rush ends with a C-major chord and a fermata over the rest. Five quiet chords follow. A low C remains beneath their changing upper notes, and the last chord, C major again, is held across several bars.

    7. The held C releases a low F

      The held C-major chord ends in silence. A low F begins the coda, and both hands leap into semiquaver triplets. The dominant harmony has resolved to F minor, but the quiet waiting has become rapid, accented piano writing.
