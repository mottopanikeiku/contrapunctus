Modest Mussorgsky / III. Romanticism

Pictures at an Exhibition

1874 · Piano

Written after the memorial exhibition for Viktor Hartmann and dedicated to Stasov. The walking Promenade eventually enters the pictures, returning among the catacombs and bells.

15 recordings · 32 min

In the Hall

  1. Promenade — Allegro giusto, nel modo russicoSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    1:41

    A trumpet steps into the gallery alone, and the brass answer. Mussorgsky’s Promenade walks in unequal bars — five beats, then six — as he moves between Hartmann’s pictures.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. One trumpet, then brass

      The trumpet begins on G, then falls to F before rising to B♭. Five beats fill the first bar, six the next. The brass answer in chords: the same tune suddenly has a whole company walking with it.

    2. A gallery for Hartmann

      Saint Petersburg, 1874. Vladimir Stasov gathers Hartmann’s drawings, paintings and architectural designs for a memorial exhibition. Mussorgsky turns his visit into a piano suite that June. The Promenade is his own figure moving through the rooms.

    3. The first picture waits

      The walking bass brings the Promenade home to B♭ major. Its last chord leads straight into Gnomus, the first picture: a wooden nutcracker that springs into alarming life.

    How it is joined

    1. The bass reaches down to G♭

      The tune reaches A♭, then a low G♭ sounds beneath it. E♭ hangs inside the chords while the bass steps down to F and back. The melody keeps its familiar rising shape, now with these darker notes underneath.

    2. The bass opens towards F major

      The bass shifts from G♭ to B♭, then descends through A and G to F. F major chords spread above it. The short phrases lengthen into a continuous walk, with a bass note for almost every beat.

  2. I. Gnomus — VivoSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    2:42

    A wooden nutcracker springs into alarming life. Low strings scurry, woodwinds creep, and the gnome keeps stopping dead in its tracks.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Lurches and full stops

      Low strings and woodwinds make a short, crooked dash, then stop on a held note. Another dash, another halt. Mussorgsky writes fermatas over notes and rests alike: the gnome can freeze halfway through a movement.

    2. A nutcracker for the tree

      Hartmann’s gnome is a wooden Christmas-tree toy for the Artists’ Club. Nuts go into its mouth. Mussorgsky takes this small domestic monster and gives it whole stretches of silence in which to prepare its next attack.

    3. Semitones under the shiver

      The wind tune stretches out above shivering strings. Under it, the bass slips down by semitones. Mussorgsky marks this passage Meno mosso: slower, with the gnome’s awkward steps enlarged into a grim procession.

    4. The gnome’s last dash

      After another fermata, the gnome bolts. Mussorgsky marks the final run velocissimo and con tutta forza — as fast as possible, with all possible force. An E♭ minor chord slams the picture shut.

    How it is joined

    1. A rest before the creeping tune

      A whole bar of rest, held under a fermata, separates the first lurches from the descending phrase. E♭ sounds low beneath it. Ravel starts the new line in upper woodwinds, then adds celesta and swooping strings.

    2. Four beats for the heavy tune

      After another fermata, the metre changes from three beats to four. Poco meno mosso, pesante asks for slower, heavy steps; the bass jumps from E♭ to A. The broad wind tune repeatedly collides with the opening’s quick three-beat scurry.

    3. The bass slips down in semitones

      The next Meno mosso expands the tune into high octaves. The bass descends chromatically beneath it, passing through E♭, D♭ and C. Ravel sets the wind melody against shivering strings.

    4. Low trills, then the creeping tune

      The broad octave tune breaks off. Three-beat bars return, with low trills alternating between E♭ and A. Over them comes the earlier descending phrase, answered by muted brass. Poco a poco accelerando starts the gnome moving faster again.

    5. A fermata releases the final run

      A forceful A♭ minor chord acquires an extra F♭, then stops under a fermata. The final run leaps away, marked velocissimo, and climbs to E♭ minor. One short chord and a rest finish it.

    2. High notes, slower steps

      The next phrase begins E♭–F–C, turning down through the walking tune. The lower line carries it first; a chordal answer then climbs above it. The five- and six-beat bars continue, with E♭ chords recurring inside the A♭-major key.

    2. The low steps become high octaves

      Low E♭ and A♭ notes mark the last heavy steps. The tune then climbs into high octaves at ritardando, while the accompanying chords move underneath. It ends very softly on E♭ major with B♭ above, followed by the instruction attacca.

  4. II. Il vecchio castello — AndanteSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    4:45

    A troubadour sings outside a medieval castle. In Ravel’s orchestration an alto saxophone carries the song, while a repeated G♯ holds its place beneath the wandering melody.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The castle’s one bass note

      The bass keeps returning to G♯, rocking in 6/8. Above it, the bassoon begins the scene and the alto saxophone carries the long song. Its phrases wander far above a note that stays in one place.

    2. A singer outside the castle

      Hartmann travels in Italy and paints its buildings. Mussorgsky puts a singer in front of one: a troubadour, standing outside a medieval castle. The long melody becomes his song.

    3. D major against G♯

      The tune expands into octaves and chords. Even a D-major chord sounds above the persistent G♯ bass: its D presses a tritone against the note below. The castle’s drone survives every change of harmony.

    4. Ravel chooses a saxophone

      In the summer of 1922, Ravel orchestrates Pictures for Serge Koussevitzky. For this medieval singer he chooses the alto saxophone. Koussevitzky introduces the new score in Paris that October.

    5. The song breaks into fragments

      The opening melody returns near the end, then breaks into short phrases separated by rests. G♯ continues underneath. A final upward call reaches the home note, and the bass has the last word.

    How it is joined

    1. New chords over the same bass

      The repeated G♯ continues beneath a melody enlarged into octaves. A-major and D-major chords pass over it, their notes rubbing against the bass. The harmony moves, but the lowest part keeps the same rocking rhythm on G♯.

    2. The first song returns over G♯

      The opening song returns in G♯ minor, marked espressivo, over the same G♯ bass. Its long line breaks into isolated chords and rests. The final upward leap reaches G♯, while the bass continues a little longer beneath it.

  6. III. Tuileries (Dispute d’enfants après jeux) — Allegretto non troppo, capricciosoSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    1:02

    Children quarrel in the Tuileries gardens: a two-note taunt, a scurry, another taunt. Mussorgsky fits the scene into thirty bars, with a smoother little tune between the squabbles.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Children answer back

      Two-note calls alternate with scurrying semiquavers. Ravel puts the taunts in the winds and the quick replies in the strings. Mussorgsky’s marking is capriccioso: these children keep changing their minds.

    2. A tune between the squabbles

      The middle tune has longer notes and a sly upward slide: A♯, B, then B♯. Beneath it, D♯ falls to D♮ while F♯ stays put. Even the smoother music cannot quite sit still.

    How it is joined

    1. The taunts become a smooth phrase

      A rising run leaves space for a longer melody beginning on A♯. Beneath it sit D♯ and F♯; then D♯ slips down to D♮ as the melody rises through B to B♯. The short calls have become a slurred phrase, with semitone changes inside the B-major key.

    2. The children return to their taunt

      The short two-note calls return, marked piano, followed by the opening figure at pianissimo. Quick runs still interrupt them. The last one lands on a short B-major chord, and the remaining beats of the bar are silent.

  7. IV. Bydło — Sempre moderato, pesanteSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    2:19

    An ox-cart on enormous wheels rolls past. Ravel gives its song to a tuba, an unexpectedly tender voice above the trudging bass.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A tuba sings

      The tuba sings in G♯ minor above a bass that trudges on four quavers to the bar. Mussorgsky marks the music pesante — heavy. Ravel starts it quietly, with room for the cart to approach.

    2. The wheels keep turning

      The middle passage rises into higher chords. A major and D major brighten the tune, while the bass climbs in steps and the quavers keep turning beneath it. The cart’s pace scarcely changes.

    3. Enormous wooden wheels

      The title is Polish for cattle. Behind the music is Hartmann’s ox-drawn wagon on enormous wheels, a subject Mussorgsky explains privately to Stasov. He leaves the wagon out of the title: just Bydło.

    4. The cart goes on

      The melody breaks into fragments and the bass steps grow quieter. Ravel leaves harp and plucked double bass at the end. The wheels still turn as the cart disappears.

    How it is joined

    1. A major above the climbing bass

      The G♯ minor phrase finishes, and A major chords appear much higher above the bass. The bass climbs A–B–C♯–D while the quaver accompaniment continues. Broad chords replace the single singing line without lightening the cart’s tread.

    2. The song returns with full force

      D♯ in the bass leads back to G♯ minor. The first melody returns in octaves, marked con tutta forza — with all possible force. The accompanying quavers continue, and poco allargando asks the heavy tread to broaden.

    3. The tune sinks towards the bass

      The tune drops back into its low octave and dwindles to fragments. G♯ minor remains, with the same quavers underneath. Ravel pares the ending down to harp and plucked double bass; Mussorgsky’s final instruction is perdendosi, dying away.

  8. Promenade — TranquilloSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    0:41

    After the ox-cart, the Promenade returns quietly in D minor. Flutes begin the walk; oboes and bassoons take their turn before the tune drops into the bass.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Flutes, oboes and bassoons

      The walking tune passes from flutes to oboes and bassoons, marked Tranquillo. D minor darkens it. Bars of five, six and seven beats make the steps unequal, even in this quiet stretch.

    2. Mussorgsky’s face in the music

      In June 1874, writing to Vladimir Stasov as he composes Pictures, Mussorgsky says: “My physiognomy can be seen in the interludes.” The man walking between Hartmann’s pictures is the composer himself.

    How it is joined

    1. The walk drops into the bass

      The D-minor tune moves down into bass octaves, beginning A–B♭–E. The upper part answers in octaves too. After this heavier exchange, the music dwindles to isolated A notes and a quick flicker of chords before the next picture.

  9. V. Ballet des poussins dans leurs coques — Scherzino, vivo leggieroSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    1:18

    Hartmann dresses child dancers as chicks still inside their eggs. Mussorgsky makes them hop and trill; Ravel adds woodwinds, harps and plucked strings.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Still inside the shell

      Tiny grace notes peck at short chords. The marking is Vivo, leggiero — lively and light — and the dance keeps hopping away from F major and back. Ravel gives the chicks woodwinds, harps and plucked strings.

    2. Children dressed as canaries

      In Saint Petersburg in 1871, children dance in Petipa’s ballet Trilby, to music by Julius Gerber. Hartmann’s costume design encloses them in eggshells, their legs sticking out underneath and canary heads perched on top like helmets.

    How it is joined

    1. Trills above a held F

      The scherzino finishes with a held D♭ and a clipped C. The trio starts a high F trill over a low F that lasts eight bars. Between them, short offbeat notes keep the dance moving. The tempo stays quick.

    2. The little dance starts again

      The trio’s last bass notes, C and A, lead back to the opening F major chords. Mussorgsky writes da capo: back to the start. The little grace notes return, but this time the dance goes straight to the coda, skipping the trio.

    3. D♭ hangs in the air

      A D♭ lasts across the first two bars of the coda. The last little flick moves from G♯ to A, and an F major chord closes the dance pianissimo. Mussorgsky leaves a rest under a fermata after it.

  10. VI. “Samuel” Goldenberg und “Schmuÿle” — AndanteSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    2:08

    Two portraits become an unequal conversation: Goldenberg has a weighty unison tune, Schmuÿle a nervous stream of repeated notes. Ravel eventually sets one muted trumpet against the whole string section.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Goldenberg speaks in unison

      Goldenberg’s phrase is broad and heavily accented. Low woodwinds and strings play it together, without a separate accompaniment. The leap from G♭ to A — an augmented second — catches in the middle of his declaration.

    2. Schmuÿle’s muted trumpet

      Schmuÿle arrives on a muted trumpet, repeating notes in quick little groups. The score changes to Andantino. Each attempt to carry the phrase onward seems to catch on another repeated note.

    3. Two portraits at home

      Mussorgsky owns both Hartmann drawings behind this scene. One shows a wealthy Jewish man in a fur hat, the other a poor Jewish man from Sandomierz. Hartmann spends a month in that Polish town in 1868; Mussorgsky brings the two separate portraits face to face.

    4. A more abrupt dismissal

      Mussorgsky’s piano piece ends on a long final note. In Ravel’s scoring Goldenberg dismisses Schmuÿle more abruptly: a warmer, pleading idea is cut short by an emphatic ending.

    How it is joined

    1. A muted trumpet takes the floor

      Goldenberg’s phrase rests on D♭. That note stays beneath the next passage, but the melody jumps high and breaks into repeated notes. Ravel hands it to a muted trumpet over woodwind chords; Mussorgsky changes the marking from Andante to Andantino.

    2. Goldenberg interrupts without stopping the trumpet

      Andante, grave brings back Goldenberg’s tune in the lower parts. Schmuÿle’s repeated D♭ continues above it. Ravel gives Goldenberg the strings and leaves Schmuÿle on one muted trumpet: the two men now speak at once.

    3. A pause before the last plea

      An accented chord cuts off the repeated notes, and a fermata holds the silence. Over B♭ in the bass, the next phrase slows con dolore — with sorrow. The opening’s clipped figures return for an emphatic final dismissal.

  11. VII. Limoges. Le marché (La grande nouvelle) — Allegretto vivo, sempre scherzandoSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    1:40

    The market at Limoges is full of gossip: a missing cow, new false teeth, a conspicuously red nose. Mussorgsky packs the chatter into quick notes that Ravel passes between violins and woodwinds.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The violins have news

      A busy violin phrase sets the market talking. Woodwinds take it up; horns add emphatic interruptions, and celesta sharpens the chatter. Mussorgsky marks it sempre scherzando — playful throughout — with accents continually landing inside the stream of semiquavers.

    2. A cow and porcelain teeth

      In his 1874 manuscript Mussorgsky sketches the market’s news in French: a man recovers his cow, a woman acquires porcelain teeth, another man’s nose remains as red as a peony. He crosses the little scene out. The gossip stays in the music.

    3. The floor opens

      A fermata interrupts the chatter. The last four bars have a slower pulse, Meno mosso, but their notes are shorter still: semidemiquavers pour across the orchestra. The score accelerates at the end and plunges straight into the catacombs.

    How it is joined

    1. D major interrupts the chatter

      The E♭ phrase ends and a D major chord, with F♯ in the bass, starts the middle passage. The semiquavers continue. Soon the bars shrink from four beats to three, bringing each new accent round more quickly.

    2. A chromatic slide back to E♭

      Repeated Ds hammer through the end of the middle passage. Beneath them, the bass runs down in semitones from C♯ to E♭. The opening E♭ phrase returns at a gentler dynamic, still chattering in semiquavers.

    3. A held rest before the plunge

      Three accented figures end in a rest under a fermata. Then Meno mosso, sempre capriccioso begins over B♭ in the bass, with E♭ major chords broken into semidemiquavers. The last bar accelerates and rises chromatically; attacca carries it straight into the catacombs.

  12. VIII. Catacombae (Sepulcrum romanum) — LargoSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    1:53

    Hartmann explores the Paris catacombs by lamplight; Mussorgsky answers with slow chords that strike and linger. Ravel gives their echoes to brass, low woodwinds and double basses.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Stone and brass

      The first notes stand almost motionless: B, then G, each held under a fermata. Ravel scores the vault in brass, low woodwinds and double basses. Abrupt changes from fortissimo to piano make one chord sound like the echo of another.

    2. Three men underground

      Hartmann puts himself inside this watercolour. He stands with a friend and a guide in the Paris catacombs; the guide carries a lamp. To their right is a pile of skulls. The artist who visits the dead will soon be dead himself.

    3. F♯ beneath the last chords

      The bass settles on F♯ while the chords above it keep changing. Even the last chord leaves the harmony unsettled. The score carries straight on into Cum mortuis, where the Promenade returns among these skulls.

    How it is joined

    1. G major turns to G minor

      The descending bass reaches F♯ and stops under a fermata. G follows, with B♮ above it; then B♮ falls to B♭. G major turns to G minor inside the same slow procession of brass chords.

    2. The bass stops on F♯

      After the large E♭ and C major chords, F♯ settles in the bass. An inner line climbs through B♯, C♯, D, E and E♯. The last chord hangs under a fermata, then diminishes; the F♯ remains unresolved as the score leads into Cum mortuis.

  13. Cum mortuis in lingua mortua — Andante non troppo, con lamentoSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    1:51

    The Promenade becomes a lament beneath trembling strings. In his manuscript Mussorgsky pictures Hartmann leading him towards skulls that glow from within.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The walker mourns

      The Promenade’s familiar tune returns in B minor, marked con lamento. Ravel puts it among quiet woodwinds and trembling strings. The melody still has the shape of a walk, but each phrase now seems to hesitate before taking another step.

    2. The skulls begin to glow

      In his 1874 manuscript Mussorgsky writes that Hartmann’s spirit leads him towards the skulls: “the skulls are illuminated gently from within”. The title means “with the dead in a dead language”. The visitor in the gallery has entered his friend’s picture.

    3. A quiet B major

      The high F♯ tremolo stays in place while the lower phrases fall silent between their answers. The last chord contains D♯: B minor has become B major. Mussorgsky leaves it under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. F♯ stays above the quiet chords

      The tremolo stops wandering and settles on high F♯. Below it, the score marks tranquillo: long chords alternate with rising F♯ major arpeggios in the bass. The melody grows shorter, with rests between its replies.

  14. IX. La cabane sur des pattes de poule (Baba-Yaga) — Allegro con brio, feroce — Andante mosso — Allegro moltoSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    3:32

    Hartmann designs a clock shaped like a hut on chicken legs; Mussorgsky sends its witch flying through the air in a mortar. Baba Yaga’s furious ride runs straight into The Great Gate of Kyiv.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The witch stamps twice

      F♯ and low G collide, with a whole bar of silence after them. Soon the Gs keep hammering. The marking is feroce — ferocious — and Ravel sends a trumpet theme through the racket, answered by other brass.

    2. Flute and a creeping bass

      In the Andante mosso, a flute flits above the slower line of bassoon and double bass. The score begins this middle passage with G and E rocking back and forth. Harp, celesta and strings gather around the low tune.

    3. A clock on chicken legs

      Hartmann’s sketch is a design for a metal clock: a little hut on chicken legs, decorated with cocks’ heads. Mussorgsky adds the owner. Baba Yaga flies about in a mortar, hunting for victims.

    4. A witches’ sabbath before

      Mussorgsky has written a sabbath before. His St John’s Night on the Bare Mountain, finished on St John’s Eve 1867, was refused by Balakirev. Orrin Howard of the LA Phil finds in Baba Yaga’s ride Mussorgsky’s “best Bald Mountain pictorialism”.

    5. The witch rides again

      Allegro molto: the hammering Gs return in two-beat bars. The trumpet’s strident tune and answering brass are back. The quiet middle has left the witch with plenty of energy for another chase.

    6. Up towards the gate

      The closing semiquavers run through octave after octave and climb to G. There is no pause at the top: attacca sends that G straight into the E♭ major opening of The Great Gate of Kyiv.

    How it is joined

    1. Hammered G becomes a rocking third

      The hard repeated Gs shrink into a quiet figure rocking between G and E. Andante mosso slows the pulse. Ravel brings flute, bassoon and double bass forward, with harp and celesta around them; the heavy brass retreat.

    2. G breaks out of the tremolo

      B, D♯ and G tremble under a fermata at the end of the middle. A forceful G starts Allegro molto, back in two-beat bars. The repeated-note figure rebuilds the witch’s stride, and the brass tune follows.

    3. The octaves climb into E♭ major

      The short phrases become a continuous stream of semiquavers in octaves. After sweeping down, they climb to G, the third of the E♭ major chord that opens the finale. The last bar is marked attacca: the gate appears before the witch’s flight can stop.

  15. X. La grande porte de Kiev — Allegro alla breve. Maestoso. Con grandezzaSkidmore College Orchestra, per the Free Music Archive and Internet Archive; not named on the Commons page
    5:28

    Hartmann’s gate never rises in Kyiv, but Mussorgsky builds it in E♭ major. Brass, a chant and pealing bells turn the last picture into a public ceremony.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The gate in brass

      The brass open in E♭ major with a tune built to carry across a square. Mussorgsky’s directions are Maestoso and Con grandezza — majestic, with grandeur. Broad chords hold the melody up while softer woodwinds answer.

    2. A helmet above the gate

      Hartmann’s design has tinted brick, an imperial eagle and a three-storey belfry with a cupola shaped like a Slavic helmet. The planned stone gates of Kyiv remain on paper. Mussorgsky supplies the bells.

    3. Dedicated to Stasov

      Mussorgsky finishes the suite on 22 June 1874. Five days later he inscribes the title page with a tribute to Vladimir Stasov, and a month after that adds a note that he means to publish it. The suite appears only in 1886, five years after his death, in Rimsky-Korsakov’s edition.

    4. Scales around the brass

      The gate theme returns in E♭ major. Strings and woodwinds pour scales around the broad brass melody, moving up and down while the tune keeps its stately pace.

    5. Ravel’s Paris première

      In the summer of 1922, Ravel turns Mussorgsky’s piano suite into an orchestral score for Serge Koussevitzky. Koussevitzky conducts it in Paris on 22 October. In 1930 he records it with the Boston Symphony Orchestra.

    6. The walker among the bells

      The Promenade returns amid the bell figures. The tune that first belonged to one trumpet now sounds inside the finale’s great gathering. Above and below it, quick notes keep the bells ringing.

    7. The gate takes larger steps

      The main theme broadens at Meno mosso, sempre maestoso — slower, still majestic. Its chords now sound against groups of three half-notes. The bass and upper parts answer across several octaves.

    8. The bass moves underfoot

      Repeated chords keep ringing while the bass moves from A♭ to B♭ and C♭. The same three-part pulse continues through each change of harmony. The chords start to swell again.

    9. E♭ from top to bottom

      The last proclamation grows broader still: Grave, sempre allargando. Low E♭ supports the great chords, and the final pages stretch across the whole range of the orchestra. The suite ends on E♭.

    How it is joined

    1. A♭ minor inside the gate

      The broad E♭ major statement ends, and an A♭ minor chord begins the chant. The score marks it senza espressione, without expression: plain notes moving together. Ravel exchanges the brass-led tune for quieter woodwinds.

    2. E♭ major returns with running octaves

      The chant ends on the bare fifth A♭–E♭. E♭ major returns forcefully beneath a descending scale, marked energico. Ravel keeps the main tune in brass while strings and woodwinds race around it.

    3. The chant returns a fifth higher

      The running octaves climb to B♭ and stop. The chant begins again, now in E♭ minor, a fifth above its first appearance. The long notes and senza espressione marking remain, with G♭ in place of the gate theme’s G♮.

    4. Low bells start to overlap

      The hymn ends on E♭ and B♭. Low A♭ and C♭ now alternate beneath tolling upper notes. Triplets enter, then quavers: the bell figures overlap more closely as the music gathers towards the Promenade.

    5. The scales stop; the gate broadens

      A descending scale runs to low C and stops under a fermata. Meno mosso, sempre maestoso brings the gate theme back in E♭ major. Its first chord has G in the bass; broad triplets now carry the melody across the orchestra.

    6. A♭ starts the final climb

      The gate theme settles on E♭. The bass then moves to A♭ beneath an F minor seventh chord, repeated in broad triplets. B♭ and C♭ follow in the bass as the chords keep ringing; a crescendo starts the final climb.
