Dmitri Shostakovich / VI. Modernism

Symphony No. 5, Op. 47

1937 · Op. 47 · Orchestra

The Largo is scored for divided strings, without brass. The finale ends in D major on a loud, repeated conclusion.

4 recordings · 41 min

In the Hall

  1. I. ModeratoPhilharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London; Artur Rodzinski, conductor · 1954, London; Westminster LP issued 1956
    14:35

    This is the first movement, Moderato, of the Symphony No. 5 in D minor, Op. 47, conducted by Rodzinski. Low strings play a phrase that the violins answer, the movement builds to a march for brass and drums, and it ends with the celesta.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening string exchange

      Cellos and basses leap from D to B♭, and the violins follow. Rests separate the phrases as they pass across the orchestra. Winds and brass do not play.

    2. Pravda editorial, 28 January 1936

      On 28 January 1936 Pravda printed an unsigned attack on Shostakovich’s Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District, headed “Muddle Instead of Music”. The opera was driven from Soviet theatres. Shostakovich began the Fifth more than a year later, in April 1937.

    3. Start of work, 18 April 1937

      Shostakovich began direct work on the symphony on 18 April 1937. He drafted the first three movements quickly. The finale and the full score took longer.

    4. Long violin melody, rehearsal 9

      From rehearsal 9 the first violins play a long melody, marked pp espressivo, over repeated notes in the lower strings. Harp chords mark the harmony beneath them.

    5. First performance, Leningrad, 1937

      On 21 November 1937 Yevgeny Mravinsky conducted the première in the Great Hall of the Leningrad Philharmonic. Shostakovich’s old teacher Maximilian Steinberg wrote in his diary: “The ovation was stupendous.”

    6. Piano entry, low register

      The piano enters low down, playing short, hard-edged figures with the cellos and basses. The horns hold longer notes above.

    7. Absence of title and dedication

      The Fifth has no dedication and no descriptive title, though Shostakovich liked titles. Harlow Robinson suggested that after the Lady Macbeth scandal Shostakovich may have judged it safer to give his critics nothing to misread.

    8. Première, 21 November 1937

      According to one listener at the première, the audience rose from their seats one by one during the finale until the whole hall was standing, and Mravinsky held the score high above his head. In her diary the artist Lyubov Yakovleva wrote that everyone repeated the same thing: “he retaliated and retaliated well.”

    9. Development climax, brass and strings

      Trumpets and trombones play heavy chords over repeated notes in the strings, and the movement becomes a march. Percussion joins the brass.

    10. Lyrical tune, flute and horn

      The lyrical tune returns in the flute and horn, over harp and quiet repeated strings. The violin section played this phrase earlier. Here two solo players pass it between them.

    11. Shostakovich on the symphony, 1938 and 1956

      In January 1938 Literaturnaya gazeta printed Shostakovich’s remark that his new work “could be called a lyrical-heroic symphony”. His 1956 score introduction gave its subject as “the shaping of the individual”, with “man with all his experiences” at the centre of a work “lyrical in essence from start to finish”.

    12. Coda, celesta scales

      The coda reduces the orchestra to solo violin, harp and muted strings. On the last page the celesta plays rising scales while the bass and inner strings, marked morendo, die away.

    Joins

    1. Violin melody over repeated strings

      The winds drop out and the strings play very softly. Harp chords and repeated notes in the lower strings set up a steady accompaniment. Above them the first violins begin their long melody, marked pp espressivo.

    2. The piano takes over the bass

      The harp’s sustained chords end, the violin line descends and stops, and the piano begins clipped figures in its low register. Cellos and double basses join it, and the horns then play a slower phrase above the moving bass. The piano is marked una corda, with the soft pedal.

    3. Flute resumes the long melody

      A slowing passage ends as the brass dies away. At Più mosso, harp chords and repeated string notes return very softly. Flute and horn share the lyrical melody above them. The heavy orchestral bass is silent.

    4. Muted strings under the final solos

      The score returns to Moderato, now at crotchet = 42. Muted violas, cellos and basses play the opening rhythm beneath a flute solo. Harp and solo violin follow. The celesta’s ascending scales come on the last page.

  2. II. AllegrettoPhilharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London; Artur Rodzinski, conductor · 1954, London; Westminster LP issued 1956
    4:28

    The Allegretto is the scherzo and second movement of Shostakovich’s Symphony No. 5 in D minor, Op. 47. Cellos and basses open it with a heavy dance figure, and a solo violin follows with a lighter one.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening, cellos and double basses

      Cellos and double basses begin alone, in A minor. The tune is in three beats to a bar, with short notes and sudden leaps.

    2. Harlow Robinson on the second movement

      Harlow Robinson heard in this short movement the sarcastic, ironic Shostakovich already familiar from the First Symphony and the First Piano Concerto.

    3. Trio, solo violin

      In the trio a solo violin plays over harp chords and pizzicato cellos, with glissandos and short, clipped phrases. A solo flute then takes up the tune.

    4. First American performance, 1938

      On 9 April 1938 Artur Rodzinski conducted the NBC Symphony Orchestra in the symphony’s first American performance, which was broadcast on the radio. He returned to the score in London in 1954 and made the performance heard here.

    5. Return of the opening theme, bassoons

      The opening theme returns in the bassoon and contrabassoon, and the strings follow with pizzicato. At the start of the movement the cellos played the theme with the bow.

    6. Recording sessions, Walthamstow, 1954

      Westminster’s sessions with Rodzinski ran from 2 to 14 October 1954 at Walthamstow, in east London. The reissue catalogue names the Royal Philharmonic. The original LP credits the “Philharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London”.

    7. Closing phrases and final chords

      The full orchestra returns for the closing phrases, and the xylophone plays the tune above the strings. Short chords end the movement.

    Joins

    1. Solo violin over harp chords

      The horns finish their phrase and fall silent. A solo violin begins over quiet harp chords, and pizzicato cellos mark out the bass. The three-beat metre continues, and the violin plays the dance with slides and light bow strokes.

    2. Bassoons take over the cello tune

      Bassoon and contrabassoon play the low tune from the opening, between rehearsals 65 and 67. When the strings rejoin, they play pizzicato. The melody keeps its shape, and the bass line passes from bowed strings to double reeds.

    3. Xylophone added to the final phrase

      High woodwinds and xylophone reinforce the last string phrase. Horns and lower brass punctuate it with chords, and the cellos and basses keep the clipped dance steps underneath. The final chords are short and separated by rests.

  3. III. LargoPhilharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London; Artur Rodzinski, conductor · 1954, London; Westminster LP issued 1956
    14:08

    The Largo is the third movement of Shostakovich’s Symphony No. 5 in D minor, Op. 47. The brass are silent throughout and the strings are divided into eight parts; the movement opens quietly as a lament, and later the massed strings play loudly with piano and xylophone.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Strings in eight parts

      Shostakovich divides the violins into three groups and the violas and cellos into two each, with the basses below. At rehearsal 75 the third violin group begins the melody in F♯ minor. The phrases of the eight parts overlap.

    2. Shostakovich on writing the Largo, 1956

      In his introduction to the 1956 score, Shostakovich wrote: “I wrote the third part in three days.” He described a long period of preparation before the music was written down. The movement lasts nearly a quarter of an hour in this performance.

    3. Flute solo with harp

      The strings stop. A solo flute plays above plucked notes in the harp, and a second flute then joins it.

    4. Strings and woodwinds, rehearsal 81

      From rehearsal 81 the string lines grow louder, and the woodwinds join them. The trumpets, horns and trombones stay silent throughout the climax, because the Largo has no brass parts.

    5. Oboe solo over string tremolo

      The oboe plays a melody, broken by rests, above a continuous tremolo in the violins. Clarinet and flute solos follow over the divided strings.

    6. Press reception after the première

      About twenty articles appeared in the press after the première. Most were positive, but Alexander Ostretsov complained that “the pathos of suffering” was in places “taken to a naturalistic cry and wail”.

    7. Piano and xylophone enter

      Piano and xylophone enter over the string lament, doubling the woodwinds and strings with hard attacks. The cellos play their line strongly. The brass rest.

    8. Shostakovich on tragedy in Soviet art, 1956

      In his 1956 introduction to the score, Shostakovich asked whether tragedy belonged in Soviet art and answered that it did. He pointed to Verdi’s and Mozart’s Requiems, whose stern characters fill men’s souls “with courage and the will to struggle”.

    9. Muted strings, rehearsal 92

      After the climax, Shostakovich marks the strings con sordino at rehearsals 92–⁠93. The opening lament returns in the muted strings, marked pianissimo.

    10. Harp and solo second violin

      The harp returns to its spare accompaniment. A solo second violin plays above the muted strings.

    11. Celesta and harp harmonics

      Celesta and harp harmonics share the final melody, with high string harmonics held above them. The strings close the movement with a chord marked morendo (dying away).

    Joins

    1. Strings stop; harp accompanies flute solo

      The strings hold their last chord and stop. The harp continues alone with plucked notes beneath a flute solo, and a second flute joins the first at rehearsal 80.

    2. The divided strings return

      The flute duet slows and fades out. At A tempo the divided strings take up their long phrases again. They begin at a low dynamic and grow louder as the woodwinds join them. The lower strings hold the bass beneath several violin lines, each moving independently.

    3. Oboe solo over violin tremolo

      The phrases in the lower strings die away under morendo markings. The violins begin a soft tremolo, and an oboe plays a solo line above it. The bass stops, and the oboe is left high above the tremolo.

    4. Piano and xylophone join the climax

      The strings and woodwinds build a crescendo. At the climax that follows, piano tremolos and xylophone notes reinforce their attack, and the cellos play the melody below. The brass do not play in this passage.

    5. From the climax to muted strings

      The winds stop after their last loud chords. Cellos and basses descend and fade out, and the upper strings put on mutes. Their next phrase is marked pianissimo, and the bass holds a soft note beneath it.

    6. Harp returns before the close

      The muted strings diminish, and the harp returns with the plucked accompaniment heard earlier under the flutes. A second-violin solo follows. In the closing bars, celesta and harp harmonics replace the bowed melody while the strings hold high harmonics.

  4. IV. Allegro non troppoPhilharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London; Artur Rodzinski, conductor · 1954, London; Westminster LP issued 1956
    8:10

    The fourth movement of the Symphony No. 5 in D minor, Op. 47, marked Allegro non troppo, ends with the brass in D major over a repeated high A. Rodzinski conducts a shortened version that cuts 37 bars from the finale.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Brass march over timpani

      After a full attack from woodwind and brass, trumpets, trombones and tuba play the march in D minor over the timpani. The strings rest.

    2. Rodzinski’s 37-bar cut

      Rodzinski cut 37 bars from this finale. He made the same cut in his 1942 Cleveland recording and kept it in the Westminster version twelve years later.

    3. Violins after the march

      The brass grow softer and stop. The violins continue in a much quieter passage, playing small repeated figures above long notes in the lower parts.

    4. Introduction to the score, 1956

      In the introduction to the 1956 Muzgiz score, published under his name, Shostakovich called the finale “an optimistic, invigorating outlet for all the tragic tensions of the earlier parts”.

    5. Première, Leningrad, 21 November 1937

      At the première in Leningrad on 21 November 1937, the artist Lyubov Yakovleva watched Shostakovich respond to the applause. In her diary she described him as pale and biting his lips: “I think he might have burst into tears.”

    6. Brass over repeated notes

      The march returns over repeated notes in the strings and woodwinds. Above this accompaniment the brass play longer phrases, and their note values lengthen towards the end.

    7. Repeated A over D major

      High strings and woodwinds repeat A above long D major chords in the brass. The harmony underneath scarcely moves.

    8. Closing chords in D major

      The repeated notes stop. Brass and strings hold the closing D major chords, and the bass drum and timpani play repeated strokes beneath them.

    Joins

    1. Violins continue after the brass chords

      The brass sustain their chords and diminish. At rehearsal 112 much of the orchestra stops, and the violins continue with repeated notes over a held bass. The tempo slows to crotchet = 80.

    2. Repeated string figures and returning brass

      The passage for low wind and drums leads into repeated figures for the strings and upper woodwinds. Trumpets and trombones re-enter above them with longer phrases, and the bass supports the climb towards the closing D major.

    3. Repeated A over D major

      The repeated upper notes stay on A while the brass broaden into long chords. Underneath, the harmony is D major, with D in the bass. The strings continue their repeated notes through the sustained brass phrases up to the final orchestral chords.
