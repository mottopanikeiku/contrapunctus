Dmitri Shostakovich / VI. Modernism

Symphony No. 5, Op. 47

1937 · Op. 47 · Orchestra

The divided strings of the Largo make a spacious lament without help from the brass. That sound is still in the ear when the finale reaches its brilliant, insistently repeated D major conclusion.

4 recordings · 41 min

In the Hall

  1. I. ModeratoPhilharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London; Artur Rodzinski, conductor · 1954, London; Westminster LP issued 1956
    14:35

    Low strings fling a phrase at the violins, and the Fifth Symphony begins. Rodzinski conducts a movement that grows from that bare exchange to a brass-and-drum march, then ends with the small, cold gleam of a celesta.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. D minor in reply

      Cellos and basses leap from D to B♭; the violins answer. The phrases pull against one another across the orchestra, with rests leaving each reply exposed. Winds and brass wait.

    2. Pravda attacks the opera

      On 28 January 1936, Pravda prints an unsigned attack on Shostakovich’s Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District, headed “Muddle Instead of Music”. The opera is driven from Soviet theatres. Shostakovich begins the Fifth the following spring.

    3. A long violin line

      The violins stretch out a quiet melody over repeated notes in the lower strings. Harp chords mark the harmony beneath them. The tune has room to breathe, though the accompaniment keeps counting.

    4. A first night in Leningrad

      On 21 November 1937, Yevgeny Mravinsky conducts the première in the Great Hall of the Leningrad Philharmonic. Shostakovich’s old teacher Maximilian Steinberg writes in his diary: “The ovation was stupendous.”

    5. The piano starts digging

      The piano enters low down, with short, hard-edged figures beside the cellos and basses. Horns stretch longer notes above it. The accompaniment has become the thing driving the music.

    6. Brass over the marching strings

      Trumpets and trombones bear down on the strings. Repeated notes and heavy brass chords turn the movement into a march, with percussion adding its hard edge. The opening’s small exchanges have become orchestral proclamations.

    7. Flute beside a horn

      The lyrical tune returns in the flute and horn, over harp and gently repeated strings. A phrase that once belonged to the violin section is now passed between two solitary players.

    8. A celesta at the edge

      The coda pares the orchestra down to solo violin, harp and muted strings. On the last page, the celesta climbs in small, bright scales while the bass and inner strings die away.

    How it is joined

    1. A long tune above repeated strings

      The winds withdraw and the strings become very quiet. Harp chords and repeated notes in the lower strings establish a steady accompaniment; above them the first violins begin their long melody, marked pp espressivo.

    2. The piano takes over the bass

      The harp’s sustained chords finish, the violin line sinks away, and the low piano begins its clipped figures. Cellos and double basses join it; the horns then lay a slower phrase over the moving bass. The piano is marked una corda, using the soft pedal.

    3. The flute resumes the long melody

      A slowing passage ends with the brass dying away. At Più mosso, harp chords and repeated strings return very softly. Flute and horn share the lyrical melody above them; the heavy orchestral bass has fallen silent.

    4. Muted strings surround the final solos

      The score returns to Moderato, now at crotchet = 42. Muted violas, cellos and basses carry the opening rhythm beneath a flute solo. Harp and solo violin follow; the celesta’s ascending scales belong to the last page.

  2. II. AllegrettoPhilharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London; Artur Rodzinski, conductor · 1954, London; Westminster LP issued 1956
    4:28

    Cellos and basses start a heavy-footed dance; a solo violin answers with something much more delicate. Shostakovich’s scherzo can turn on a change of bow stroke.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The dance starts downstairs

      Cellos and double basses begin alone, in A minor. The tune stamps through three beats to a bar, with short notes and sudden leaps. This waltz wears heavy boots.

    2. One violin takes the floor

      A solo violin dances over harp chords and plucked cellos. Its little slides and clipped phrases bring a sly smile into the trio. A flute takes up the tune in turn.

    3. Rodzinski takes it to America

      On 9 April 1938, Artur Rodzinski conducts the NBC Symphony Orchestra in the Fifth’s first American performance, broadcast on the radio. He is back at the score in London in 1954, making the performance heard here.

    4. Bassoons in the heavy boots

      The opening tune comes back in the bassoon and contrabassoon. The strings answer with plucked notes. The same dance now has a dry, wooden chuckle where the cellos first dug in with their bows.

    5. A final orchestral stamp

      The closing phrases bring the larger orchestra back, with the xylophone sharpening the tune above the strings. Short chords cut the dance off.

    How it is joined

    1. The harp makes room for a violin

      The horns finish their phrase and fall silent. A solo violin begins over quiet harp chords, with pizzicato cellos marking out the bass. The three-beat metre continues; the violin’s slides and light bow strokes change the dance.

    2. Bassoons inherit the cello tune

      Bassoon and contrabassoon recover the opening’s low tune, quietly. When the strings rejoin, they pluck their notes. The melody keeps its shape while the bass changes from bowed strings to double reeds.

    3. Xylophone sharpens the final phrase

      High woodwinds and xylophone reinforce the last string phrase. Horns and lower brass punctuate it with chords, while the cellos and basses keep the clipped dance steps underneath. The final chords are short and separated by rests.

  3. III. LargoPhilharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London; Artur Rodzinski, conductor · 1954, London; Westminster LP issued 1956
    14:08

    Shostakovich leaves the brass silent and divides the strings into eight parts. The Largo begins as a quiet lament, then finds a terrible force in massed strings, piano and xylophone.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Eight string parts

      A third group of violins begins the lament in F♯ minor. Shostakovich divides the violins into three groups, the violas and cellos into two each, with the basses below. Their phrases overlap like voices in a choir.

    2. Three days at the desk

      In his introduction to the 1956 score, Shostakovich writes: “I wrote the third part in three days.” He describes a long period of preparation before the music reaches the page. The movement lasts nearly a quarter of an hour here.

    3. Flutes above a harp

      The strings fall silent. A flute sings above the harp’s plucked notes, and a second flute joins it. After so many bowed voices, the harp leaves little spaces around each note.

    4. The strings gather the winds

      The string lines swell, joined by woodwinds. Shostakovich makes the orchestra cry out while every trumpet, horn and trombone remains silent. The violins have to carry the strain themselves.

    5. Oboe over trembling strings

      An oboe sings above the violins’ quiet tremolo. The bowing trembles continuously beneath a melody that has to pause for breath. Later, clarinet and flute take their own turns in this exposed space.

    6. Piano and xylophone bite

      Piano and xylophone strike into the string lament. Their hard attacks cut across the bowed sound; the cellos drive their line out in force. The brass are still silent.

    7. The strings put on mutes

      After the long outcry, Shostakovich asks the strings to put on mutes. The bow strokes soften, and the opening lament returns in a veiled sound. The score marks it pianissimo.

    8. Harp beside one violin

      The harp returns to its spare accompaniment. A solo from the second violins answers above the muted strings. What began with a whole group singing now rests with one player.

    9. Celesta and harp harmonics

      Celesta and harp harmonics share the final melody. High string harmonics hang above them, thin and pale. Then the strings close the movement with a chord marked morendo — dying away.

    How it is joined

    1. The strings release the melody

      The strings hold their last chord and stop. The harp continues alone beneath a flute solo; a second flute joins the first at rehearsal 80. Sustained bowing has been replaced by separate plucked notes under the wind melody.

    2. The strings resume their lament

      The flute duet slows and dies away. At A tempo, the divided strings resume their long phrases, beginning quietly and swelling as woodwinds join them. The lower strings carry the bass beneath several independently moving violin lines.

    3. An oboe above the tremolo

      The lower strings’ phrases die away under morendo markings. The violins begin a soft tremolo, and an oboe sings above it. The bass falls silent, leaving the solo high over the trembling bows.

    4. Piano and xylophone harden the lament

      The strings and woodwinds gather into a crescendo. At the ensuing climax, piano tremolos and xylophone notes reinforce their attack, with the cellos driving the melody below. Shostakovich builds the outcry from strings, reeds and struck notes; the brass have no part.

    5. The outcry ends in muted strings

      The winds stop after their last emphatic chords. Cellos and basses descend and die away, and the upper strings put on mutes. Their next phrase is marked pianissimo, with the bass held softly beneath it.

    6. The harp picks up the thread

      The muted strings diminish, and the harp returns with the plucked accompaniment heard earlier under the flutes. A second-violin solo follows. In the closing bars, celesta and harp harmonics replace the bowed melody while the strings hold high harmonics.

  4. IV. Allegro non troppoPhilharmonic Symphony Orchestra of London; Artur Rodzinski, conductor · 1954, London; Westminster LP issued 1956
    8:10

    The Fifth ends with brass in D major and a high A repeated until it seems impossible to stop. Rodzinski conducts a shortened version, cutting 37 bars from the finale.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Brass over the timpani

      After a full wind-and-brass attack, trumpets, trombones and tuba set out the march in D minor. Timpani beat beneath them. The strings wait through this opening proclamation, their silence making the brass sound all the more bare.

    2. Thirty-seven bars cut

      Rodzinski removes 37 bars from this finale. He makes the same cut in his 1942 Cleveland recording and carries it into the Westminster version twelve years later.

    3. Violins after the march

      The brass soften and fall away. The violins continue in a much quieter passage, their small repeated figures suspended above long lower notes. The march’s clenched rhythm has loosened.

    4. Optimism in the printed score

      In his introduction to the 1956 score, Shostakovich calls the finale “an optimistic, invigorating outlet for all the tragic tensions of the earlier parts”. Those are the words he puts into print beside this music.

    5. The composer on the stage

      At the première in Leningrad on 21 November 1937, artist Lyubov Yakovleva watches Shostakovich answer the applause. In her diary she describes him as pale and biting his lips: “I think he might have burst into tears.”

    6. Brass above the repeated notes

      The march rebuilds around repeated notes in the strings and woodwinds. The brass stretch their phrases across that busy accompaniment. Their notes grow longer as the music bears down on the ending.

    7. A, again and again

      High strings and woodwinds repeat A above the brass’s long D major chords. The upper notes keep beating while the harmony underneath scarcely moves. It is an ending with its teeth clenched.

    8. The bass drum answers

      The repeated notes finally stop. Brass and strings hold the closing D major chords while the bass drum and timpani hammer beneath them.

    How it is joined

    1. The violins continue after the brass

      The brass sustain and diminish their chords. At rehearsal 112, the violins continue with quiet repeated notes while much of the orchestra stops; the bass holds beneath the change. The score eases to crotchet = 80.

    2. Repeated strings carry the brass back

      The low-wind and drum passage grows into repeated figures for the strings and upper woodwinds. Trumpets and trombones rejoin with longer phrases above them; the bass supports the climb towards the closing D major.

    3. Repeated A over D major

      The repeated upper notes settle on A while the brass broaden into long chords. D major takes hold underneath, with D in the bass. The strings keep striking their repeated notes through the sustained brass phrases, right up to the final orchestral chords.
