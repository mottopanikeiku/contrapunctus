Johannes Brahms / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 1, Op. 68

1876 · Op. 68 · Orchestra

The finale’s horn call first appeared in a birthday greeting Brahms sent Clara Schumann from the Alps in 1868. It precedes a brass chorale and the broad C major Allegro.

4 recordings · 46 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Un poco sostenuto — Allegro — Meno allegroCzech National Symphony Orchestra
    15:09

    Brahms’s First Symphony begins with timpani hammering C while strings climb and winds pull downwards. The Czech National Symphony Orchestra plays a movement whose stern opening finally comes to rest in a quiet C major.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Timpani hold their ground

      Timpani hammer C. Violins and cellos climb by semitones while winds and violas pull downwards. The double basses and contrabassoon reinforce the drumbeats: the floor stays put while everything above it strains in opposite directions.

    2. Karlsruhe, November 1876

      On 4 November 1876, Otto Dessoff conducts the première in Karlsruhe. Brahms is forty-three. Two serenades, a piano concerto, the German Requiem and the Haydn Variations are already behind him; his First Symphony has taken much longer to reach the platform.

    3. The Allegro takes flight

      The Allegro begins with a low C and the introduction’s rising semitones, now quickened. First violins climb through an arpeggio above throbbing inner strings. Cellos and bassoons keep the three-note ascent moving underneath.

    4. An oboe over murmuring strings

      The gentler second theme belongs to the oboe, then the clarinets, in E♭ major. Violas repeat notes beneath it. The melody’s sighs and small chromatic steps recall the introduction, though the oboe now lets them sing.

    5. The arpeggio lands in B

      The violin arpeggio reappears in B major, a semitone below the home keynote. Soon the strings subside into tremolo and a bassoon takes up the phrase. B major darkens into B minor.

    6. Simrock grows impatient

      On 22 February 1873, the publisher Fritz Simrock writes to Brahms: “Aren’t you doing anything anymore? Am I not to have a symphony from you in ’73 either?” Three more years pass before the First is finished.

    7. The bass begins to climb

      After a long wait on G, the bass begins climbing by semitones. Horns repeat the short-note rhythm; oboes and clarinets answer with descending phrases. Each repetition pushes the bass a little higher, until trumpets and timpani join the insistence.

    8. C minor without a pause

      The rising semitones carry straight into the return. The first violins recover their arpeggio in C minor while the orchestra is still in full motion. Brahms cuts away part of the opening group and moves on sooner than before.

    9. Plucked strings, softer semitones

      Hammered thirds become quiet plucked notes beneath a long violin phrase. Later, Meno allegro slows the music, and winds and strings pass the rising semitones between them. The arpeggio finally opens into C major; the last chords are soft.

    How it is joined

    1. A plucked G releases the Allegro

      The cellos finish the introduction with a quiet plucked G, the dominant of C. The Allegro answers with a low C and quickened rising semitones in the winds. First violins then launch their arpeggio above the same semitones in cellos and bassoons.

    2. A whole tone leads to B

      The closing descent takes a whole step down to B. Strings begin the first theme’s arpeggio in B major, supported by wind chords. Eight bars later the violins turn to tremolo; a solo bassoon and low strings continue the theme as the harmony moves into B minor.

    3. The G pedal starts to move

      The repeated G in the bass has held the dominant in place. Now low strings begin a rising sequence, moving by semitones beneath horn rhythms and answering oboe and clarinet phrases. The strings climb higher and the crescendo continues until the bass settles back onto the dominant.

    4. The semitones carry straight through

      The wind semitones and rushing strings continue through the join. Brahms omits the low C that announced the exposition; the rising motto enters within the ongoing sequence. The violin arpeggios follow in C minor, and the returning first group is compressed.

    5. Hammered thirds become plucked strings

      The repeated thirds soften into pizzicato accompaniment. Violins stretch the rising semitone figure into a longer phrase over plucked cellos. The coda first cadences in C minor; at Meno allegro, winds and strings exchange the figure above a held C before the final turn to major.

  2. II. Andante sostenutoCzech National Symphony Orchestra
    8:37

    The First Symphony’s Andante opens in E major with strings and bassoon, then makes room for an oboe’s long song. Near the end, horn and solo violin join that song; the violin holds its last note across the orchestra’s closing chords.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Bassoon beside the violins

      Strings begin a gentle rise and fall in E major, with a bassoon doubling the violins. Three low horn notes bridge into a darker answer. The phrase is already bending away from its sunny opening.

    2. The oboe’s long phrase

      The strings mostly withdraw, leaving the oboe to sing over wind harmony and light viola accompaniment. Its melody climbs to a high point; underneath, the strings quietly bring back their opening rise and fall.

    3. A dance loses its footing

      Violins carry a lilting dotted rhythm out of the E-major cadence. Then their accents slip away from the downbeat, and rising semitones pull the music towards C♯ minor. The lower strings take up the rhythm with the tread of a funeral march.

    4. A publisher waits

      On 22 February 1873 Brahms’s publisher Fritz Simrock wrote to him: “Aren’t you doing anything anymore? Am I not to have a symphony from you in ’73 either?” The première came in November 1876. Hanslick wrote that the musical world had awaited it “with such tense anticipation”.

    5. The opening changes hands

      Oboes and clarinets carry the opening tune back into E major. Flutes sing above them, strings sweep around them, and cellos pluck triplets below. The melody is familiar, but nearly everyone has a different part.

    6. Friends disagree about the finale

      Brahms’s friends hear very different symphonies. Clara Schumann finds the finale’s brilliant ending unconvincing; Theodor Billroth admires it but objects to the first movement’s harshness. Hermann Levi thinks these gentler middle movements belong in another work.

    7. Three voices share the song

      The oboe melody returns with horn and solo violin beside it. The violin steps out of its section and remains a solo voice for the rest of the movement. Plucked cello triplets keep the tune gently afloat.

    8. The violin decorates the horn

      The horn begins the oboe tune once more. Above it, the solo violin adds curling decoration, quickening into groups of six notes. Low strings hold E while the other parts move around that steady foundation.

    9. One violin holds on

      Plucked arpeggios climb through the strings and reach the solo violin, still playing with the bow. Its last note holds over the orchestra’s soft repeated chords. Below it, the other strings return to their bows for the final answer.

    How it is joined

    1. The cadence starts a dotted dance

      The E-major cadence overlaps with a new dotted violin phrase. Second violins and violas echo its lilt while first violins rise by semitones, shifting their accents off the beat. Bassoons and horns join the growing phrase as it turns towards C♯ minor.

    2. A timpani roll carries the return

      Strings and winds overlap in four-note groups across the 3/4 bars. A soft timpani roll remains as the other parts fall away. Oboes and clarinets bring back the opening theme in E major, with flute harmony above and sweeping strings and plucked cello triplets below.

    3. A solo violin crosses the cadence

      The strings reach a B-major cadence. Across its arrival, a solo violin begins the upbeat of the oboe tune, now shared with oboe and horn as the harmony turns towards E. Plucked cello triplets and rising answers from the other strings keep moving beneath the three soloists.

    4. The bass arrives before the melody

      The bass reaches E while the upper parts avoid completing the cadence. Horn takes the opening of the oboe melody; the solo violin decorates it above. Low strings and second horn hold the tonic, and strings, flutes and timpani continue their off-beat triplets.

  3. III. Un poco allegretto e graziosoCzech National Symphony Orchestra
    4:55

    A clarinet and plucked cellos give the First Symphony a few minutes of ease. Even when the clarinet’s tune comes home, the middle section’s repeated notes keep dancing above it.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Clarinet and plucked cellos

      A clarinet wanders through a five-bar phrase above steadily plucked cellos. Its answer turns the same melody upside down: each rise becomes a fall. Bassoon and horn fill in the harmony, and the upper strings join near the end of the phrase.

    2. Karlsruhe, 4 November 1876

      Otto Dessoff conducted the première in Karlsruhe on 4 November 1876. Brahms was forty-three. He had already called the symphony “long and not exactly amiable”, and audiences and critics split over it.

    3. F minor stirs the strings

      In F minor, the strings split a quick four-note pulse between high and low instruments. Clarinet begins an agitated melody; flute and oboe take it on. The easy walking pace has acquired a nervous accompaniment.

    4. B major, three against two

      The middle section steps into B major and 6/8. Winds repeat three notes; strings answer with falling broken chords. The beat now divides into threes, giving the exchange a buoyancy quite different from the clarinet’s opening stroll.

    5. Levi objects to the interlude

      Hermann Levi admires the finale, yet finds the two middle movements out of place in this great sweeping symphony. Here Brahms keeps the orchestra occupied with a clarinet tune, plucked strings and a brief dance in B major.

    6. The clarinet has company

      The clarinet’s opening melody returns in A♭ major, with its plucked cello accompaniment. Above it, flutes and oboes keep playing the trio’s repeated notes as triplets. For a few bars, both sections sing at once.

    7. A symphony for four hands

      In October 1877 the publisher Simrock issued the full score and, beside it, Brahms’s arrangement of the whole symphony for piano four hands. Two pianists could play all four movements, this clarinet tune included, at one keyboard.

    8. The trio says goodnight

      Poco a poco più tranquillo: gradually more tranquil. The trio’s repeated notes return softly in the violins, answered by falling broken chords in the lower strings. A brief turn through D♭ leads back to A♭; the final cadence waits for the winds and strings to finish their exchange.

    How it is joined

    1. E♭ becomes D♯

      Clarinet, horn and violin descend through an E♭ chord while the cellos pluck upwards. E♭ becomes D♯, the third of B major. The metre changes from 2/4 to 6/8, and three repeated wind notes alternate with descending string arpeggios.

    2. Triplets cross into the old metre

      D♯ turns back into E♭ as the strings hint at the opening tune in A♭ minor. The score returns to 2/4, but winds retain the trio’s repeated notes as triplets. A quiet plucked string descent prepares the clarinet’s return in A♭ major.

    3. The clarinet returns beneath the trio

      Plucked cellos recover their opening pulse in A♭ major. Clarinet and horn bring back the first phrase, while flutes and oboes continue the trio’s repeated-note triplets above it. The reprise is shorter than the opening section: its F-minor episode has gone.

    4. A cadence turns back to the trio

      The approaching cadence opens into the trio’s repeated notes, now in violins, with descending arpeggios below. Brahms marks poco a poco più tranquillo. The strings turn briefly towards D♭; flutes, clarinets and bassoons answer gently and lead back to A♭.

  4. IV. Adagio — Più andante — Allegro non troppo, ma con brio — Più allegroCzech National Symphony Orchestra
    16:57

    A horn call, a quiet trombone chorale and a great C-major string tune transform the First Symphony’s finale. Brahms saves the chorale for the final pages, where brass and strings proclaim it together.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Four notes falling

      Violas, cellos and double basses begin a four-note descent. Above them, the violins unfold a slow, troubled version of the melody that will later become the great C-major tune. For now it lives in C minor, interrupted by accelerating plucked strings.

    2. Everything plucked

      In the introduction every string player plays pizzicato. Two-note figures pass between the groups, starting very quietly and gathering speed and volume through G minor, E♭ minor and F minor, until a chord is suddenly cut off. A second plucked passage, shorter and faster, comes later.

    3. A horn above the tremolo

      C major opens under a horn call. Muted violins and violas tremble beneath the long notes, and the timpani roll softly. The tune passes between horns so it can keep singing without a break for breath; flutes take it up next.

    4. A birthday tune for Clara

      In 1868 Brahms sends Clara Schumann a birthday greeting containing the melody that becomes this horn call. Eight years later, the private tune stands at the turning point of his First Symphony.

    5. The quiet trombone chorale

      Trombones and bassoons sing a short chorale. The trombones have waited until this movement to play at all. Their little hymn will stay out of sight through the Allegro, returning with the full orchestra near the end.

    6. The violins’ great tune

      Low in their range, the violins begin the broad C-major tune over plucked lower strings. Horns fill out the harmony. The phrase takes seventeen bars: its second half delays the cadence by one bar, giving the melody another breath.

    7. “Every jackass hears it”

      The big C-major tune recalls the “Ode to Joy” in Beethoven’s Ninth. Asked about the likeness, Brahms is reported to have retorted: “Yes indeed, and what’s more remarkable is that every jackass immediately hears it.”

    8. The tune is cut into pieces

      A third statement of the big tune seems to begin, for the full orchestra, but it breaks off at once and only its opening three-note turn is kept. Running scales in strings and bassoons carry the music towards G major, with loud punctuation from winds, brass and low strings.

    9. The call returns, then the descent

      Flute and horn bring back the alphorn call from the introduction, the horn echoing the flute. Then the low strings settle on the four-note descent that opened the movement, and the violins sing a second tune above it, winding down, leaping back up and flowing on.

    10. The exposition closes in E minor

      Violas begin a tremolo that builds. The strings join in a powerful downward arch that leads to a sighing dotted rhythm, with loud cadences from winds and brass. Near the end the full orchestra, trombones included, hammers out the E-minor cadence.

    11. The tune ventures out again

      The broad tune returns in C major with a fuller accompaniment. Its continuation soon turns towards E♭ and then F♯. The violins have brought back a familiar melody and immediately found it new places to go.

    12. The plucked strings come back

      In the development, plucked figures pass from violins to low strings, as in the introduction’s two odd pizzicato passages. Horns and bassoons hold chords over them, and trumpets join as the passage builds. The key slides up to F♯ major, and the plucking starts again.

    13. The development proper begins

      Where the transition once began its running scales, two bars of heavy accents in the strings interrupt it. The scales then settle on D minor, richly harmonised, and taper off. Above them oboe and flute pass fragments of the big tune’s opening turn.

    14. The orchestra blasts the call

      Winds hammer out the big tune’s turn figures over brass chords and timpani rolls. At the climax the full orchestra shouts descending figures from the horn call, off the beat, in C minor, and the trombones enter for the first time since the exposition.

    15. The horn call, changed

      The horn call returns, now with smoothly moving violas and cellos beneath it. The introduction’s trembling strings have gone. Violins extend the tune, then descend as Brahms marks calando — dying away.

    16. Four descending notes come home

      The second theme returns in C major. Its four-note descending bass passes between low strings and flute, clarinet and bassoon. The little descent from the finale’s first bars now has a whole family of voices.

    17. Closing music keeps to C minor

      In the exposition this closing material was in E minor. Transposed strictly it would now be in A minor, but the symphony must end in C, so Brahms holds it in C minor. Violas tremble, strings plunge in unison, and loud cadences lean towards B♭.

    18. The symphony crosses the Atlantic

      Leopold Damrosch conducts the first American performance at New York’s Steinway Hall on 15 December 1877. Boston hears it on 3 January 1878 under Carl Zerrahn. The Boston Symphony Orchestra plays it in its first season, on 10 December 1881, under Georg Henschel.

    19. Low brass recall the opening

      Trombone, contrabassoon and low strings recall the main tune in its darker form. Violin arpeggios climb above a timpani roll. The phrases shorten and accelerate until seven hammered chords launch Più allegro.

    20. Più allegro: churning strings

      Più allegro, in cut time. The strings churn on three notes taken from the big tune’s turn, with timpani beneath. Winds and brass strike two jarring diminished-seventh chords against them, each resolving to a warmer harmony.

    21. The chorale fills the orchestra

      The quiet chorale returns in brass and strings together. It has waited since the introduction for this moment. Around it, the strings’ turning figures keep the faster coda moving towards the final C-major chords.

    22. Three chords and a long one

      The churning returns with loud brass and wind chords, working upward. A timpani roll sounds under a powerful rising arpeggio in the basses. Then three short C-major chords from the whole orchestra, and one long final chord over the roll.

    How it is joined

    1. C major opens under the horn

      The surging C-minor introduction breaks off. Low strings descend and diminish into Più andante; the horn call opens in C major over a soft timpani roll and the trombones’ first entry. Muted violins and violas begin their tremolo beneath the tune.

    2. The flute leaves the trombones speaking

      The flutes finish the C-major horn melody. Trombones and bassoons answer with a compact chorale in measured chords; a timpani roll marks its final downward leap. Horns then resume the call above string tremolo, building towards the dominant chord that prepares the Allegro.

    3. Held brass release the violin tune

      Horn and trombone hold the dominant chord on G as the other instruments fall silent. Violins emerge low in their range with the broad C-major melody, supported by horns and plucked cellos and basses. The marking is Allegro non troppo, ma con brio.

    4. Quiet winds lead the tune home

      After the closing E-minor cadence, quiet rising wind figures lead back to the broad violin tune in C major. Its accompaniment is fuller now. The continuation turns towards E♭ and F♯, taking the returned melody into another long development.

    5. The horn call yields to descending basses

      The returning horn call broadens over moving strings. Violins extend it, then descend under pulsating timpani, marked calando. All strings introduce the second group’s four-note descent in C major; subsequent statements alternate between low strings and flute, clarinet and bassoon.

    6. Quiet fragments gather over tremolo

      The C-minor closing cadence subsides into quiet rising figures from horns, violins and clarinets over viola tremolo. Trombone, contrabassoon and low strings recall the main tune above a long timpani roll. Violin arpeggios shorten and climb; stringendo drives the passage towards the faster C-major coda.
