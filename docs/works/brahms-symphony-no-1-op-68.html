Johannes Brahms / III. Romanticism

Symphony No. 1, Op. 68

1876 · Op. 68 · Orchestra

The finale’s horn call first appeared in a birthday greeting Brahms sent Clara Schumann from the Alps in 1868. It comes before a brass chorale and the broad C major Allegro.

4 recordings · 46 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Un poco sostenuto — Allegro — Meno allegroCzech National Symphony Orchestra
    15:09

    The Czech National Symphony Orchestra plays the first movement of Brahms’s Symphony No. 1 in C minor, Op. 68. It opens with the timpani repeating C while the strings rise and the winds descend, and it ends in C major.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening bars, timpani pedal on C

      The timpani repeat C. Violins and cellos climb by semitones while winds and violas move downwards. The double basses and contrabassoon double the drumbeats.

    2. First performance, Karlsruhe, 1876

      Otto Dessoff conducted the première in Karlsruhe on 4 November 1876. Brahms was forty-three. By then he had already written two serenades, a piano concerto, the German Requiem and the Haydn Variations, and the First Symphony had taken much longer to reach performance.

    3. Opening of the Allegro

      The Allegro begins with a low C and the rising semitones of the introduction, now faster. First violins climb through an arpeggio above repeated notes in the inner strings. Cellos and bassoons continue the three-note ascent underneath.

    4. Second theme, oboe and clarinets

      The second theme is in E♭ major. The oboe plays it first, then the clarinets, while the violas repeat notes beneath. Its falling figures and small chromatic steps come from the introduction.

    5. Violin arpeggio in B major

      The violin arpeggio returns in B major, a semitone below the home keynote. The strings then drop to tremolo and a bassoon takes over the phrase. B major turns to B minor.

    6. Letter from Simrock, 1873

      On 22 February 1873 the publisher Fritz Simrock wrote to Brahms: “Aren’t you doing anything anymore? Am I not to have a symphony from you in ’73 either?” Brahms finished the First three years later.

    7. Retransition, chromatic bass ascent

      After a long pedal on G, the bass rises by semitones. Horns repeat the short-note rhythm, and oboes and clarinets reply with descending phrases. With each repetition the bass moves higher, until trumpets and timpani enter.

    8. Recapitulation in C minor, bar 340

      The rising semitones lead directly into the recapitulation. The first violins play their arpeggio in C minor again while the full orchestra is still moving. Brahms shortens the first group and moves on sooner than in the exposition.

    9. Coda, bars 475–⁠511

      The hammered thirds return as soft pizzicato notes under a long violin phrase. At bar 495, marked Meno allegro, the tempo slows and winds and strings pass the rising semitones between them. The arpeggio moves into C major, and the last chords are soft.

    Joins

    1. From the plucked G to the Allegro

      The cellos end the introduction with a quiet plucked G, the dominant of C. The Allegro opens on a low C, and the winds play rising semitones at a faster tempo. The cellos and bassoons then take over the same semitones, and the first violins enter above them with their arpeggio.

    2. Down a whole tone to B

      The closing descent moves down a whole step to B. The strings begin the arpeggio of the first theme in B major, with chords in the wind. Eight bars later the violins change to tremolo. A solo bassoon and the low strings continue the theme, and the harmony moves into B minor.

    3. The G pedal begins to move

      A repeated G in the bass holds the dominant. The low strings then begin a rising sequence that moves by semitones. Above it the horns play rhythmic figures, and oboe and clarinet phrases alternate. The strings climb higher and the crescendo continues until the bass returns to the dominant (bars 273–⁠320).

    4. Into the recapitulation without the low C

      The wind semitones and rushing strings continue through the join. Brahms omits the low C that announced the exposition, and the rising motto enters within the ongoing sequence. The violin arpeggios follow in C minor. The returning first group is compressed.

    5. Repeated thirds pass to pizzicato strings

      The repeated thirds become pizzicato accompaniment. Over plucked cellos, the violins extend the rising semitone figure into a longer phrase. The coda first cadences in C minor. At Meno allegro, winds and strings exchange the figure above a held C, and the music then turns to the major.

  2. II. Andante sostenutoCzech National Symphony Orchestra
    8:37

    The Andante sostenuto of the Symphony No. 1 in C minor, Op. 68, opens in E major with strings and bassoon, after which the oboe plays a long melody. Near the end, horn and solo violin join the oboe’s melody, and the violin holds its last note across the orchestra’s closing chords.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening phrase, strings and bassoon

      The strings open with a rising and falling line in E major, and a bassoon doubles the violins. Three low horn notes lead into a phrase in a darker harmony.

    2. Oboe melody, bars 18–⁠23

      Most of the strings drop out, and the oboe plays over wind harmony and a light viola accompaniment. Its melody climbs to a high point. Underneath, the strings bring back their opening rise and fall.

    3. Dotted rhythm and turn to C♯ minor

      After the cadence in E major the violins play a dotted rhythm. Their accents then move off the downbeat into syncopation, and rising semitones lead the harmony towards C♯ minor. The lower strings take over the rhythm at the pace of a funeral march.

    4. Letter from Simrock, 1873

      On 22 February 1873 the publisher Fritz Simrock wrote to the composer, “Aren’t you doing anything anymore? Am I not to have a symphony from you in ’73 either?” The première took place in November 1876. Hanslick wrote that the musical world had awaited it “with such tense anticipation”.

    5. Return of the opening theme, winds

      At bar 67 oboes and clarinets play the opening theme again, now back in E major. Flutes play above them, the upper strings add a new accompaniment and the cellos play pizzicato triplets below.

    6. Reactions of Brahms’s friends

      Brahms’s friends responded to the symphony in different ways. Clara Schumann found the brilliant ending of the finale unconvincing, and Theodor Billroth admired it but objected to the harshness of the first movement. Hermann Levi thought the gentler middle movements belonged in another work.

    7. Return of the oboe melody

      The oboe melody returns with horn and solo violin. The violin leaves its section and plays as a soloist for the rest of the movement. The cellos accompany in pizzicato triplets.

    8. Coda, horn and solo violin

      The horn plays the oboe tune again. Above it the solo violin adds decoration that quickens into sextuplets. The low strings hold E beneath the other parts.

    9. Closing bars, solo violin

      Pizzicato arpeggios rise through the strings to the solo violin, which plays with the bow. Its last note is held over the orchestra’s soft repeated chords. Below it the other strings return to the bow for the final chord.

    Joins

    1. E major cadence overlaps a dotted phrase

      The E major cadence overlaps with a new dotted phrase in the violins. Second violins and violas echo the phrase while first violins rise by semitones, shifting their accents off the beat. Bassoons and horns join as the phrase grows and moves towards C♯ minor.

    2. Timpani roll before the return

      Strings and winds overlap in four-note groups across the 3/4 bars. The other parts drop out, leaving a soft timpani roll. Oboes and clarinets bring back the opening theme in E major. Flutes harmonise above them, and below them the strings play and the cellos pluck triplets.

    3. Solo violin enters across the cadence

      The strings reach a cadence in B major. As the cadence closes, a solo violin begins the upbeat of the oboe tune. Oboe and horn now share the tune with it, and the harmony moves towards E. Beneath the three soloists the cellos play pizzicato triplets and the other strings play rising figures in reply.

    4. Bass reaches E before the melody

      In bars 97–⁠104 the bass reaches E, but the upper parts do not complete the cadence. The horn takes the opening of the oboe melody and the solo violin decorates it above. Low strings and second horn hold the tonic. Strings, flutes and timpani continue their off-beat triplets.

  3. III. Un poco allegretto e graziosoCzech National Symphony Orchestra
    4:55

    The third movement of the Symphony No. 1 in C minor, Op. 68, marked Un poco allegretto e grazioso, opens with a clarinet tune over pizzicato cellos. When the clarinet’s tune returns, the repeated notes of the middle section continue above it.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening clarinet phrase and inversion

      A clarinet plays a five-bar phrase above pizzicato cellos. The answering phrase inverts the melody, so that each rise becomes a fall. Bassoon and horn fill in the harmony, and the upper strings enter near the end of the phrase.

    2. First performance, Karlsruhe, 4 November 1876

      Otto Dessoff conducted the première in Karlsruhe on 4 November 1876. Brahms was forty-three. He had already called the symphony “long and not exactly amiable”, and audiences and critics were divided over it.

    3. F minor section, bars 45–⁠61

      In F minor, the strings divide a quick four-note pulse between high and low instruments. The clarinet begins an agitated melody, which the flute and oboe then take over.

    4. Middle section in B major, 6/8

      The middle section moves to B major and 6/8. The winds repeat three notes, and the strings follow with falling broken chords. The beat now divides into threes, where the clarinet’s opening theme moved in twos.

    5. Levi on the middle movements

      Hermann Levi admired the finale of the First Symphony, but thought the two middle movements out of place in it. Here Brahms gives the orchestra a clarinet tune, plucked strings and a brief dance in B major.

    6. Return of the clarinet theme

      The clarinet’s opening melody returns in A♭ major over its plucked cello accompaniment. Above it, flutes and oboes continue the trio’s repeated notes as triplets, so for a few bars material from both sections sounds together.

    7. Piano four-hand arrangement, 1877

      In October 1877 Simrock published the full score together with Brahms’s arrangement of the whole symphony for piano four hands. In the arrangement two pianists play all four movements, this clarinet tune included, at one keyboard.

    8. Coda, bars 154–⁠164

      The coda is marked Poco a poco più tranquillo (gradually more tranquil). The violins play the trio’s repeated notes softly, and the lower strings follow with falling broken chords. The harmony turns briefly to D♭ and returns to A♭, and the final cadence comes after an exchange between winds and strings.

    Joins

    1. E♭ respelled as D♯

      Clarinet, horn and violin descend through an E♭ chord while the cellos play pizzicato upwards. E♭ is respelled as D♯, the third of B major. The metre changes from 2/4 to 6/8, and three repeated wind notes alternate with descending string arpeggios.

    2. Return to 2/4 with wind triplets

      In bars 109–⁠114, D♯ is respelled as E♭ and the strings play a fragment of the opening tune in A♭ minor. The score returns to 2/4, and the winds continue the trio’s repeated notes as triplets. A quiet pizzicato descent in the strings prepares the clarinet’s return in A♭ major.

    3. The clarinet returns beneath the trio

      The cellos return to their plucked opening pulse in A♭ major. Clarinet and horn bring back the first phrase, and above them flutes and oboes continue the trio’s repeated-note triplets. The reprise is shorter than the opening section and omits its F minor episode.

    4. The cadence opens into trio material

      The approaching cadence opens into the trio’s repeated notes, now in violins, with descending arpeggios below. Brahms marks poco a poco più tranquillo. The strings move briefly towards D♭, and flutes, clarinets and bassoons follow and lead back to A♭.

  4. IV. Adagio — Più andante — Allegro non troppo, ma con brio — Più allegroCzech National Symphony Orchestra
    16:57

    The finale of Brahms’s Symphony No. 1 in C minor, Op. 68 (Adagio — Più andante — Allegro non troppo, ma con brio — Più allegro) introduces a horn call, a quiet trombone chorale and a C major string tune. The chorale returns in the final pages, played by brass and strings together.

    Listening notes · joins
    1. Opening descent, lower strings

      Violas, cellos and double basses begin a four-note descent. Above them the violins play a slow version of the melody that later returns as the main tune in C major. Here it is in C minor, and accelerating pizzicato strings interrupt it.

    2. Pizzicato passages in the introduction

      In the introduction all the strings play pizzicato. Two-note figures pass between the groups, starting very soft and growing faster and louder through G minor, E♭ minor and F minor, until a chord is suddenly cut off. A second pizzicato passage, shorter and faster, follows later.

    3. Horn call, Più andante

      The music moves to C major, and a horn plays the call. Muted violins and violas play a tremolo beneath the long notes, and the timpani roll softly. The tune passes between the horns, so the line continues without a break. The flutes take it up next.

    4. Birthday greeting to Clara Schumann, 1868

      In 1868 Brahms sent Clara Schumann a birthday greeting containing the melody that became this horn call. Eight years later he placed it at the turning point of his First Symphony.

    5. Trombone chorale

      Trombones and bassoons play a short chorale. The trombones play for the first time in the symphony in this movement. The chorale is absent through the Allegro and returns with the full orchestra near the end.

    6. Allegro theme, violins

      Low in their range, the violins begin the broad tune in C major over plucked lower strings, and horns fill out the harmony. The phrase runs to seventeen bars. Its second half delays the cadence by one bar.

    7. Resemblance to Beethoven’s Ninth

      The big C major tune recalls the “Ode to Joy” in Beethoven’s Ninth. When Brahms was asked about the likeness, he is reported to have replied: “Yes indeed, and what’s more remarkable is that every jackass immediately hears it.”

    8. Transition to G major

      A third statement of the big tune begins in the full orchestra and breaks off at once; only its opening three-note turn is kept. Running scales in strings and bassoons move towards G major, punctuated loudly by winds, brass and low strings.

    9. Alphorn call and second theme

      Flute and horn bring back the alphorn call from the introduction, the horn echoing the flute. The low strings then repeat the four-note descent that opened the movement, and above it the violins play a second tune that winds down, leaps back up and continues.

    10. Close of the exposition, E minor

      Violas begin a tremolo that grows louder. The strings join in a descending arch that leads to a dotted rhythm, with loud cadences from winds and brass. From bar 176 the full orchestra, trombones included, plays the extended cadence in E minor.

    11. Return of the main theme

      The broad tune returns in C major with a fuller accompaniment. Its continuation soon turns towards E♭ and then F♯.

    12. Pizzicato passages, development

      In the development (bars 204–⁠219), plucked figures pass from the violins to the low strings, as in the two pizzicato passages of the introduction. Horns and bassoons hold chords over them, and trumpets join as the passage builds. The key moves up to F♯ major, and the pizzicato starts again.

    13. Start of the development

      At the point where the transition began its running scales in the exposition, two bars of heavy accents in the strings break in. The scales then move to D minor, fully harmonised, and thin out. Above them, oboe and flute exchange fragments of the opening turn of the big tune.

    14. Climax with the horn call

      Winds play the turn figures of the main theme over brass chords and timpani rolls. At the climax the full orchestra plays descending figures from the horn call off the beat, in C minor, and the trombones enter for the first time since the exposition.

    15. Return of the horn call

      The horn call returns over smoothly moving violas and cellos. The tremolo strings of the introduction are absent. Violins extend the tune, then descend, marked calando — dying away.

    16. Return of the second theme

      The second theme returns in C major. Its four-note descending bass passes between the low strings and flute, clarinet and bassoon. The descent comes from the finale’s first bars.

    17. Closing material in C minor

      In the exposition this closing material was in E minor; strictly transposed it would now be in A minor, but the symphony ends in C, so in bars 316–⁠339 Brahms keeps it in C minor. The violas play tremolo, the strings descend in unison and loud cadences move towards B♭.

    18. American performances, 1877–⁠1881

      Leopold Damrosch conducted the first American performance at New York’s Steinway Hall on 15 December 1877. Boston heard it on 3 January 1878 under Carl Zerrahn. The Boston Symphony Orchestra played it in its first season, on 10 December 1881, under Georg Henschel.

    19. Transition to the coda

      Trombone, contrabassoon and low strings play the main theme in its darker form. Violin arpeggios rise above a timpani roll. The phrases shorten and speed up until seven hammered chords begin the Più allegro.

    20. Più allegro, bars 391–⁠406

      Più allegro, in cut time. The strings repeat three notes taken from the turn in the main theme, over the timpani. Against them the winds and brass play two diminished-seventh chords, and each one resolves.

    21. Chorale reprise in the coda

      The quiet chorale from the introduction returns in brass and strings together. Around it, the strings play turning figures as the faster coda (bars 407–⁠457) moves towards the final chords in C major.

    22. Closing bars and final chords

      The churning figure returns under loud chords in the brass and wind, which work upward. The basses play a loud rising arpeggio over a timpani roll. The full orchestra then plays three short C major chords and a long final chord over the roll.

    Joins

    1. Più andante: horn call in C major

      The C minor introduction breaks off. Low strings descend and diminish into Più andante. The horn call opens in C major over a soft timpani roll, with the trombones’ first entry. Muted violins and violas begin a tremolo beneath the tune.

    2. Horn call, trombone chorale, dominant preparation

      The flutes finish the C major horn melody. Trombones and bassoons follow with a short chorale in measured chords, and a timpani roll marks its final downward leap. The horns then take up the call again above string tremolo and build towards the dominant chord that prepares the Allegro.

    3. Brass hold G before the violin theme

      Horn and trombone hold the dominant chord on G and the other instruments stop. The violins then enter low in their range with the broad C major melody, accompanied by horns and by cellos and basses playing pizzicato. The marking is Allegro non troppo, ma con brio.

    4. Wind figures lead to the theme’s return

      After the cadence in E minor, quiet rising figures in the winds lead back to the broad violin tune in C major, now with a fuller accompaniment. The continuation moves towards E♭ and F♯, and the returned melody goes into another long development.

    5. Horn call to the descending basses

      The horn call returns and broadens over moving strings. The violins extend it, then descend over pulsating timpani, marked calando. All the strings introduce the four-note descent of the second group in C major. Later statements alternate between the low strings and the flute, clarinet and bassoon.

    6. From the C minor cadence to the coda

      The C minor closing cadence dies down, and horns, violins and clarinets play quiet rising figures over viola tremolo. Trombone, contrabassoon and low strings bring back the main tune above a long timpani roll. The violin arpeggios grow shorter and rise in pitch, and a stringendo leads into the faster C major coda.
