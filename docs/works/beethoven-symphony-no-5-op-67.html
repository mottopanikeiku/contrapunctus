Ludwig van Beethoven / II. Classical

Symphony No. 5, Op. 67

1804–08 · Op. 67 · Orchestra, enlarged in the finale

A quaver rest launches the famous four notes. The scherzo leads straight into a C major finale, where piccolo, contrabassoon and trombones enter for the first time.

4 recordings · 36 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Allegro con brioSkidmore College orchestra
    8:20

    Three Gs fall to E♭, and Beethoven stops the clock with a fermata. From that tiny opening he builds a movement of horn calls, whispered exchanges and one unaccompanied oboe, before the orchestra drives home C minor.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Three Gs, then silence

      A quaver rest, three Gs, then E♭ held under a fermata. Three Fs follow, falling to an even longer D. Beethoven gives the conductor two chances to suspend the beat before the movement has properly begun.

    2. The horns open a door

      The horns turn the opening rhythm into a broad call: B♭–B♭–B♭, then E♭, F and a lower B♭. The intervals widen, and E♭ major offers a little room to breathe.

    3. A song above the rhythm

      The first violins begin a sweet E♭-major melody, marked dolce. Under it, the cellos keep returning to three short B♭s and a longer E♭. The opening rhythm has slipped into the accompaniment.

    4. An evening at the theatre

      Vienna, 22 December 1808: the audience at the Theater an der Wien hears this symphony for the first time. The same programme includes the Sixth Symphony, Fourth Piano Concerto and Choral Fantasy. Beethoven gives them an extraordinary amount of new music in one evening.

    5. Back to the first G

      Two silent bars lead back to the opening. The whole exposition repeats: the fierce C-minor music, the horn call, the E♭-major song. Each tune gets a second hearing before Beethoven begins to break it apart.

    6. Fate at the door

      In 1840, thirteen years after Beethoven’s death, his former secretary Anton Schindler reports that Beethoven described the opening bars to him: “Thus Fate knocks at the door!” Schindler also forged entries in Beethoven’s conversation books, and historians have long doubted his word.

    7. The horns change direction

      After the second E♭-major ending, the horns call again, this time falling to G. The strings answer with three D♭s falling to C. The old rhythm now brings a sharp clash: D♭ against the horns’ held G.

    8. One chord answers another

      The quick rhythm has dwindled to single chords. Winds and strings answer across gaps, getting quieter. The violins move through B♭, C♭ and D♭ before dropping to low C♯. There is scarcely a tune left, yet the waiting is intense.

    9. The oboe speaks alone

      The orchestra stops on a G-major chord. One oboe keeps its G, then descends through F, E♭ and D in a little Adagio. A turn curls around the line. For a few seconds, one player has the whole movement to themselves.

    10. The bassoons inherit the call

      The bassoons take the call that belonged to the horns. Their G–G–G, C, D, G leads to the violin song in C major. The melody is a minor third lower than before, and its new companions give it a warmer voice.

    11. Horns that could not

      In the exposition the horns call in E♭ major. In the recapitulation the same call falls in C major, and Beethoven gives it to the bassoons, since the E♭ horns would have had to change to C crooks. The milder bassoons weaken the solution that the major key seemed to promise.

    12. High C, changing ground

      The violins hold and repeat a high C while the bass moves C–A♭–E–F. The same top note belongs to changing chords beneath it. This is a long coda, with more arguments still to come before the final C-minor chords.

    13. The first hold returns

      Near the end the opening’s held E♭ returns under a fermata. Three Fs fall to a long D, as they did in the first five bars. Beethoven stops the pulse once more with the same unequal holds.

    How it is joined

    1. A pause leaves the horns alone

      A diminished-seventh chord over C cuts off, followed by a silent bar. A short B♭-major chord over D points towards E♭ major. The horns fill the cleared space with three B♭s, then E♭, F and B♭. Their held note carries into the quiet violin melody, marked dolce.

    2. Two silent bars reset the opening

      Three short dominant chords resolve to E♭ major: the bass moves B♭–E♭ and the first violins D–E♭. Two silent bars follow. The repeat brings back the opening Gs and held E♭, with the strings and clarinets playing in octaves. The broad closing chord has narrowed to a single line.

    3. D♭ clashes with a held G

      The E♭-major cadence and two silent bars return. This time the horns play B♭–B♭–B♭ down to G. The strings answer D♭–D♭–D♭–C against that held G, briefly forming a tritone. After the fermata, quiet second violins and bassoons introduce F minor over the cellos’ C.

    4. Two chords become one

      Winds and strings exchange pairs of slow chords. The strings answer with C seventh over G, then F minor over A♭. Soon each reply contains only one chord, with a rest while the other group speaks. A diminuendo leads towards pianissimo F♯ minor; the repeated quavers have disappeared.

    5. Fragments recover the opening rhythm

      Quiet winds and strings trade B–D–F–A♭, the diminished seventh that points towards C minor. Fortissimo A♭–A♭–A♭–F fragments break into those exchanges and crowd closer together. The bass moves from F to three Gs; the opening E♭ arrives under its fermata, now reinforced by winds and horns.

    6. A half cadence becomes an oboe solo

      The orchestra reaches G major, a half cadence in C minor. The strings stop, leaving the oboe on G. Its unaccompanied Adagio descends F–E♭–D, turns through E♭–D–C–D and descends again. A fermata holds the final D before the violins resume their three A♭s falling to F.

    7. Bassoons lead the song into C

      A silent bar precedes a G-major chord over B, the dominant of C. Bassoons take the call: G–G–G–C–D–G. Their final G continues beneath the quiet violin melody, now beginning G–C–B–C. The exposition’s horn call and E♭-major song have returned in C major, a minor third lower.

    8. The closing chord starts another argument

      G major resolves to C major, with G–C in the bass and B–C in the first violins. That arrival also begins the coda. High C alternates between held notes and tremolo while the bass moves C–A♭–E–F. Beneath the repeated C, the chords change from C major to F minor and back.

  2. II. Andante con motoSkidmore College orchestra
    9:59

    Violas and cellos begin a gentle A♭-major song over plucked basses. Beethoven keeps finding new ways to dress it, while trumpets and timpani repeatedly interrupt with a C-major fanfare.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Violas and cellos sing

      The violins wait. Violas and cellos sing in octaves, with a dotted rhythm that gives the A♭-major melody a gentle swing. Underneath, the double basses pluck their notes. Beethoven marks the tune dolce, sweetly.

    2. The trumpets answer in C

      The orchestra breaks into C major. Trumpets and timpani reinforce the dotted tune, while the strings run beneath it. The quiet song has acquired a public, ceremonial voice.

    3. The tune grows smaller notes

      Violas and cellos recover the opening melody in A♭ major, now filling its outline with semiquavers. Plucked basses support them again. The extra notes make the tune flow without changing its three-beat bar.

    4. Two names on the title-page

      Prince Lobkowitz and Count Razumovsky share the Fifth’s dedication. Both names also appear on the Sixth Symphony, its companion at the 1808 première.

    5. “Again!”

      The Choral Fantasy ends the evening of 22 December 1808 badly. Beethoven forgets the plan he gave the players and repeats a section, so the concertmaster stops and calls out dryly: “Again!” Afterwards Beethoven asks the orchestra’s pardon.

    6. The bow works harder

      The lower strings divide the tune into still quicker notes: demisemiquavers. The melody’s outline survives inside the fast bow strokes. Later a fermata clears the air for a quieter succession of clarinet, bassoon and flute phrases.

    7. Four years of work

      Beethoven sketches the Fifth in 1804, soon after the Eroica, and finishes it in 1808. Between times he writes Fidelio, the Razumovsky quartets, the Appassionata, the Violin Concerto, the Fourth Piano Concerto and the Fourth Symphony.

    8. The fanfare loses its brass

      The third C-major fanfare dies down. Trumpets and timpani withdraw, leaving smaller figures in the strings and woodwind. A♭ minor darkens the familiar tune before the opening melody can return in full.

    9. The song fills the orchestra

      The opening tune returns in A♭ major, fortissimo. Winds and violins sing the line that began in violas and cellos, while the lower strings play quick accompanying figures. The melody can bear all that weight.

    10. A bassoon takes a walk

      The coda gives the bassoon a little solo above detached strings. Più moto asks for more movement, still in 3/8. A♭ remains home; Tempo I later restores the opening pace for the last phrases.

    11. A hundred gulden

      Nobody records what the concert of 22 December 1808 earned for Beethoven. A record does show that Prince Nikolaus Esterházy, who had commissioned the Mass in C, ordered 100 gulden paid to him for the benefit.

    12. A♭ has the last word

      The final cadences bring the bass from E♭ to A♭. The key of the low strings’ opening song closes the movement, with the whole orchestra joining in.

    How it is joined

    1. The bass slips back to A♭

      After the C-major fanfare, the bass descends E–E♭–D–D♭–C, then turns through D♭ to E♭. That last note is A♭ major’s dominant. Violas and cellos bring back the opening melody in semiquavers, above plucked basses. The brass falls silent, and the Andante continues at the same pace.

    2. A second descent, still quicker notes

      The C-major fanfare again withdraws through the bass’s E–E♭–D–D♭–C descent. The return to A♭ major brings the low-string melody in demisemiquavers. Its notes crowd together inside the same 3/8 bars, with the original tune still shaping their rise and fall.

    3. The brass withdraws

      The C-major fanfare diminishes. Trumpets and timpani stop while the strings continue with small repeated figures, passing them between upper and lower parts. Woodwind join the quieter conversation. The following A♭-minor passage introduces C♭ and G♭ into the tune’s familiar outline.

    4. The full orchestra recovers the song

      Rising figures pass through the strings, with a crescendo drawing in the winds. The opening melody arrives fortissimo in A♭ major. Winds and first violins sing it together, while violas and cellos fill the accompaniment with quick notes above the basses’ A♭.

    5. The bassoon quickens the step

      The full A♭-major statement gives way to quieter strings. At Più moto, a bassoon sings above detached chords and short bass notes; the three-quaver bar remains. The wind and strings gradually build another crescendo before Tempo I restores the original pace.

  3. III. AllegroSkidmore College orchestra
    5:36

    The Fifth’s scherzo begins in the cellos and double basses, almost under its breath, then answers with a horn call. Its strangest stroke comes at the return: the horns’ challenge has become a whisper, and the timpani begin the long wait for the finale.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Cellos in the dark

      Cellos and double basses climb through the notes of C minor. A quiet answer comes from above, then a pause. The low strings try again. Beethoven leaves the three-beat dance suspended between these phrases.

    2. The horns remember

      Three short notes, then a long one: the horns bring back the rhythm that opened the symphony. Their repeated notes hammer at one pitch after the cellos’ climb through a chord.

    3. The basses take off

      C major, and a running tune for cellos and double basses. The other strings take it up in turn, each new entry catching the previous one before it has finished. The players at the bottom of the orchestra have set everyone running.

    4. Mozart in the sketchbook

      Beethoven’s sketches show that the first and third movements were the first conceived. The sketchbook he uses for the Eroica also links this scherzo to the finale of Mozart’s G minor Symphony, which likewise begins by climbing a minor triad.

    5. An enormous December concert

      Vienna, 22 December 1808: Beethoven introduces the Fifth and Sixth symphonies in the same concert at the Theater an der Wien. He also plays his Fourth Piano Concerto. The evening includes music from the Mass in C and ends with the Choral Fantasy.

    6. Twice through, or once

      Beethoven’s autograph has the scherzo and trio played twice before the last scherzo. The first printed parts leave the repeat out, and Jonathan Del Mar’s Bärenreiter edition argues that Beethoven himself ordered the cut. This performance plays the shorter form.

    7. The scherzo whispers

      The opening returns in C minor, with its voice lowered. Clarinets and bassoons exchange the repeated-note rhythm with plucked strings. The horn call has become something furtive, stepping through the same dance on tiptoe.

    8. Three trombones waiting

      Three trombonists, a piccolo player and a contrabassoon player wait through this movement. Beethoven saves their first notes for the finale. The orchestra still has another sound in reserve.

    9. C against A♭

      The timpani repeat C. Cellos and basses hold A♭ beneath the upper strings’ C, a strange place to wait for C major. The violins begin to stir; a long crescendo carries the music straight into the finale.

    How it is joined

    1. The basses start running

      The scherzo closes in C minor. The trio turns to C major and sends cellos and double basses off in running quavers. Successive string entries carry the tune upwards, with bassoons joining the chase. The three-beat bar stays in place.

    2. The horn call becomes a whisper

      The trio subsides and the low-string arpeggio returns in C minor. Beethoven keeps the scherzo quiet: the repeated-note call passes to clarinets and bassoons, with pizzicato strings around it. The familiar rhythm now comes in small, dry attacks.

    3. Timpani under held strings

      The detached phrases stop. Cellos and basses hold A♭; first violins and violas hold C, marked pianississimo. The timpani repeat C against that bass. Gradually the violin line moves and the orchestra builds a crescendo, carrying the unsettled chord into the finale’s C major.

  4. IV. Allegro — PrestoSkidmore College orchestra
    11:47

    Piccolo, contrabassoon and three trombones join the orchestra for the Fifth’s C-major finale. Beethoven gives them a blazing entrance, then lets the scherzo’s quiet C-minor music return once more before the last charge.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Three trombones join the orchestra

      C–E–G: the opening climbs a C-major triad. Three trombones give the chords a new breadth, while piccolo and contrabassoon extend the orchestra at both ends. Beethoven has saved these players for the finale.

    2. The tune moves to G

      G major brings a lighter exchange between strings and winds. Short repeated notes keep the phrases moving; the opening’s great C-major chords have made room for more nimble conversation.

    3. Better noise than six kettledrums

      In March 1808 Beethoven tells Count Oppersdorff about his three trombones and piccolo. The finale, he promises, “will make more noise than 6 kettledrums and better noise at that”. He knows exactly what those extra players can do.

    4. The brass entrance comes again

      The exposition repeats, restoring the opening C-major march and its full brass sound. The music will travel to G major again before Beethoven takes the themes into less settled territory.

    5. A concert on little rehearsal

      Reichardt reports that Beethoven met great resistance in staging his benefit on 22 December 1808. Singers and orchestra come from many groups, and the long programme, full of difficulties, cannot be rehearsed in full. Four hours of music are played regardless.

    6. The bass slips sideways

      This time the repeated exposition takes its second ending. The low strings and contrabassoon move down by semitones under held wind chords. Running figures spread through the strings and then the woodwind; the themes begin to travel.

    7. In Lobkowitz’s box

      The composer Johann Friedrich Reichardt watches the première from Prince Lobkowitz’s box. “There we sat, in the most bitter cold, from half past six until half past ten,” he writes, and concludes that one may easily have too much of a good thing.

    8. The scherzo returns in whispers

      C minor and the scherzo’s three-beat bars return. Quiet string notes leave gaps for bassoon and clarinet replies; the trombones fall silent. This little recollection casts a shadow across the C-major finale.

    9. The whole orchestra comes back

      The quiet bridge grows into a crescendo, and the opening C-major tune returns with piccolo, contrabassoon and trombones. Its later melodies will also settle in C. The orchestra has recovered the finale’s full reach.

    10. One of the most important works

      E. T. A. Hoffmann reviews the Fifth in 1810 for the Allgemeine musikalische Zeitung and calls it “one of the most important works of the time”. He revises the essay in 1813 for his collection Kreisleriana.

    11. An ending with room left

      The coda gives the familiar figures another long stretch of music. Quiet wind phrases gather players around them, then the pace increases through Sempre più allegro towards Presto. Beethoven still has plenty of C-major chords in reserve.

    12. Fate by the throat

      In November 1801 Beethoven writes to his friend Franz Wegeler that he will “seize fate by the throat”: it shall certainly never wholly overcome him. His hearing has already begun to fail.

    13. C major, again and again

      The orchestra keeps striking C-major chords, with rests making each new attack count. Beethoven stretches the final cadence far beyond a polite farewell. At last, all the players land on C.

    How it is joined

    1. G major hands back to C

      The exposition closes with G-major chords and a brief rest. The repeat restores the opening C-major triad, C–E–G, above C in the bass. Trombones reinforce the wind and string chords, and the piccolo rises over them. The Allegro pace continues.

    2. The second ending lowers the bass

      The second ending keeps the G-major closing music moving. The bass then slips from G through F♯ to F beneath held wind chords, while the strings carry running figures between parts. The Allegro continues as these figures pass into the woodwind and the harmony moves away from G.

    3. The scherzo slips back into the room

      The full orchestra sustains G, the dominant of C, before Tempo I changes the metre to 3/4. C-minor scherzo music returns in pianissimo strings. The bass leaves spaces between its short notes, and bassoon and clarinet answer above. Piccolo, contrabassoon and trombones fall silent.

    4. The quiet bridge fills with players

      The quiet wind phrases and repeated viola chords gather into a crescendo. Strings return to the bow; timpani join the approach. Allegro and common time restore the opening C-major tune over C in the bass, with piccolo, contrabassoon and all three trombones back in the orchestra.

    5. The closing music keeps moving

      The C-major closing music continues into running string figures and forceful wind chords. Cello and double bass carry the figures down into the bottom of the orchestra. The Allegro still holds; later, quiet bassoon and horn phrases begin another build towards the coda’s faster ending.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. I. Allegro con brio — side 1 of 2Koninklijk Concertgebouworkest, Willem Mengelberg · 1939–40
    4:43

    Willem Mengelberg and the Concertgebouw Orchestra take Beethoven’s four-note opening from fierce octaves to the soft exchanges of the development. This is the first of two disc sides: the movement continues after the needle reaches the end.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Three Gs, then a hold

      Beethoven begins with a rest, three Gs and an E♭ held under a fermata. Three Fs fall to a longer D. Mengelberg has two written pauses to shape before the strings begin passing the rhythm between them.

    2. A horn call opens out

      The horns keep the three-short-note rhythm and widen its intervals: B♭–B♭–B♭, E♭, F, B♭. The violins answer with a sweet E♭-major song. Below it, the cellos continue the little repeated-note figure.

    3. A Telefunken disc in 1941

      Telefunken issues this performance in 1941. The Concertgebouw Orchestra plays under Mengelberg; SK 2210 carries the first part of the opening movement. A listener at home needs the other side to finish it.

    4. One chord at a time

      The development pares the four-note rhythm down to slow chords. Winds and strings reply across rests, with Beethoven asking for ever softer playing. The thin exchanges eventually break open as the short repeated notes return.

  2. I. Allegro con brioBerlin Philharmonic Orchestra, Arthur Nikisch · 10 November 1913
    6:45

    In 1913, Arthur Nikisch and the Berlin Philharmonic play Beethoven’s Fifth into a recording horn. The omitted exposition repeat takes the first movement straight from its E♭-major song into the development, where the familiar rhythm begins to break apart.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Two pauses in five bars

      Three Gs fall to E♭; three Fs fall to D. Each long note has a fermata, giving Nikisch room to hold the orchestra still. The second hold lasts across two written bars.

    2. An orchestra before a horn

      On 10 November 1913, Nikisch and the Berlin Philharmonic record the whole symphony in one day. Engineer Max Hampe captures their playing through a horn: air moves a diaphragm, and a stylus cuts the music into wax.

    3. Horns before the violin song

      The horns stretch the opening rhythm into a call across wider intervals. Their last B♭ stays beneath the violin melody, which begins softly in E♭ major. The cellos keep the repeated notes moving under the song.

    4. Straight into the development

      Nikisch leaves out the exposition repeat. After the E♭-major cadence, the horns’ three B♭s fall to G; the strings answer D♭–D♭–D♭–C. That clash begins the development immediately.

    5. The opening with reinforcements

      G–G–G–E♭ returns, with winds and horns reinforcing the strings. Beethoven has rebuilt the opening out of short fragments and quiet chord exchanges. The fermatas stop the orchestra again.

    6. One oboe remains

      A G-major chord stops the orchestra, but the oboe keeps its note. Alone, it descends F–E♭–D, adds a little turn and descends again. Beethoven writes Adagio over this tiny solo inside the Allegro.

    7. Bassoons take the horn call

      The bassoons inherit the horn call, now G–G–G–C–D–G. The violin melody follows in C major. Its first leap, G up to C, keeps the familiar shape while bringing the song closer to the home key.

    8. The bass shifts under C

      The violins stay high on C while the bass moves C–A♭–E–F. Those changing foundations keep the cadence from settling. The coda has a long way to go before its closing C-minor chords.

  3. I. Allegro con brioOdeon Grand Orchestra; conductor disputed (Friedrich Kark / Eduard Künneke) · 1910–11
    6:30

    Odeon’s early recording sends Beethoven’s four-note opening through a horn and onto shellac. The horn calls, violin song and solitary oboe carry a whole orchestral drama across that small surface.

    Notes as it plays
    1. A rest before the blow

      The famous opening starts with a quaver rest. Then three Gs fall to E♭, held under a fermata; three Fs answer, falling to D. The pauses are as carefully written as the attacks.

    2. A melody above repeated notes

      After the horn call, the first violins sing in E♭ major, marked dolce. The cellos keep returning to three short B♭s and a longer E♭. Beethoven lets the new tune rest on the old rhythm.

    3. Eight sides of Beethoven

      Odeon issues the complete symphony on eight sides, numbered 76147–76154. The labels name the company orchestra and leave the conductor unnamed. At home, Beethoven’s continuous arguments arrive in portions small enough to fit a disc.

    4. The strings answer in D♭

      The horns’ repeated B♭s fall to G. The strings answer with three D♭s and a C, briefly clashing against the held horn note. The opening rhythm starts leading into new keys.

    5. The rhythm almost disappears

      Winds and strings exchange single slow chords, with a rest between each reply. Beethoven writes a diminuendo, then asks for still softer playing. The four-note rhythm has dwindled to the barest outline.

    6. An Adagio for one oboe

      The orchestra stops after a G-major chord. The oboe continues alone, descending through F, E♭ and D, then turning around the line before another held D. A few notes make a private room inside the symphony.

    7. The call changes players

      Bassoons take over the earlier horn call. Its new notes, G–G–G–C–D–G, prepare the violin melody in C major. The same tune now sits a minor third lower.

    8. C refuses to settle

      High C stays in the violins while the bass changes beneath it: C, A♭, E and F. A cadence becomes the start of the coda. The final chords will bring the movement back to C minor.
