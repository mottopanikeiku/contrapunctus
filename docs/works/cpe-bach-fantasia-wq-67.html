Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach / II. Classical

Fantasia, Wq 67

1787 · Wq 67 · Keyboard

A late fantasia moving between measured passages and the freedom of recitative. The related version with violin is titled C. P. E. Bachs Empfindungen — Bach’s feelings.

13 min

The recording

  1. Fantasia in F♯ minor, Wq 67Joan Benson
    12:33

    Joan Benson plays Bach’s late F♯ minor Fantasia on the clavichord, an instrument whose quiet voice can still snap from a whisper to an outburst. Long runs spill beyond the bar lines; little chords stop them short.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Two bars, then freedom

      Adagio. Small pairs of chords sound above a steady bass, separated by rests. After two measured bars, the music breaks loose: a run rises through the keyboard, and the bar lines disappear. Sudden piano and forte markings interrupt one another within a single phrase.

    2. A pianist starts again

      Benson arrives at Fritz Neumeyer’s clavichord lessons as an accomplished pianist. He refuses to let her play pieces at first. For months she works only on making a tone. Recalling those lessons in 1999, she says, “I had to become a beginner again”.

    3. Bach’s rule for moving people

      “A musician cannot move others unless he too is moved.” Bach writes this in his 1753 keyboard treatise. He singles out improvisations and fantasias as places where the player can most directly command an audience’s feelings.

    4. Nearly eleven in Hamburg

      After dinner in Hamburg in 1772, Bach sits at his clavichord for Charles Burney. He plays until nearly eleven. Burney watches his fixed eyes, fallen lower lip and gathering sweat; the little instrument has its player completely absorbed.

    5. Benson tries to make a cut

      Preparing this fantasia for a 1961 radio appearance, Benson considers shortening it. She finds she cannot: each section leads into the next. In her 1999 interview with Richard Troeger, she recalls finding the connection in the bass line and allowing the piece to change with every performance.

    6. A recording among the stars

      Benson’s first recording sessions in 1962 need three locations. Cleaners interrupt one; a radio station intrudes on another. Finally, a house in Marin County gives her quiet. Between takes she goes outside to look at the stars or pet the dog.

    7. Largo over repeated F♯s

      The last Largo brings back bar lines and a twelve-eight metre. Repeated low F♯s hold the music in place while the right hand makes its final turns. The last phrase is marked pianissimo; rests follow the closing chord.
