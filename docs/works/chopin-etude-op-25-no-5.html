Frédéric Chopin / III. Romanticism

Étude, Op. 25 No. 5

before 1837 · Piano

4 min

The recording

  1. VivaceEdward Neeman
    3:45

    Quick semitone clashes give this E-minor étude its crooked smile; the middle opens into an E-major song deep in the piano. Edward Neeman plays the two sides of Chopin’s little scherzo.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A semitone sideways

      The right hand keeps brushing against the note next door. These quick semitone clashes give the E-minor tune a teasing edge. Chopin marks the music scherzando — joking — and breaks its phrases into short, lightly played gestures.

    2. The left hand sings

      Più lento: slower, and now in E major. The melody moves down into the left hand, in the range of a cello. Above it, the right hand rises and falls in flowing figures. The little jokes have stopped for a song.

    3. Marie d’Agoult’s études

      The twelve études of Op. 25 appear in 1837 with a dedication to Marie d’Agoult, the companion of Chopin’s friend Franz Liszt.

    4. A sonata from his teacher

      Larry Sitsky, one of Neeman’s teachers, writes his First Piano Sonata especially for him. Neeman’s debut album in 2016 places its first recording beside Rachmaninoff.

    5. The clipped tune returns

      The E-minor opening returns at its quicker pace. The low singing line disappears, and the piano resumes its broken rhythms and little dissonant nudges. The hands have traded long phrases for short exchanges.

    6. E major has the last word

      The ending suddenly grows emphatic. Fortissimo chords and an upward flourish carry the piano into E major, the key of the middle song. Chopin ends his light scherzo with both hands speaking at full strength.

    How it is joined

    1. E major brings a low melody

      The E-minor scherzando closes and Più lento introduces E major. The left hand carries a sustained melody in the cello’s range. Rising and falling right-hand figures replace the opening’s clipped chords and semitone clashes.

    2. E minor brings back the joke

      The E-major song closes, and the quick E-minor opening returns. The left hand leaves its long melody; the piano resumes short broken chords and semitone dissonances. The marking scherzando again describes the clipped exchange between the hands.

    3. The piano ends in E major

      The returning E-minor scherzando leads to a forceful coda. Fortissimo chords and an ascending piano flourish establish E major. The major key of the middle song now supplies the closing harmony.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. VivaceMartha Goldstein, piano
    3:08

    Martha Goldstein plays an 1851 Érard in this étude of awkward little clashes and a generous E-major song. Chopin asks the same piano to joke in short, broken phrases and then sing like a cello.

    Notes as it plays
    1. An Érard from 1851

      Goldstein’s piano is an Érard built in 1851, two years after Chopin’s death. The recording comes from her survey of the études for Pandora Records, preserved in the Al Goldstein collection.

    2. A cello inside the piano

      The middle section changes to E major and Più lento. Its melody lies low, where a cello might sing, while the right hand supplies rising and falling figures. Chopin writes long phrases here after the opening’s clipped semitone clashes.

    3. Back to the short phrases

      The E-minor tune returns with its quick dissonances. Chopin’s scherzando writing flicks between broken chords and short replies. The same hands that carried the middle song now have to release each little gesture quickly.

    4. The Érard’s last chords

      The close expands into fortissimo chords and an upward flourish in E major. The right hand’s final G♯ hangs high above the bass.
