Lili Boulanger / V. The French Line

Pie Jesu

1918 · Solo voice, string quartet, harp and organ

Dictated to Nadia when Lili could no longer write. The prayer for rest leaves each of its few instruments exposed around the solo voice.

5 min

The recording

  1. Pie JesuAlain Fauqueur, boy soprano; J. J. Grunenwald, organ; members of the Orchestre des Concerts Lamoureux; Igor Markevitch, conductor
    4:46

    Alain Fauqueur’s boy soprano sings Boulanger’s last completed work, a prayer surrounded by restless organ chords and four solo strings. The harp waits until the closing pages, where the music finds G major.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Semitones beneath the prayer

      The organ’s paired notes creep up and down by semitones. Above them, the strings hold their notes and the voice stretches the Latin prayer into long phrases. The harp has nothing to play yet.

    2. Nadia takes the dictation

      In 1918 Lili is too ill to write the notes herself. From her bed she dictates Pie Jesu to Nadia, her elder sister. It is her last completed work.

    3. The organ grows

      With the singer silent, the organ takes the chromatic figure into both hands. Boulanger asks the player to add stops little by little. The string lines climb with it, and the score insists on Sans presser — without hurrying.

    4. A plaintive voice

      The voice returns pianissimo, marked plaintif. Underneath, the organ pedal repeats the same low note while the upper chords shift. Little string solos enter around the singer: first viola, then second violin and cello.

    5. A boy’s voice

      A boy sings this prayer. The Everest sleeve names the soloist as Alain Fauqueur of the Petits Chanteurs de Vincennes, and David Noakes’s note on the sleeve hears in the music “ineffable serenity” at the composer’s last hour.

    6. The waiting harp

      The harp finally joins in. Boulanger marks the strings muted and the tempo Lent; the voice is calm and clear. Chromatic notes still pass through the accompaniment, but the final Amen settles over a low G, and the last chord is G major.

    7. Nadia’s lifelong ambition

      Lili dies at Mézy-sur-Seine on 15 March 1918, aged twenty-four. Forty-two years later Everest issues this record, and its sleeve says it had been Nadia’s “lifelong ambition” to have her younger sister’s music recorded.

    How it is joined

    1. The organ takes both lines

      At figure 2 the singer rests. The organ’s chromatic figure spreads into both hands above long pedal notes, and the strings climb through a crescendo. Boulanger gradually adds organ stops, then writes Sans presser: the G-minor prayer swells without a faster beat.

    2. One quiet voice

      The strings cut off after their forceful chord. The organ drops to pianissimo, its pedal beating a repeated low note beneath held, shifting chords. The voice resumes with Pie Jesu, marked plaintif; the viola and other strings return one at a time.

    3. G major, with harp

      A long ritardando leads to Lent and a new, one-sharp key signature. The harp enters, the strings put on mutes, and the organ holds a low G. Semitones still pass through the upper parts; G major becomes fully clear in the final Amen.
