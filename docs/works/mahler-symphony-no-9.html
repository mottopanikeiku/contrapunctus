Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Symphony No. 9

1909–10 · Orchestra

Begins in D major with a halting, syncopated figure in the cellos and horn and ends in D♭ with an Adagio that thins to nothing, marked ersterbend — dying away. In 1912 Berg called the first movement the most magnificent thing Mahler had written.

4 recordings · 1 h 11 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Andante comodoVienna Philharmonic; Bruno Walter, conductor · 16 January 1938
    25:01

    Mahler’s Ninth grows from a broken rhythm and a two-note sigh into an immense first movement. Bruno Walter conducts the Vienna Philharmonic in 1938, in the hall where he gave its première in 1912.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A rhythm finding its feet

      Cellos begin a halting rhythm, pp, and a horn completes it, stopped and then marked offen (Echo). The harp answers at f with firmer steps, a double bass holds a harmonic, and the violas begin to rock. Mahler starts with separate voices, each leaving room for the next.

    2. A song in short phrases

      At rehearsal 1 the second violins begin a melody in short segments separated by rests, full of small falling sighs. Divided violas rock beneath it in sextuplets, and cellos and basses pluck the steps.

    3. Walter returns to the Musikverein

      Vienna, 16 January 1938. Bruno Walter stands before the orchestra that gave the Ninth its première under him in June 1912, in this same Musikverein hall. Mahler never heard the symphony; he died a year before that first performance.

    4. D major turns minor

      After only a few minutes the music moves into the minor for a darker, impassioned passage. It proves short-lived: a quasi-fanfare in the trumpets brings back the opening material in heightened form.

    5. A falling second, and Les Adieux

      Every movement of the Ninth is built on a falling second, from the third to the second step of the scale. Those are the first two notes of Beethoven’s Das Lebewohl Sonata, Op. 81a. In 1875 Mahler’s father let his fifteen-year-old son study in Vienna after the boy had played that sonata.

    6. A chord that settles nothing

      The exposition ends on a great chord from the whole orchestra, and then everything dies away over a timpani roll that falls from ff to pp. No goal has been reached.

    7. The horns remember

      Tempo I subito (aber nicht schleppend) — back to the first tempo, but without dragging. Horns and trombones, muted, bring back motives from the opening bars over a timpani roll, pp, and the lyrical tune appears only in fragments.

    8. Two machines beside the timpani

      Producer Fred Gaisberg sits near the timpani, facing Walter. Two machines take turns cutting the performance into wax: one records while the other is reloaded. Beside the engineer, a musician follows the full score, keeping the joins clear of the music’s path.

    9. A waltz for lost youth

      The violins bring in a gentle tune that quotes Johann Strauss’s waltz Freuet euch des Lebens, written in 1870 for the opening ball of the Musikverein’s new golden hall, in the building of the Conservatory where Mahler studied. It returns twice more.

    10. Trumpets stand fast

      Muted trumpets quietly play triplet figures. The orchestra swells: timpani rolls, violin trills, horns marked gestopft climbing to fff, until at Mit Wut the full orchestra erupts.

    11. “Mit Wut”

      At rehearsal 9 Mahler writes Mit Wut, with rage, and Allegro risoluto, but not too fast. The horns are marked immer gestopft, always stopped; they snarl beside open trumpets while the strings fight in dissonant counterpoint.

    12. A cry, then a collapse

      A cymbal stroke, swells in the harps and a sweeping horn line carry the music to a brilliant, dissonant peak. At rehearsal 11 the key signature changes to E♭ major, and rapid chromatic runs plunge through violas, cellos and basses as the orchestra collapses.

    13. Strings in the shadows

      Mahler writes Schattenhaft — shadowy. Horns sigh above muted trombones while the violas, cellos and basses rock in whispered sextuplets. Then Allmählich an Ton gewinnend — gradually gaining in tone — and the lyrical tune begins to come back in D major.

    14. “O days of youth!”

      Over the Strauss waltz in the draft Mahler writes, in the score itself: “O Jugendzeit! Entschwundene! O Liebe! Verwehte!” — oh, days of youth, vanished; oh, love, scattered on the wind.

    15. A presentiment of death

      Alban Berg writes to his wife that this whole movement is “a presentiment of death”. It “appears again and again”, he says, and “all that is earthly and dreamy culminates in it”.

    16. A dissonance mit höchster Gewalt

      Trombones and tuba, bells raised, hammer a dissonance marked fff (mit höchster Gewalt) — with the greatest force — with the timpani, while woodwinds and strings tumble downward in rapid figures. The score says Stringendo just before it.

    17. Like a funeral procession

      The blow leads into a march marked Wie ein schwerer Kondukt, like a heavy funeral procession, over a timpani ostinato. Three deep bells join the timpani.

    18. Whose heartbeat?

      Leonard Bernstein heard Mahler’s irregular heartbeat in the opening rhythm. The rhythm keeps returning in the score; at the movement’s crisis, trombones and tuba hammer it out mit höchster Gewalt, with the greatest force.

    19. Birdcalls

      Plötzlich bedeutend langsamer (Lento) und leise, Misterioso: the music drops to a hush. Piccolo, flute, oboes and a solo violin weave delicate lines that commentators hear as birdcalls.

    20. A horn opens the coda

      Schon ganz langsam — already very slow. A horn, marked molto espr. and sehr weich hervortretend — very softly to the fore — sings in D major; harp arpeggios rise beneath held wind chords and the strings fade to a few notes.

    21. A solo violin at the end

      The closing melody passes to a solo violin, marked schmeichelnd — coaxing — and later dolciss. The harp climbs; horns and woodwinds answer softly. At the last bar, Zögernd, the strings pluck harmonics on the G string, pp, under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. The music turns minor

      After only a few minutes the D-major song gives way to the minor for a darker, impassioned passage. It proves short-lived: a quasi-fanfare in the trumpets brings back the opening material in heightened form.

    2. A timpani roll fades

      The exposition ends on a great chord. A timpani roll survives it and fades from ff to pp. At Tempo I subito horns and muted trombones bring back motives from the opening bars.

    3. Trumpets set the orchestra swelling

      Muted trumpets play quiet triplets, and the orchestra swells with timpani rolls, trills and stopped horns rising to fff. At Mit Wut. Allegro risoluto, with horns immer gestopft, the full orchestra presents the main theme.

    4. Schattenhaft

      A fermata, and then Schattenhaft: horns sigh above muted trombones while the low strings rock in whispered sextuplets. Allmählich an Ton gewinnend, the D-major tune begins to return.

    5. A dissonance in trombones and tuba

      At Stringendo the orchestra presses forward, and woodwinds and strings tumble downward. Trombones and tuba strike a dissonance marked fff (mit höchster Gewalt) with the timpani.

    6. The opening comes back

      The development goes on wie ein schwerer Kondukt, with muted trumpets and bells. Then, at Wie von Anfang, the opening returns: harp, plucked low strings and timpani under horns and woodwinds, marked espress.

    7. The coda begins

      At Schon ganz langsam a horn sings molto espr., harp arpeggios rise beneath held wind chords, and the strings thin out to a few notes.

  2. II. Im Tempo eines gemächlichen LändlersVienna Philharmonic; Bruno Walter, conductor · 16 January 1938
    15:46

    Mahler’s country dancers have heavy shoes, and his waltzers have sharp elbows. Three dances share the second movement of the Ninth, until the orchestra leaves only a piccolo and a contrabassoon to pick up the pieces.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Fiddles in heavy shoes

      Bassoons and violas offer a quick upbeat, and the clarinets answer. The second violins bring in the Ländler, a country dance. Mahler marks the page Etwas täppisch und sehr derb — somewhat clumsy and very coarse — and asks the violins to play wie Fiedeln, like fiddles.

    2. The waltz elbows in

      The tempo suddenly quickens — Poco più mosso subito (Tempo II) — and a waltz elbows in, louder and weighted on the downbeat, in four sharps. The sigh-shaped melodic lines of the first movement are still there, but chromaticism and dissonance grow.

    3. The fish sermon, quoted

      The movement quotes the scherzo of Mahler’s Second Symphony, his orchestral version of St Anthony’s sermon to the fish, for a few bars among the dances.

    4. A trial in rehearsal

      Fred Gaisberg later admitted that rehearsing this Ländler movement for the recording was a time-consuming trial. He had arranged five rehearsals before the concert so that his engineers could test their equipment.

    5. A slower pair of feet

      After a long Molto riten. a solo bassoon leads into Tempo III (Ländler, ganz langsam), a very slow dance. The sigh figures of the faster dances are still in it, but at this pace, with a triangle added, the mood lightens.

    6. The waltz spins faster

      The waltz returns, reharmonized and varied. At rehearsal 22 the key signature is two sharps, the strings alternate plucked and bowed notes, and the woodwinds add little turns to the tune.

    7. Three Ninths in Vienna

      At the Vienna festival in June 1912, Walter introduces Mahler’s Ninth alongside two older Ninths: Beethoven’s, conducted by Felix Weingartner, and Bruckner’s, under Arthur Nikisch. Three conductors take on three very different endings.

    8. A dance for fewer players

      Two bassoons quietly recover the opening upbeat. The old Ländler now belongs to a solo second violin and a solo viola, with the other violas plucking beneath. Its heavy shoes have become much easier to lift.

    9. Seventy minutes

      Walter’s performance lasts about seventy minutes, close to the “hour and thirteen minutes actual playing length” of the première in 1912 and eleven minutes brisker than his own stereo recording of 1961.

    10. The dancers lose the tune

      The first tempo returns abruptly and the Ländler resumes with uncharacteristically heavy accents. They soon lighten, and the dance begins to come apart in fragments.

    11. “The dance is over”

      Bruno Walter wrote of this movement in 1936: “A tragic undertone sounds in the joy and one feels that ‘the dance is over’.”

    12. The dance slips away

      In the coda, muted sounds and a pianissimo give the music a mischievous air. Fleeting fragments of the Ländler emerge and sneak away, instrument by instrument.

    13. Piccolo meets contrabassoon

      At opposite ends of the orchestra, piccolo and contrabassoon share the opening upbeat, under the marking Nicht eilen bis zum Schluss — don’t hurry to the end. The dance has dwindled to this improbable pair. Plucked strings supply the last small punctuation.

    How it is joined

    1. The waltz arrives suddenly

      The first Ländler begins to fade. Then, at Poco più mosso subito (Tempo II), the tempo quickens and the waltz intrudes, louder and weighted on the downbeat, with a new key signature of four sharps.

    2. A bassoon upbeat slows the dance

      A long Molto riten. slows the waltz almost to a stop. A solo bassoon leads into Tempo III (Ländler, ganz langsam), a very slow dance that keeps the sigh figures of the faster ones.

    3. The waltz gathers from little scraps

      The slow Ländler gives way to the waltz again, reharmonized and varied. By rehearsal 22 the key signature is two sharps, the strings alternate plucked and bowed notes and the woodwinds add little turns.

    4. Two bassoons recover the beginning

      The slow dance fades. Two bassoons quietly bring back the opening upbeat, and the Ländler returns in a solo second violin and a solo viola, with the other violas plucking.

    5. The first tempo comes back

      The waltz is marked etwas frischer, somewhat brisker, and then the first tempo abruptly returns. The Ländler resumes with heavy accents, which soon lighten.

  3. III. Rondo-Burleske — Allegro assai. Sehr trotzigVienna Philharmonic; Bruno Walter, conductor · 16 January 1938
    11:26

    The Ninth’s Rondo-Burleske makes the whole orchestra a virtuoso, with jagged tunes elbowing past one another at speed. In the middle, a trumpet sings a D-major melody that the finale will remember.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A trumpet starts a fight

      A solo trumpet throws out a short figure, and Mahler marks the movement Allegro assai. Sehr trotzig — very defiant. Strings answer with jagged figures; horns and woodwinds add short ones of their own. Then the violins take up the main melody at ff, a tune that keeps arguing with itself.

    2. To my brothers in Apollo

      Mahler dedicates the movement, with a sneer, “to my brothers in Apollo”: his fellow disciples of the god of music.

    3. A tune out strolling

      The first episode lightens the tone: smaller forces, softer dynamics, one flat in the key signature, and melodies with the swing of operetta. The rondo’s dissonance is still there underneath.

    4. Vienna before the Anschluss

      This performance takes place in January 1938. Two months later, Nazi Germany annexes Austria. Walter, already driven from Germany because he is Jewish, must leave his adopted country too; Mahler’s music disappears from its concert programmes.

    5. A muted trumpet calls

      A muted trumpet calls out twice, ff and fading at once, while a triangle roll swells and the violins churn on repeated notes.

    6. Counterpoint, in Walter’s words

      In 1936 Bruno Walter called this movement “defiantly agitated” and found in it “most striking proof” of Mahler’s contrapuntal mastery.

    7. The trumpet starts to sing

      A cymbal stroke halts the fugue. Brass chords sound a dissonant chorale, and then, at Etwas gehalten, D major arrives: a solo trumpet, marked p subito poco espressivo, sings a gentle melody over trembling strings. That little turn will return in the finale.

    8. A bewildered refugee in Paris

      A few weeks after the concert, Fred Gaisberg meets Walter in Paris to play him the test pressings: “a bewildered refugee”. Walter is “so delighted” with the results that his “usually sober face brightened up considerably”.

    9. Harp sweeps clear the stage

      Scraps of the rondo keep creeping into the song, and each time a harp glissando sweeps them away, leaving the violins trembling am Steg, near the bridge, in divided parts.

    10. The song is turned away

      The mood darkens. The oboe, and then other instruments, keep trying to bring the lyrical melody back, but the orchestra rejects it, and the rondo’s fragments creep back.

    11. Faster, then faster again

      Più stretto, then Presto: the closing pages tighten the screw. Winds, brass and strings repeat the opening’s short figures with fierce accents. The movement ends on the same kind of sharp call that set it going.

    How it is joined

    1. A cymbal crash, then D major

      A cymbal stroke clears away the crowded lines. Flutter-tongued flutes and trembling strings hold on, brass chords sound a dissonant chorale, and at Etwas gehalten the harmony settles into D major under a solo trumpet.

    2. A harp glissando clears the scraps

      The broad melody falls apart into replies. Scraps of the rondo break in, and a harp glissando sweeps them away, restoring the quiet string tremolo, am Steg.

    3. The short figures accelerate

      The repeated figures press into Più stretto and later Presto. Strings, winds and brass crowd the short opening motifs together, with percussion reinforcing their accents. The final A-minor attacks cut off the rush.

  4. IV. Adagio — Sehr langsam und noch zurückhaltendVienna Philharmonic; Bruno Walter, conductor · 16 January 1938
    18:30

    The Ninth ends with a string hymn in D♭ major, a semitone below the key in which the symphony began. Mahler lets its long phrases break into smaller and smaller pieces, until even a single viola note can hold the room.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The violins speak together

      Both violin sections begin in unison, with a forceful rising leap and falling phrase. Then the other strings enter quietly, dividing into many lines. The little turn that flickered through the Rondo-Burleske has become part of a hymn.

    2. A hymn tune, and heaven

      Commentators hear the hymn tune Eventide, “Abide with Me”, in the opening theme. As the theme grows more assertive it also touches a phrase from Urlicht, sung there to “Je lieber möcht’ ich im Himmel sein”: I would rather be in heaven.

    3. A bassoon below the hymn

      The strings leave a note hanging in the first violin, p subito and morendo. From far below, a solo bassoon marked pp langsam climbs slowly towards it. This small, bare passage will keep returning between the hymn’s much fuller statements.

    4. A middle section becomes an elegy

      The movement’s hymn incorporates a direct quotation from the middle section of the Rondo-Burleske. Here it becomes an elegy, a fervent prayer that fits inside the hymn’s falling phrases.

    5. An enormous empty space

      Mahler writes Plötzlich wieder langsam (wie zu Anfang) und etwas zögernd — suddenly slow again, as at the start, and a little hesitant. Contrabassoon and cellos bring back the bassoon’s rising line far below, while the violins, in their highest register, play pp ohne Empfindung — without feeling.

    6. Mahler’s music in slow motion

      Reinhold Kubik hears this twenty-minute finale as a review of Mahler’s own music in slow motion: Urlicht, Ablösung im Sommer, the scherzo of the Third, Der Abschied and Oft denk’ ich all echo in it. A circle, he writes, seems to be closing.

    7. The hymn returns in pieces

      The interlude presses forward at Pesante, then a rit. leads to a tempo (Molto adagio) in five flats. The hymn is back, warmed by a horn, the violins and violas told to play stets breitester Strich — always the broadest stroke.

    8. Three kinds of death

      Leonard Bernstein ended his fifth Norton lecture by suggesting that this whole movement prophesies three deaths: Mahler’s own, the death of tonality, and the death of “Faustian” culture in all the arts.

    9. Walter’s melting cloud

      In his 1936 book on Mahler, Bruno Walter describes this finale as “like the melting of a cloud in the ethereal blue”. Two years later he conducts the performance playing here, with the Vienna Philharmonic.

    10. Der Abschied in the woodwinds

      High woodwinds weave intertwining lines that refer to Der Abschied, the farewell that closes Das Lied von der Erde. Beneath them the ascending bassoon idea surfaces again, now without its earlier bleakness.

    11. The orchestra leaves the strings

      The orchestra thins at dim. and morendo. High violins hold a note at ppp above the dwindling winds, an English horn answers with an espr. phrase, and soon only strings remain, marked ersterbend — dying away.

    12. A page of farewells

      Adagissimo. Muted second violins begin a small rising phrase, and the remaining strings answer in scraps, with long rests between them. Whole tunes have shrunk to a turn, a falling step, a note held until the next breath.

    13. “Welt! Lebe wohl!”

      On the last page of the draft Mahler wrote words over the music: “O Schönheit! Liebe!” — then “Lebt wol! Lebt wol!” and, over the final pianissimo, “Welt! Lebe wohl!” World, farewell. He signs off the draft at Toblach in the first days of September 1909.

    14. “The day is fine”, one last time

      On the closing pages the first violins quote the Kindertotenlieder: “Der Tag ist schön auf jenen Höh’n”, the day is fine on yonder heights. The last note is marked ersterbend, dying away.

    15. The last page

      On the last page the second violins and lower strings play stets mit Dämpfer, always muted, while the first violins play without a mute. Mahler writes Langsam und ppp bis zum Schluss, then Äusserst langsam and pppp, and under the final fermata, ersterbend — dying away.

    How it is joined

    1. The low instruments take over

      At Plötzlich wieder langsam (wie zu Anfang) und etwas zögernd the music drops to pp. Contrabassoon and cellos take up the rising line the bassoon played before, while the violins, far above, play pp ohne Empfindung — without feeling.

    2. A rit., then the hymn in D♭ major

      The interlude presses forward at Pesante, then a rit. leads to a tempo (Molto adagio) in five flats. The violins and violas play stets breitester Strich — always the broadest stroke — and a horn adds its warmth.

    3. A high note above the orchestra

      The orchestra withdraws at dim. and morendo. A high violin note sustains at ppp, the woodwinds drop out, an English horn answers with an espr. phrase, and the strings are left alone, marked ersterbend.

    4. Muted strings at Adagissimo

      At Adagissimo the second violins, violas, cellos and basses take their mutes and the strings continue alone, Langsam und ppp bis zum Schluss, their phrases broken by rests.
