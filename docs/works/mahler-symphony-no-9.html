Gustav Mahler / IV. Fin de siècle

Symphony No. 9

1909–10 · Orchestra

Begins in D major with a halting, syncopated figure in the cellos and horn and ends in D♭ with an Adagio that thins to nothing, marked ersterbend — dying away. In 1912 Berg called the first movement the most magnificent thing Mahler had written.

4 recordings · 1 h 11 min

In the Hall

  1. I. Andante comodoVienna Philharmonic; Bruno Walter, conductor · 16 January 1938
    25:01

    Mahler’s Ninth grows from a broken rhythm and a two-note sigh into an immense first movement. Bruno Walter conducts the Vienna Philharmonic in 1938, in the hall where he gave its première in 1912.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A rhythm finding its feet

      Cellos begin a halting rhythm and a horn completes it. The harp answers with firmer steps, then another horn tries a little melody. Mahler starts with separate voices, each leaving room for the next.

    2. Two notes become a song

      The second violins take a falling second — just two neighbouring notes — and grow a D-major tune from it. Divided violas rock underneath. The melody keeps coming back to that small downward sigh.

    3. Walter returns to the Musikverein

      Vienna, 16 January 1938. Bruno Walter stands before the orchestra that gave the Ninth its première under him in June 1912, in this same Musikverein hall. Mahler never heard the symphony; he died a year before that first performance.

    4. D major turns minor

      D minor darkens the music. First violins push upwards in angular phrases, leaning on weak beats; pieces of the gentler tune fall around them. The two melodies pull in opposite directions.

    5. The horns remember

      The orchestra subsides to a timpani roll. Four horns bring back the broken rhythm from the opening; the timpani answer with the steadier one. Low winds and strings pick up the scraps in the uneasy country between B♭ major and G minor.

    6. Two machines beside the timpani

      Producer Fred Gaisberg sits near the timpani, facing Walter. Two machines take turns cutting the performance into wax: one records while the other is reloaded. Beside the engineer, a musician follows the full score, keeping the joins clear of the music’s path.

    7. Strings in the shadows

      Low strings begin their rocking figure again, barely stirring. Above them the violins climb by semitones, one group following another. Horns repeat the little falling sigh as the D-major melody slowly finds its way back.

    8. The small rhythm becomes enormous

      Trombones and tuba thunder out the hesitant rhythm that began the symphony. The running notes fall away around it. Timpani, plucked basses and muted violins are left to answer in fragments.

    9. A horn opens the coda

      A horn lifts the minor third back into D major. Harp arpeggios rise beneath a held wind chord, and the horn continues a long phrase over rocking cellos. The orchestra is becoming a handful of players.

    10. One D left ringing

      The closing melody passes to a solo violin. Clarinets and horns answer with falling seconds; the harp climbs through a D-major chord. At the end, a plucked note catches the light of a held D in piccolo and cello.

    How it is joined

    1. The horn’s sigh turns dark

      The horn repeats the falling second as the harmony turns to D minor. Cellos add rising triplets; first violins press into the sharper second theme on a weak beat. Falling phrases from the first tune remain in the accompanying parts.

    2. A B♭ chord leaves a timpani roll

      The exposition reaches a B♭-major chord with an added sixth. A timpani roll survives it and fades. Four horns recover the opening’s broken rhythm, then the timpani answer with the other opening figure as the harmony moves between B♭ and G minor.

    3. Trumpet calls set the strings running

      Muted trumpet tattoos pass to the timpani as the orchestra grows. Winds and stopped horns sound the rising call; low winds and strings answer with descending chromatic notes. The pace quickens, with triplets crossing the strings’ semiquavers.

    4. The low strings begin again

      The short descending phrases dwindle into a pause. Low strings quietly restart the rocking figure; first and second violins climb by semitones in overlapping lines. Horns keep repeating a falling minor second until D major begins to emerge.

    5. The opening rhythm in trombones and tuba

      Rushing orchestral lines plunge downwards. Through them, trombones and tuba hammer an enlarged version of the first cello-and-horn rhythm. The rapid notes die away, leaving timpani blows and the low brass to repeat the opening figures.

    6. Harp and basses recover the pulse

      A muted trumpet call accompanies the cadence into D major. Harp and plucked low strings recover the steadier opening rhythm. Horns continue the first theme, with clarinets and shimmering violins moving beneath it.

    7. The horn raises the third

      The rising string fragments disappear in D minor. A horn raises the third by a semitone, restoring D major. Harp arpeggios and a sustained wind chord open the coda, with the horn’s long phrase above rocking cello triplets.

  2. II. Im Tempo eines gemächlichen LändlersVienna Philharmonic; Bruno Walter, conductor · 16 January 1938
    15:46

    Mahler’s country dancers have heavy shoes, and his waltzers have sharp elbows. Three dances share the second movement of the Ninth, until the orchestra leaves only a piccolo and a contrabassoon to pick up the pieces.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Fiddles in heavy shoes

      Bassoons and violas offer a quick upbeat; clarinets answer on three square beats. The second violins bring in the Ländler, a country dance. Mahler asks them to play like fiddles, and marks the whole movement somewhat clumsy and very coarse.

    2. The waltz elbows in

      The strings turn to a quicker waltz in E major. Its little upbeat comes from the country dance, but the accent on the third beat changes its gait. Soon the tune acquires enormous downward leaps and a distinctly ungentle manner.

    3. A slower pair of feet

      F major brings a gentler Ländler, with four falling seconds at the start of its melody. The phrases have room to breathe. Bits of the first dance still intrude, briefly borrowing its more deliberate pace.

    4. The waltz spins faster

      The waltz returns in D major, a little faster than before. Mahler inserts little turns into the tune and lets the glockenspiel glint among the strings. The dance keeps accelerating until he has to rein it in.

    5. Three Ninths in Vienna

      At the Vienna festival in June 1912, Walter introduces Mahler’s Ninth alongside two older Ninths: Beethoven’s, conducted by Felix Weingartner, and Bruckner’s, under Arthur Nikisch. Three conductors take on three very different endings.

    6. A dance for fewer players

      Two bassoons quietly recover the opening upbeat, and C major returns. The old Ländler now belongs to solo viola and cello, then solo violin. Its heavy shoes have become much easier to lift.

    7. The dancers lose the tune

      The first Ländler returns, but its phrases keep breaking apart. Winds and strings toss the little upbeats between them. A solo horn stretches the falling seconds into long farewells while the other players remember only scraps of the dance.

    8. Piccolo meets contrabassoon

      At opposite ends of the orchestra, piccolo and contrabassoon share the opening upbeat. The dance has dwindled to this improbable pair. Plucked upper strings supply the last small punctuation.

    How it is joined

    1. The upbeat crosses into E major

      The C-major dance softens and sinks into the bass. A horn phrase overlaps a version of the quick opening upbeat, which leads straight into the E-major waltz. The pace quickens and the strings put fresh emphasis on the third beat.

    2. A bassoon upbeat slows the dance

      The waltz breaks into short figures and slows. A repeated bassoon upbeat ushers in the F-major dance after a long ritardando. Its melody starts with falling seconds, over an accompaniment that keeps the earlier dance’s three-note upbeat.

    3. The waltz gathers from little scraps

      Inner strings and woodwinds slip pieces of the waltz into the slower dance. Its upbeat sounds while the music is still held back; then D major and a quicker Tempo II take over. Little turn figures now decorate the tune.

    4. Two bassoons recover the beginning

      The slower melody fades on a held note. Two bassoons quietly bring back the opening upbeat, followed by the woodwinds’ square reply. C major returns, but the Ländler itself arrives in solo viola and cello, then solo violin.

    5. The waltz pushes past the brass

      Brass and woodwinds darken the opening’s square reply into C minor. Waltz figures creep into the strings and take over the faster pace. Their downward leaps become wider and heavier, with falling chromatic phrases in the horns.

    6. The horns stumble back into Tempo I

      The horns try the opening upbeat at waltz speed, then the first tempo abruptly returns. The C-major Ländler reappears in scattered woodwind and string phrases. Its figures keep interrupting one another as the orchestra shrinks towards the ending.

  3. III. Rondo-Burleske — Allegro assai. Sehr trotzigVienna Philharmonic; Bruno Walter, conductor · 16 January 1938
    11:26

    The Ninth’s Rondo-Burleske makes the whole orchestra a virtuoso, with jagged tunes elbowing past one another at speed. In the middle, a trumpet sings a D-major melody that the finale will remember.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Three notes start a fight

      The trumpet throws out three notes, the last a falling tritone. Strings answer with a jagged arch; horns and woodwinds add their own short figures. These scraps lock together into a tune that keeps arguing with itself.

    2. A tune out strolling

      The rondo also has a lighter F-major tune, sauntering along at the same speed. Its jaunty upbeat comes from the fierce opening. Even this apparently carefree melody carries the family’s sharp features.

    3. Vienna before the Anschluss

      This performance takes place in January 1938. Two months later, Nazi Germany annexes Austria. Walter, already driven from Germany because he is Jewish, must leave his adopted country too; Mahler’s music disappears from its concert programmes.

    4. The trumpet starts to sing

      D major. A solo trumpet sings above held, trembling strings, its melody circling one note before leaping upwards. That little turn will return in the finale, slowed and entrusted to the strings.

    5. The clarinet bares its teeth

      The E♭ clarinet takes the trumpet’s gentle turn and spits it back at twice the speed. Scraps of the rondo start to intrude. Harp sweeps repeatedly clear them away, leaving the violins trembling on a high note.

    6. The rondo forces its way back

      Fragments gather quietly in winds and strings before trombones and low strings bring the opening tune back in force. The strolling F-major melody has gone. The jagged first tune now has the remaining pages to itself.

    7. Faster, then faster again

      Più stretto, then Presto: the closing pages tighten the screw. Winds, brass and strings repeat the opening’s short figures with fierce accents. The movement ends on the same kind of sharp call that set it going.

    How it is joined

    1. A cymbal crash leaves a high A

      A cymbal crash clears away the crowded lines. Trembling strings and flutter-tongued flutes hold a high A, and the harmony settles into D major. The solo trumpet reshapes the earlier turn-and-leap figure into a gentle melody.

    2. The E♭ clarinet sharpens the turn

      The broad melody falls apart into replies. The E♭ clarinet plays its turn twice as fast, followed by scraps of the rondo in flutes and violins. An ascending harp glissando cuts them off and restores the quiet string tremolo.

    3. Trombones finish what the strings begin

      The opening figures slip quietly into the strings, then the winds. Muted horns sound a warning; trombones and low strings burst in with the principal theme. The running lines resume across the orchestra, leaving the central trumpet song behind.

    4. The short figures accelerate

      The repeated figures press into Più stretto and later Presto. Strings, winds and brass crowd the short opening motifs together, with percussion reinforcing their accents. The final A-minor attacks cut off the rush.

  4. IV. Adagio — Sehr langsam und noch zurückhaltendVienna Philharmonic; Bruno Walter, conductor · 16 January 1938
    18:30

    The Ninth ends with a string hymn in D♭ major, a semitone below the key in which the symphony began. Mahler lets its long phrases break into smaller and smaller pieces, until even a single viola note can hold the room.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The violins speak together

      Both violin sections begin in unison, with a forceful rising leap and falling phrase. Then the other strings enter quietly, dividing into many lines. The little turn that flickered through the Rondo-Burleske has become part of a hymn.

    2. A bassoon below the hymn

      The strings leave a D♭ hanging in the violin. From far below, a bassoon climbs slowly towards it. This small, bare passage will keep returning between the hymn’s much fuller statements.

    3. An enormous empty space

      The violins hold a high A. Contrabassoon and cellos bring back the bassoon’s rising line, far beneath them. The instruments seem separated by an enormous empty space; little violin phrases begin to cross it.

    4. The hymn returns in pieces

      D♭ major returns. Horn and second violins take part of one tune while first violins and violas carry the other. Their falling phrases and turns fit together, and the quiet beginning soon catches fire.

    5. Walter’s melting cloud

      In his 1936 book on Mahler, Bruno Walter describes this finale as “like the melting of a cloud in the ethereal blue”. Two years later he conducts the performance playing here, with the Vienna Philharmonic.

    6. The strings pull apart

      The strings press into another long ascent while other lines fall against them. Low woodwinds and brass join the climb. The small turn is everywhere, passed from voice to voice as the orchestra gathers for its great late climax.

    7. The orchestra leaves the strings

      High violins hold a C♮ above the dwindling orchestra. The cor anglais answers with a falling phrase. Soon only strings remain, passing the tune downwards until the cellos can carry it no further.

    8. A page of farewells

      Adagissimo. Muted second violins begin a small rising phrase, and the remaining strings answer in scraps, with long rests between them. Whole tunes have shrunk to a turn, a falling step, a note held until the next breath.

    9. The viola’s last turn

      D♭ major holds at the end. The viola turns its little phrase upwards, where so many earlier turns fell. The chord remains while the notes within it disappear, one by one.

    How it is joined

    1. High A, then the low instruments

      The violins leap to a high A and soften to pianissimo. Contrabassoon and cellos begin the rising line previously heard in the bassoon. The upper strings keep fragments of the hymn above this slow ascent.

    2. The horn joins the returning strings

      Woodwinds repeat falling seconds as the interlude presses forward. D♭ major and the original tempo return. Horn and second violins sing part of the second theme against the first theme in violins and violas.

    3. The violins hold an A♭

      The climbing violin line reaches a high A♭ and suddenly softens. Oboe and clarinet take fragments of the two tunes while a solo violin keeps the turn. The broad string writing breaks into these small replies.

    4. The strings gather the scattered phrases

      A crescendo on the first theme brings back the second in strongly accented strings. Low woodwinds add chords as the pace quickens. Some lines climb while others descend; horns join them with a broad rising phrase.

    5. High C remains above the orchestra

      The violins rise to C♮ and hold it as the orchestra withdraws. Woodwinds softly take the hymn’s descending notes; the cor anglais continues with a falling octave. Second violins bring back the rising scale, then the remaining string phrases pass down into the cellos.

    6. Muted violins after the pause

      The cello phrase sinks into silence. At Adagissimo, muted second violins resume the rising scale. Only strings remain, their phrases broken by rests, with the viola’s turn unfolding inside the last D♭-major chords.
