Jean Sibelius / IV. Fin de siècle

Symphony No. 2, Op. 43

1901–02 · Op. 43 · Orchestra

Sketches made in Rapallo link the slow movement to Don Juan and Death. The scherzo’s rising bridge opens directly into the D major finale.

44 min

The recording

  1. Complete: I. Allegretto · II. Tempo andante, ma rubato · III. Vivacissimo · IV. Finale: Allegro moderatoLondon Symphony Orchestra; Pierre Monteux, conductor · 18–20 June 1958, Kingsway Hall, London
    43:57

    Three rising notes in the strings grow into the finale’s great D major tune. Pierre Monteux and the London Symphony Orchestra play the complete symphony, including the long crescendo that carries the scherzo straight into the finale.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Three notes in the strings

      The strings begin with three rising notes in D major. Woodwinds and horns answer in short phrases, with room between them. These modest beginnings contain the outline of the great tune that will fill the finale.

    2. Don Juan at Rapallo

      In February 1901, Sibelius is in Rapallo, Italy, sketching a theme for the slow movement. Beside it he writes a scene for Don Juan: a stranger enters his castle and refuses to answer. When the stranger sings, Don Juan recognises Death.

    3. Two themes share the room

      The opening dance and the longer woodwind theme return together. Sibelius fits them against one another in counterpoint: one phrase can keep moving while the other sings across it. The scattered beginnings have found company.

    4. Plucked steps, a bassoon song

      The slow movement opens in D minor. Cellos and basses pluck a long line before the bassoons begin their sombre song. Each low note sounds like another step towards the door.

    5. Strings after the brass

      After the brass climax and a pause, divided strings unfold a long, quiet melody. Sibelius labels its early sketch “Christus”. Its calm is repeatedly interrupted as the movement continues.

    6. Helsinki, then Stockholm

      Sibelius conducts the première in Helsinki on 8 March 1902. He revises the symphony afterwards; his brother-in-law Armas Järnefelt conducts the new version in Stockholm on 10 November 1903.

    7. Strings at full tilt

      The scherzo sends the strings racing through rapid repeated figures. Slower wind phrases ride above them; the flute can sound remarkably unhurried while the bows are working furiously below.

    8. Nine notes from the oboe

      The trio slows the dance into G♭ major. An oboe repeats the same note nine times, supported by clarinets and horns, before its melody opens out. So little changes, and suddenly everything feels tender.

    9. The trumpets answer in D

      The scherzo’s long crescendo carries straight into the finale. Strings sing the three-note rise in D major, broad and unashamed; trumpets answer where woodwinds answered in the first movement. The little opening dance has grown to fill the orchestra.

    10. One more step upward

      The closing D major music draws out the rising figure once more. Late in this coda, its familiar three notes finally reach a fourth. Sibelius has saved that extra step for the last great stretch of the symphony.

    How it is joined

    1. A pause leaves room for woodwinds

      The opening dance stops for a pause. Woodwinds take up a longer, more insistent phrase, changing the scale of the melody after the strings’ short, separated answers.

    2. Plucked strings begin the next argument

      Pizzicato strings begin the development. A rocking phrase and short woodwind figures grow around them, while the opening string rhythm keeps returning beneath the new phrases.

    3. The two themes return together

      The crescendo relaxes, and the opening dance returns alongside the longer woodwind phrase. Their different rhythms fit together in counterpoint; Sibelius lets both themes speak at once.

    4. D major closes; plucked D minor begins

      The first movement closes quietly in D major. After the break, cellos and basses begin the slow movement in D minor, plucking a long line before the bassoons join. The marking is Tempo andante, ma rubato: a walking pace, freely shaped.

    5. Divided strings answer the brass

      The brass climax stops, leaving a pause. Divided strings begin the contrasting melody quietly, with long phrases in place of the preceding broken calls. Sibelius’s sketch for this theme bears the heading “Christus”.

    6. The bass breaks the long melody

      The long string melody reaches its climax, then the contrary phrase in the bass interrupts its progress. The music breaks into quiet fragments, recalling pieces of the earlier tune.

    7. Two plucked notes, then racing bows

      Two pizzicato strokes end the slow movement. After the break, Vivacissimo sends the strings into fast repeated figures, with longer woodwind phrases above them. The bows have scarcely time to rest.

    8. The oboe holds the dance still

      The fast string figures stop. The trio settles into G♭ major, and an oboe repeats one note nine times over clarinets and horns. The melody opens gradually out of that repeated note.

    9. The horns add a rising figure

      The scherzo resumes its fast string figures. Sibelius changes the slower phrases above them and adds a rising horn figure. The same scurrying accompaniment now carries a new call across it.

    10. The slow oboe tune comes back

      The strings’ rapid figures subside and the slow trio comes back. The oboe melody returns to the foreground. This time the trio continues into the rising bridge to the finale.

    11. G♭, A♭, B♭ begin the climb

      The trio becomes a crescendo built around G♭–A♭–B♭. The three rising notes recall the shape heard at the beginning of the symphony. Repetition lengthens the climb until the finale’s D major tune can break through.

    12. D major without a break

      The crescendo carries straight into D major. Strings sing the three-note rise as a broad melody; trumpets answer. The finale’s Allegro moderato opens out from the bridge, with no silence between the movements.

    13. Woodwinds crowd around the returning tune

      The quiet passage begins a long crescendo towards the return of the broad string tune. Short woodwind figures gather around the melody, keeping their sharper rhythm against its long phrases.
