François Couperin / I. The Baroque

Les Nations

1726 · Two treble instruments and continuo

Four sonatas followed by French suites: La Françoise, L’Espagnole, L’Impériale and La Piémontoise. Some of the sonatas had first circulated under an invented Italian name.

10 min

The recording

  1. La Piémontaise · SonadeNew Comma Baroque: Matthew C. Cataldi, Baroque violin; Leighann Daihl, Baroque flute; Phillip W. Serna, viola da gamba; Emily J. Morlan, historical keyboards · 10 September 2012
    10:03

    A violin and flute trade long notes, quick runs and graceful little airs in the sonata that opens La Piémontaise. New Comma Baroque plays it with viola da gamba and keyboard sharing the bass: four people make Couperin’s three-part conversation.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Four players, three lines

      Violin and flute take the two upper parts; gamba and keyboard share the bass. In the opening Gravement, one upper voice often holds a note while the other moves. Trills decorate the long notes, and the bass has ornaments of its own.

    2. The bass joins the conversation

      The Vivement gives the bass running semiquavers as well as the upper parts. A phrase can begin below the violin and flute, then rise into their conversation. The keyboard and gamba have plenty to say together.

    3. An Italian name for Couperin

      In his 1726 preface, Couperin tells a story about his first sonata. He presents it as the work of an Italian composer, whose name is an anagram of his own. The audience welcomes it. Encouraged, he writes more sonatas under the same disguise.

    4. The violin sings alone

      The first air is in G major and marked Gracieusement. The violin carries its opening phrase while the flute rests. The bass answers with small steps and ornaments; then the flute takes up the tune and the violin falls silent.

    5. Four nations on the title pages

      The four ordres of Les Nations bear grand names: France, Spain, the Empire and Piedmont. Each opens with an Italian-style sonata, then turns to French dances. Here, after the sonata, the printed score continues with an allemande marked “nobly, and without slowness”.

    6. Corelli beside Lully

      Couperin names both Corelli and Lully in his preface to Les Nations, “both of whose compositions I shall love as long as I live”. Italian sonatas and French dances share his book. He has no wish to give up either composer.

    7. Dotted notes above a held bass

      The later Gravement et marqué begins with a long C in the bass. The violin sets out a dotted figure; the flute enters with it a bar later. Their short and long notes pull against the bass’s unbroken sound.

    8. Six quavers, three moving parts

      The final Légèrement changes to six-eight. The flute begins the running quavers while the violin rests; soon the figures pass between the upper parts and the bass. The long sonata ends with all three lines still taking their turns.

    How it is joined

    1. The bass breaks into running notes

      A D major chord closes the slow opening. Vivement begins in G minor and common time: the violin drops out after its first note while flute and bass keep moving. Running semiquavers soon pass between the gamba, keyboard and upper instruments. Couperin replaces the opening’s long overlaps with brisk exchanges.

    2. E♭ under the held notes

      The new Gravement opens on E♭ major: E♭ in the bass, G and B♭ in the upper parts. The metre returns to three-two, and violin and flute hold long notes. Later Couperin asks for notes égales, evenly played notes, as the gamba and keyboard bass begin moving beneath them.

    3. A graceful tune over G

      The quick movement slows to très lentement and closes on D major. The air answers in G major, with G in the bass and a three-beat metre. Marked Gracieusement, the violin begins a rounded, ornamented phrase over gamba and keyboard; the flute rests before taking its turn.

    4. From dotted notes to six-eight

      The slow section reaches D major before Légèrement begins over G in the bass. Six-eight replaces common time. The flute starts a stream of quavers; the violin gives a single note, then rests. Gamba and keyboard keep dotted crotchets beneath the first phrase before joining the quicker movement.
