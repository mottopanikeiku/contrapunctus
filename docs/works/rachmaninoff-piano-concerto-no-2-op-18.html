Sergei Rachmaninoff / IV. Fin de siècle

Piano Concerto No. 2, Op. 18

1900–01 · Op. 18 · Piano and orchestra

Dedicated to Dr Nikolai Dahl, who helped Rachmaninoff through his depression. The pianist rings the opening bells, then accompanies the orchestra’s first melody.

3 recordings · 35 min

In the Hall

  1. I. ModeratoSviatoslav Richter, Warsaw National Philharmonic Orchestra, Stanisław Wisłocki · 1959
    11:09

    Sviatoslav Richter begins with the piano’s tolling chords, then pours out arpeggios beneath the orchestra’s first melody. Rachmaninoff’s Second Concerto makes its soloist an accompanist before letting him sing.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The piano becomes a bell

      The orchestra waits. The piano strikes a chord, reaches down to a low F and begins again. Each toll grows stronger, from pp towards f; only after this slow gathering does the first tune arrive.

    2. A tune over restless hands

      Violins, violas and clarinet sing the C minor melody. Beneath them, the piano sweeps up and down in broken chords, while cellos and double basses pluck the bass notes. The soloist has the busiest part and somebody else has the tune.

    3. Dahl’s daily promise

      “You will begin to write your concerto.” In the first months of 1900, Dr Nikolai Dahl repeats that promise to Rachmaninoff during their daily treatment sessions. After the failure of his First Symphony, the composer is struggling to write. The new concerto will carry Dahl’s name on its dedication page.

    4. The soloist gets the song

      The piano takes the long second melody in E♭ major. Its upper notes sing over its own broken chords; the orchestra leaves space around them. The instrument that carried the first tune’s accompaniment now carries both parts.

    5. The tunes break into phrases

      The long melodies break into shorter phrases. Piano and woodwinds pass them back and forth, changing keys as they go. A march-like rhythm begins to take shape in the piano; it will stand against the returning first tune.

    6. Moscow, November 1901

      On 9 November 1901 Rachmaninoff sits at the piano in Moscow for the first complete performance. His cousin and former teacher Alexander Siloti conducts. The Adagio and finale already have a successful première behind them; the first movement is the new arrival.

    7. A march beneath the melody

      The orchestra brings back the first tune in C minor. The piano answers with hard, marching chords in place of its original arpeggios. Two ideas fill the same space: the broad melody above and the insistent rhythm below.

    8. A horn left singing

      A solo horn takes the second melody in A♭ major, marked p dolce. The piano falls silent. Quiet repeated string chords leave the horn’s long notes exposed after the crowded march.

    9. A quiet start to the coda

      Meno mosso: the first tune returns softly in the strings, over small piano figures. Rachmaninoff holds the ending back before the final acceleration. The movement closes in C minor; the concerto still has two movements to reach C major.

    How it is joined

    1. The bells turn into arpeggios

      The repeated low F makes way for C, and the piano opens its solid chords into arpeggios. Violins, violas and clarinet begin the C minor theme, marked con passione in the strings. Cellos and double basses pluck the bass beneath the soloist’s moving hands.

    2. The piano takes the melody

      The quick più mosso piano passage drives through cadences towards E♭ major. The sound then softens and the piano begins the second melody, sustained above its own broken chords. Short woodwind answers surround the solo line.

    3. The long tunes become short exchanges

      The E♭ major closing rush subsides. Woodwind phrases and piano figures take up fragments of the two themes, repeating them over changing bass notes. The melody moves from one instrument to another as the harmony leaves the exposition’s settled keys.

    4. The orchestra sings over the march

      The accelerating piano chords and brass calls lead straight into the C minor return. The orchestra restores the first melody while the piano keeps the march-like rhythm developed in the middle. The home tune arrives in the thick of the crescendo.

    5. A quiet chord leaves the horn

      The piano descends chromatically and settles on an A♭ major chord at ppp. It then rests while a solo horn sings the second subject, p dolce. The strings repeat soft chords underneath; the tune that belonged to the piano now fits inside a single horn phrase.

    6. The first tune returns softly

      The late piano arpeggios touch C major before the coda turns back towards C minor. At Meno mosso, the strings recall the first tune at pp, with short piano figures above a slower-moving bass. The final drive grows out of this quiet return.

  2. II. Adagio sostenutoSviatoslav Richter, Warsaw National Philharmonic Orchestra, Stanisław Wisłocki · 1959
    11:54

    A flute begins the Adagio’s long melody and a clarinet carries it on, while Sviatoslav Richter turns quiet piano arpeggios beneath them. The song has a stormy middle to survive before it comes home to E major.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. Four bars to E major

      Muted strings begin in the first movement’s C minor. Clarinets, bassoons and horns join their slow chords as the notes shift by semitones. Four bars carry the music into E major, ready for the piano’s quiet arpeggios.

    2. The clarinet carries it on

      The flute starts the song above the piano’s circling notes. A clarinet continues the phrase, marked dolce e sempre espressivo — sweetly, always expressive. The melody takes its time; the piano keeps moving underneath.

    3. Heard before the opening movement

      On 15 December 1900, in Moscow, Rachmaninoff plays the Adagio and finale with Alexander Siloti conducting. The concert benefits the Ladies’ Charity Prison Committee. The audience greets the music enthusiastically; the first movement still waits to be finished.

    4. The phrases grow shorter

      The piano’s quick figures come forward. Bassoons and horns answer in short phrases, and the bass keeps moving under the repeated shapes. The long opening song breaks into smaller exchanges between soloist and orchestra.

    5. A cadenza for two hands

      The orchestra reaches a held chord and leaves the piano to continue. Runs climb through the keyboard, thick chords interrupt them, and trills hold the sound in the air. The cadenza spends its energy before the slow song returns.

    6. The violins recover the song

      Adagio sostenuto, Tempo I: the piano returns to quiet E major arpeggios. The first violins take up the opening song, marked espressivo. After the cadenza’s crowded hands, the melody has room to breathe again.

    7. A doctor who plays music

      Nikolai Dahl, the doctor to whom Rachmaninoff dedicates the concerto, is an amateur musician and hosts chamber-music evenings. In 1900 the composer visits him daily for treatment. On the concerto’s first page, above the opening bars, stands “À Monsieur N. Dahl”.

    8. Light flutes above broad chords

      The piano sings in chords over its own broken-chord accompaniment. Above it, flutes play short staccato notes and the strings hold long ones. The closing song has a gentle patter overhead.

    9. The piano has the last movement

      The wind phrases fall silent, leaving the piano’s last moving notes above sustained strings. A final upward figure reaches the E major chord. Piano and strings hold it together under a fermata.

    How it is joined

    1. Held chords become circling notes

      The muted strings’ C minor chords move chromatically into E major. The piano enters at p, circling through broken chords over a low E. A solo flute begins the melody and a clarinet continues it, with the piano’s steady motion beneath.

    2. Short wind answers surround the piano

      The piano’s quick notes cease to be a quiet background. Its hands exchange short phrases while bassoons, horns and upper winds answer. The lower strings move beneath these repeated figures, carrying the harmony through chromatic changes away from the opening’s settled E major.

    3. A held chord releases the piano

      A timpani roll and sustained wind, trombone and string notes swell beneath the piano’s rising run. At rehearsal 25, an orchestral chord is followed by a long fermata over rests. The piano continues alone, climbing from the bass through runs and trills.

    4. The cadenza finds the old accompaniment

      The piano’s long run settles into a trill and a ritardando. Adagio sostenuto, Tempo I restores the E major arpeggios at pp. The first violins bring back the melody, p espressivo, above the low E and the soloist’s circling notes.

    5. The winds leave the last chord

      The flutes and clarinets finish their short figures and fall silent. The strings hold their notes while the piano completes its broken chords and final rising line. E major remains beneath the closing phrase; piano and strings end together under a fermata.

  3. III. Allegro scherzandoSviatoslav Richter; Warsaw National Philharmonic Orchestra; Stanisław Wisłocki · 1959
    11:40

    The finale’s broad melody begins quietly with oboe and violas and ends blazing in C major across the orchestra. Sviatoslav Richter and Stanisław Wisłocki bring the concerto home through quick exchanges, a solo flourish and one last great song.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. The strings set off quietly

      The strings begin with little phrases that pass between violins and cellos. Clarinets, bassoons and a quiet tuba support them; the piano waits. The introduction moves from the Adagio’s E major back towards C minor.

    2. Leaps, then short replies

      The piano bounds across the keyboard, then settles into short, accented figures. Flutes and clarinets answer, and the strings pluck their accompaniment. This first tune is quick on its feet.

    3. Richter in Warsaw

      On 26 and 28 April 1959 Sviatoslav Richter records the concerto in Warsaw’s Philharmonic Hall, with Stanisław Wisłocki conducting the Warsaw orchestra. Deutsche Grammophon issues it on an LP, DG 138 076, alongside Rachmaninoff preludes.

    4. Oboe and violas, singing together

      Moderato: oboe and violas introduce the long B♭ major melody. The piano rests while horns hold the harmony and cellos and basses pluck underneath. After all the soloist’s quick work, the orchestra supplies the tune everyone remembers.

    5. The piano takes the tune

      At rehearsal 31 the soloist returns with the long melody, marked mf dolce. The right hand sings it in chords above flowing broken chords in the left, while the orchestra thins to a few held notes. The piano is tune and accompaniment at once.

    6. Tolstoy withholds his blessing

      In early 1900 Rachmaninoff and the bass Feodor Chaliapin perform one of his songs for Leo Tolstoy, hoping for encouragement. Tolstoy asks: “Tell me, do you really think anybody needs music like that?”

    7. The short tune takes charge

      The first theme’s clipped figures return to the foreground. The piano strikes chords high on the keyboard while winds, brass and strings answer in sharp bursts. These little phrases will occupy most of the development.

    8. Dr Dahl and the concerto

      From January to April 1900 Rachmaninoff sees the psychiatrist Nikolai Dahl every day. Dahl’s hypnosis sessions repeat one promise: “You will begin to write your concerto.” By summer the ideas are flowing again, and the finished work is dedicated to Dahl.

    9. The finale is heard first

      On 15 December 1900, at a Moscow benefit concert, Rachmaninoff plays the second and third movements with Siloti conducting. The audience gives them a triumph; the first movement is still unwritten. The complete concerto follows on 9 November 1901, with the same soloist and conductor.

    10. The song in D♭

      The long melody returns in D♭ major, carried by flute and first violins. The piano rests again before taking it up. The tune is a minor third higher than its first appearance, with brighter voices singing it.

    11. The piano repeats the handover

      At a tempo (rehearsal 37) the piano takes the tune from the flute and violins, again marked mf dolce. Right-hand chords carry the melody above rolling left-hand arpeggios, and the orchestra thins to a few held notes once more.

    12. Full Moon and Empty Arms

      The finale’s long melody acquires another life as Full Moon and Empty Arms. Frank Sinatra records the song.

    13. Quiet figures start the climb

      Short phrases pass through the woodwinds and strings. The piano joins softly, with poco a poco accelerando above its repeated figures. The bass begins to move, and the route to the final flourish gathers speed.

    14. Presto, then a flourish alone

      Presto: the orchestra hammers fff chords, then stops under long fermatas marked lunga. The piano sweeps the keyboard in a quasi glissando and runs on to a fermata of its own. Maestoso follows.

    15. C major, at last

      Maestoso: the long melody arrives in C major across winds and strings, above the piano’s full chords. Horns, trombones and tuba support it. The little oboe song now fills the orchestra.

    16. One last burst of speed

      Più vivo sets the piano moving again after the broad melody. Quick figures and repeated orchestral chords drive towards the final C major cadence. The concerto ends with four emphatic attacks.

    How it is joined

    1. The piano bounds into C minor

      The short string phrases grow into an orchestral crescendo. The piano sweeps in and then leaps between low bass notes and high figures in C minor. As its accented tune takes shape, flutes and clarinets answer over plucked strings.

    2. Oboe and violas inherit the tune

      A ritardando slows the piano’s broken chords. At Moderato, the soloist rests and oboe and violas begin the B♭ major melody. Horns hold quiet chords, while cellos and basses pluck the bass beneath the long phrase.

    3. The piano recovers its clipped figures

      The lyrical group’s B♭ major close breaks into forceful exchanges. The piano climbs through a quick run and strikes high chords at ff; winds, brass and strings answer with short, accented phrases. The first theme’s small figures become the development’s main business.

    4. The returning song rises to D♭

      The piano slows through Meno mosso and a ritardando, then falls silent at Moderato. Flute and first violins bring back the long theme in D♭ major. Clarinets and bassoons hold the harmony while the lower strings pluck their bass notes.

    5. Small phrases start to accelerate

      The long melody breaks into short wind and string phrases. The piano enters quietly over a held low C, with poco a poco accelerando above its repeated figures. Horns join the rhythm, the bass begins to move, and the passage gathers towards Presto.

    6. A solo flourish opens the final song

      Presto brings a short orchestral blaze, then a long fermata over rests leaves the piano alone. Its flourish crosses the keyboard and slows into Maestoso. The second melody returns in C major, carried by winds and strings above full piano chords and a low C in the bass.

Other performances

Historical and comparison recordings, outside the concert’s running order.

  1. I. Moderato — opening 78-rpm side onlySergei Rachmaninoff, piano; Philadelphia Orchestra; Leopold Stokowski, conductor · 10 April 1929
    3:10

    Rachmaninoff plays his concerto with Leopold Stokowski and the Philadelphia Orchestra in 1929, turning the opening bell chords into a running accompaniment for the strings. This transfer preserves the first 78-rpm side of the movement.

    Notes as it plays
    1. The composer rings the bells

      The piano begins alone. Each chord is followed by a low F, and the repeated strokes grow from pp towards f. Rachmaninoff writes a little ritardando before the hands open into the arpeggios beneath the first tune.

    2. Stokowski’s orchestra carries the tune

      The strings and clarinet have the C minor melody; Rachmaninoff has the running arpeggios beneath it. Cellos and basses pluck their notes. The pianist who began alone now accompanies the orchestra’s long phrase.

    3. Philadelphia, 10 April 1929

      Rachmaninoff and Stokowski make this recording at Philadelphia’s Academy of Music on 10 April 1929. They record the opening three times. Victor chooses the third take, which also reaches Britain on HMV D.B. 1333.

    4. Siloti beside the piano

      At the complete concerto’s première in Moscow on 9 November 1901, Rachmaninoff plays and Alexander Siloti conducts. Siloti is his cousin and former piano teacher. By then the last two movements have already delighted a Moscow audience at a charity concert the previous December.

  2. II. Adagio sostenuto — opening 78-rpm side onlySergei Rachmaninoff, piano; Philadelphia Orchestra; Leopold Stokowski, conductor · 10 April 1929
    3:05

    Rachmaninoff accompanies the flute and clarinet in the opening song of his Adagio, with Leopold Stokowski conducting the Philadelphia Orchestra in 1929. This transfer preserves the movement’s first 78-rpm side.

    Notes as it plays
    1. Four bars between two keys

      Muted strings begin in C minor, the key of the first movement. Their slow chords move by semitones towards E major; clarinets, bassoons and horns join them. The piano waits until the new key is ready.

    2. Flute, then clarinet

      The piano’s arpeggios circle quietly beneath a wind melody. A flute begins the phrase; a clarinet continues it, marked dolce e sempre espressivo — sweetly, always expressive. Rachmaninoff gives the soloist the moving notes and the wind players time to breathe.

    3. The first take survives

      At the Academy of Music in Philadelphia on 10 April 1929, Rachmaninoff and Stokowski record two takes of this Adagio opening. The first becomes the master; the second is destroyed. The issued side carries the music on HMV D.B. 1334.

    4. A charity concert in Moscow

      On 15 December 1900 Rachmaninoff plays this Adagio and the finale in Moscow, with Alexander Siloti conducting. The concert raises money for prisoners through the Ladies’ Charity Prison Committee. The first movement is still unfinished.
