Jean-Philippe Rameau / I. The Baroque

La Poule

c. 1728, publication · Solo piano; originally harpsichord

3 min

The recording

  1. La PouleMarcelle Meyer, piano · 29–30 October 1953, Salle Adyar, Paris
    3:06

    A hen’s clucking grows into repeated chords and running passages in Rameau’s La Poule, played here by Marcelle Meyer on piano. The bird starts on a single G.

    Notes as it plays · how it is joined
    1. A hen on one G

      The right hand repeats G, then flicks upwards. Rameau prints “co co co co co” beneath the opening notes. The left hand joins the clucking, and the little flourish begins to climb higher.

    2. Meyer at Salle Adyar

      Paris, October 1953: Marcelle Meyer records Rameau’s three harpsichord books at Salle Adyar, playing them on the piano. La Poule belongs to the third book, alongside dances, Les Sauvages and L’Enharmonique.

    3. One note becomes a chord

      The repeated-note figure keeps acquiring companions. Sometimes one line clucks alone; elsewhere both hands repeat chords beneath a longer upper note. Rameau marks exchanges of fort and doux — loud and soft — across the piece.

    4. The hen starts on D

      The second half begins with the clucking figure on D, after a cadence in D minor. The same quick upward flick sets off another stretch of repeated notes and runs, now working back towards G minor.

    5. The bass makes its descent

      Near the end, repeated notes trade places between the hands while quick runs sweep across them. The last G minor chord stays above a descending bass run, which carries the piano down to a low G.

    How it is joined

    1. D minor starts another round

      The first half closes in D minor, with the bass running down beneath the held chord. After the double bar, the right hand alone repeats D and adds the familiar upward flick. The left hand re-enters as the figure climbs.

    2. Clucking and runs converge on G

      The piano exchanges repeated notes between high and low parts, with quick runs crossing them. The final phrase settles into G minor. Its upper chord holds while the bass runs down to G.
