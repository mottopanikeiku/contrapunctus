A Song for St Cecilia’s Day — opening

1687
EnglishFrom harmony, from heavenly harmony, This universal frame began. When Nature underneath a heap Of jarring atoms lay, And could not heave her head, The tuneful voice was heard from high, “Arise, ye more than dead!” Then cold, and hot, and moist, and dry, In order to their stations leap, And Music’s power obey.

1785; revised c. 1803
GermanFreude, schöner Götterfunken, Tochter aus Elysium, Wir betreten feuertrunken, Himmlische, dein Heiligtum. Deine Zauber binden wieder, Was die Mode streng geteilt, Alle Menschen werden Brüder, Wo dein sanfter Flügel weilt.

Erlkönig — opening stanzas

1782
GermanWer reitet so spät durch Nacht und Wind? Es ist der Vater mit seinem Kind; Er hat den Knaben wohl in dem Arm, Er faßt ihn sicher, er hält ihn warm. “Mein Sohn, was birgst du so bang dein Gesicht?” “Siehst, Vater, du den Erlkönig nicht? English · Contrapunctus translationWho rides so late through night and wind? Schubert’s 1815 setting, D 328, distributes the speakers by register and harmonic character while the piano’s repeated triplets maintain the ride. FrenchDe la musique avant toute chose, Et pour cela préfère l’Impair Plus vague et plus soluble dans l’air, Sans rien en lui qui pèse ou qui pose. Car nous voulons la Nuance encor, Pas la Couleur, rien que la nuance ! Oh ! la nuance seule fiance Le rêve au rêve et la flûte au cor !

1920
FrenchDes nuées tourbillonnantes laissent entrevoir, par éclaircies, des couples de valseurs. Elles se dissipent peu à peu : on distingue une immense salle peuplée d’une foule tournoyante. La scène s’éclaire progressivement. La lumière des lustres éclate au ff. Une Cour impériale, vers 1855.
English · Contrapunctus translationWhirling clouds give glimpses, through gaps, of waltzing couples. Little by little they scatter: one makes out an immense hall filled with a turning crowd. Ravel printed this scenario at the head of the score of his “poème chorégraphique”. It is also a set of orchestration instructions in disguise: the clouds are the muted, divided double basses that open the piece; the gaps are the first waltz fragments surfacing in the bassoons; “éclate au ff” marks the exact dynamic at which the full orchestra arrives. Ravel insisted the setting was 1855, not 1914 — but the score’s violent ending has been heard as an epitaph for that court ever since.
